Distributed, yet Unified

Daniel Burger·23 November 2026·4,159 words

TL;DR

The fourth and last operational requirement of my Ontology is called "distributed unification", which might sound like an oxymoron at first. Every brain is already distributed, yet unified, and the requirement asks how far this can be stretched, and what does the stretching.

Originally posted on XReplies and discussion happen there.Open on X →

The fourth and last operational requirement of my Ontology is called "distributed unification", which might sound like an oxymoron at first. It basically goes into two aspects of thought that come up whenever we talk about brains and consciousness, and the Ontology's two lines for it are "small node count unified" (the weak form) and "large-scale, graceful degradation" (the strong one).

First we start with the unified aspect of phenomenality, and it's pretty common to do this by putting on Husserl's bracket (the epoché, so no claims about brains or the world yet, only what's given to you as a conscious observer). The script is pretty simple: Look at what you experience right now and try to find one experience that comes on its own. You won't, I think, because whatever is there comes together with the rest, as parts of one stream, which Husserl calls synthesis, "the primal form belonging to consciousness" (Cartesian Meditations: §17 and §18). The critical part for later is that he describes this unity as something that consciousness DOES, so an achievement that has to keep going. And the most basic form of it is the synthesis of inner time (or more plainly, the now has a certain width) => the just-past is still held in it and the about-to-come is already expected (his living present).

David Pearce (@webmasterdave) states the same thing as a problem for physics (and actually quite rigorously: [physicalism.com]). It's the binding problem => why isn't a pack of 86 billion membrane-bound neurons a "micro-experiential zombie", mind-dust with nobody home? His own answer is quantum coherence. I'm not following him entirely there (maximally philosophically conservative, as usual), but I take the problem as stated. Also importantly, depending on how you read them both, Pearce and Husserl aren't talking about quite the same unity. Pearce asks how everything hangs together at one moment (the self that persists over time he leaves aside), and Husserl's might be better labelled a unity over time. For today I can't keep the two apart tho => pull the parts of a brain away from each other and the signals between them need longer (yesterday's boring formula), so "how far apart" sooner or later turns into "how wide is the now".

Now, I want to contest this a bit, and not only from the philosophical or bracketing point of view but from a neuroscientific one => do we indeed have a unified consciousness? The bracket only gives me how it seems, which can't tell me how the thing is made, or whether it's as complete as it looks from inside (with two streams in one skull, how would either know about the other?). And the bracket has to come off the moment somebody takes a scalpel to a brain. What neuroscience finds, so far as I can tell/found/understand, is no place and no instant where it all comes together. Colour is perceived about 80 ms before motion, from which Semir Zeki concluded that visual consciousness consists of many "microconsciousnesses" spread over space and time ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov]). An average myelinated fibre of the corpus callosum needs more than 25 ms from one temporal lobe to the other ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov]). So the unity that Husserl describes is manufactured, out of parts that are centimetres and tens of milliseconds apart, and within tolerances. Which means that every brain is ALREADY distributed, yet unified! The oxymoron, as stated before, describes you right now, in this moment, and the requirement in my Ontology asks how far this can be stretched and also asks what does the stretching.

Consider the following cases:
– Split. Cutting the corpus callosum gave Sperry and Gazzaniga patients whose hemispheres each perceived and answered on their own, hence the textbook's "two streams [of consciousness] in one skull". Two such patients were retested => they couldn't compare stimuli across the midline, but they reported stimuli anywhere in the visual field, with either hand or in words ([doi.org]). So perception was divided and the agent apparently wasn't (contested, and it's two patients).
– Almost split. In last year's cohort, one centimetre of spared fibres at the back of the callosum kept the hemispheres fully integrated, and only the complete cuts split ([doi.org]).
– Half gone. Adults who had a hemisphere removed as children are one person each, with the usual functional networks ([doi.org]).
– The reverse. The twins from day eight of my Meditations have a bridge of thalamic tissue between their brains, and some contents seem to cross it, but they're still two subjects.

As a general rule-of-thumb you might expect that the more wire there is, the more unity we wind up with. These four cases say otherwise.

For the question of which PARTS can go missing I rely on Hugues Duffau's review of glioma surgery ([doi.org]). A low-grade glioma grows over years => more than 90% of these patients have a normal neurological exam, whereas after a stroke in the same place only about a quarter recover fully. To a first approximation, how fast something gets lost matters more than what gets lost. Surgeons have removed Broca's area and other regions that textbooks call "eloquent", and the patients went back to their normal working lives. And at a second operation years later, sites that disturbed a function when they were stimulated the first time do nothing any more, so the function has moved while the person has obv. stayed inside that brain. This can't go on forever tho, and the most obvious limit is the long-range wiring (the white matter), which doesn't get compensated. Duffau speaks of "nodes within a wide network", and in the one distributed mind that we know, nodes can apparently get lost if it happens slowly, but the connections between them can't. It's also only half of what we need => it shows that another piece of brain can take over (and nothing yet about another substrate).

Now pull the parts apart, in theory over large distances. Is that still one thing with one now, or does it break down? Whatever it is that unifies a brain, is it still all there afterwards? The nice thing about asking it like this is that it's robust to my ignorance. A brain is one already (to whatever degree, see above). If every physical influence that went between two of its parts before still goes between them afterwards, with the same timing, then the unifier is untouched, whatever it is. So I can preserve unity without owning a theory of it, which I couldn't say about creating it between two brains (day eight's collective mind is a much stronger claim than today's, although the hardware would be the same).

Afaik the one theory with an explicit criterion for when two things are one is integrated information theory (IIT: [arxiv.org]). If you'd weaken the corpus callosum step by step, Tononi and Koch expect the one mind to fall apart into two quite abruptly at some point, and in the limit of a strong enough link between two brains they expect the opposite (that's Koch's Übermind from day eight). However, in all cases a part that's only simulated doesn't count for them, because a simulation has no causal power of its own, whereas an artificial "connectivity booster" between real brains is fine. That's about where I land as well, just without Φ.

The links are what I called "virtual long-range axons" in our essay last year ([danburonline.substack.com]). In particular, I see that some researchers are already working on virtual axons, for example, regardless of what they call it: living axon tracts grown in a hydrogel column and then implanted ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov]), a neuromorphic chip that reconnected two cut-apart cultures in real time ([doi.org]), or indeed the biohybrid bridge from a recent conversation thread on X (below).

But here a simplified and too heavily coarse-grained view of the brain might be detrimental. There will be places where a virtual axon is adequate, bc all that crosses there is spikes and their timing, and places where it isn't. Like, e.g. what about ephaptic coupling? That'd also need to be recreated wherever it does some of the work, and it does some => in slices of hippocampus, slow waves keep running across a complete cut, with nothing but the electric field carrying them over (e.g.: [doi.org]). Some people even say that the field itself is the experience (Susan Pockett's theory, @cube_flipper has a good introduction here: [smoothbrains.net]), and then a virtual axon carries over nothing of what matters. So you have a choice about where to cut, and it seems helpful to notice this choice. I'd cut in the white matter, bc that's where the brain comes closest to just being a "wire" (annoyingly it's also the part that doesn't get compensated in Duffau's reorganisation patients => so mistakes there are quite expensive). Even if a tract looks like just wire tho, it might well not be, so I'd treat that as a hypothesis and test it for every single tract.

Now, let me tell you about a modified version of the China brain ([en.wikipedia.org]). In Ned Block's original thought experiment, a billion people with two-way radios each "play" one neuron, and a functionalist then has to say that the nation has a mind (radically summarising here, go read the article!). I think that the original mixes two jobs: people talking over the radio and people doing calculations. So let's run it twice:
– In version one, China carries the mail. We leave your brain where it is at this very moment, cut one single tract and put one person per axon at each of the two stumps. Radio in hand, they wait for a spike to arrive at their axon and then call it through to a colleague on the other side that stimulates the matching axon after the right amount of delay. The result: The synapses on the other side still get actual spikes, at the times when they'd have got them anyway.
– In version two, China does the sums. Now a part of your brain gets taken out, and people calculate on their sheets of paper what it would have sent, and radio the results in.

So with all that in mind (no pun intended), how does our little thought experiment impact the distributed brain idea? In version one I'd say that nothing that I care about has changed, as long as (!) the tract really was just wire. It's the Swiss car train example from yesterday's meditation, and it's a pretty small claim btw (something about getting signals across, nothing about what a mind is). In version two the physics of the missing part only gets calculated, which is yesterday's GPS map analogy. Block's nation is basically version two for all neurons at once, and a distributed brain only ever needs version one.

...and version one seems to be exactly what @The_Technocrat was pitching in a discussion on X; a biohybrid bridge as a growth avenue => new neurons wire themselves in by the brain's own rules, nothing gets read out or copied, and unlike replacing things in place, a bridge "opens room to grow". Whatever grows at the far end is a second node tho, so it depends on today's requirement.

In the same thread @guillefix described his current favourite => a BCI at near single-neuron resolution, a part of the brain that's "temporarily shut down while an external emulation of it is connected to all its inputs and outputs", and then "see how that feels, then revert back", repeatedly, blinded as far as that goes, with e.g. friends and family checking whether you're still yourself. Once you're confident, it doesn't have to be temporary. He names the weak spots himself (the emulation might fool you, and you don't miss what you've forgotten (some form of recursive p-zombie?)). I replied that this is usually referred to as dancing qualia ([consc.net]) on a smaller scale than hemispheres, and that it needs a flavour of computational functionalism and/or a "local engram theory". I promised in the thread on X to do this properly, so:

– If "emulation" means that the part gets calculated (that's how I read @guillefix), then this is version two from above. The actual problem with the test, I think, is who gets asked. The one who answers "feels the same" is the rest of the brain, which sits behind the interface and gets exactly the inputs that it always got. So it'd say "feels the same" whether the experience that the part used to contribute is still there or not, and this happens even if the test is blinded. E.g. Chalmers finds it absurd that experience could change without anybody noticing. I'm less sure, if you consider that noticing is something that a brain does like anything else, and that it can fail (as a strong case: people with Anton syndrome are blind and deny it).
– It also assumes that a part of the brain is something like a module, where all that it holds can be had at its inputs and outputs. That engrams aren't local is a hundred years old (Lashley's rats, then Pribram's and Pietsch's holograms, all in e.g. [smoothbrains.net]), and in mice the engram of one single memory is a connected complex across many regions (e.g.: [doi.org]). Disturbing one site also disturbs the rest => in animals, briefly silencing an area disrupted a learned skill, whereas a permanent lesion in the same area left it intact after a few days ([doi.org]). So "shuting it down and see" measures the rest of the brain in shock more than the part, and "revert" doesn't lead back to where you were, as the network has started to compensate in between.
– Which is what I meant by a "recompensation of engrams". What gradual replacement leans on is what Duffau's patients do, so the whole network compensating again after every small loss. A new piece has to take part in exactly that (the way a living and plastic tissue naturally does). Playing back the local engram that it was handed won't do, as there prob is no such thing.
– @The_Technocrat then asked whether this doesn't just give you a copy with its own world-line, and @guillefix's answer was that the region can be small enough to stay confident about continuity. I don't think that the size of the region helps tho => if every piece gets calculated, you end up with a calculated brain, however small the pieces were. Gradualness was never the point for me (see rule one below).

However, if it's possible, what would a distributed brain look like, and why would one even want this? The short answer to the why is fault tolerance. You can make a mechanistic brain out of the most durable stuff that you can think of, and there'll still be something that destroys it in one go (a bomb, or an asteroid for all I care), bc the whole thing sits in ONE place (one skull or one vat, or tbh one planet). That's what a single point of failure is. My essay from last year had a picture for the way out, with many sites that are far away and isolated from each other, so that a local attack takes out 2% and the process just goes on. And I don't think that I need to remind you of Galántai's scale from yesterday's Meditation, which is a way to rank civilisations based on how big of a disaster they can survive (or get wiped out by): You climb this scale by spreading out further, so that the next largest disaster can't reach and destroy all of you. The shrinking of the brain substrate from yesterday helps here, as light nodes are easier to spread around the world, as satellites or whatever.

So how far apart could the nodes be? Yesterday's formula (a delay is a length divided by a speed) can be read in both directions. If I take a slow carrier, the same delay fits into less space, which is what I did yesterday. But if I take a fast carrier, the same delay covers a lot more distance, and that's what I need today. Light in a glass fibre, e.g., would need about 20 ms from San Francisco to New York (if the fibre went in a straight line, which it doesn't), which is less time than the average callosal fibre takes to get across your head. So a brain that's spread over a whole continent would, in terms of delay, have no two parts further apart than your hemispheres are right now!

And is there a limit? There is, and somebody has already worked it out => Michael Timothy Bennett (@MiTiBennett) has a preprint from this year ([preprints.org]) in which the parts of one conscious moment have to be able to influence each other in both directions inside that moment's own window. From this he gets that "unity has a speed limit", so for a given signal speed a mind can only get so big. I read his description of this window as the width of the now from above from Husserl, which now comes with a number => for links at the speed of light and a now of 100 ms you get about 15000 km; so roughly the size of the Earth. If you stay on one planet, you'll probably be okay (most likely with mild perturbations). Time dilation, which I had on my list as a problem, is one of them and turns out to be harmless => your head already ages a tiny bit faster than your feet (measured over 33 cm, [doi.org]), and no brain cares, bc what it needs is the right timing between its parts and no common clock.

If you stretch from e.g. the Sun out to Neptune, heaven help you. The only way that I see to stay ONE mind over that distance is to make the now wider and so to run slower as a whole, in this case about three hundred thousand times slower, so a year outside would feel like about two minutes. Corollary: Climbing Galántai's scale as one mind has a price, as you get safer against anything local and slower to react to anything fast. Are you sure you want that? I don't really know what to do about that yet tbh.

What does all of this do to the process world-line? I'm having some difficulty articulating it, but here's a preliminary attempt. I propose the braid as an alternative picture/handle to the line. Problem with "line": It seems to imply that the process is in one place at each moment, which isn't even true for a brain in a skull (that already takes up 15 cm). In a braid every node is a strand, and what holds the strands together is the signals on their way between them. A braid stays one braid although no single strand has to run through all of it, which is day ten's continuer migration of my daily meditations challenge. However, a braid can also come apart, so how do we make this watertight, without a thought experiment left that would disprove it or, well, make it absurd? A summary of the rules that I have in mind (not meant to be a complete list):

1. Join before leave. A new part only counts as part of the process once it acts on the rest and the rest acts back on it, within the now. Only then may an old part go, and slowly (what the new part has to be made of is another question, days six and nine).

This is also where I'd answer Wiley and Koene's fallacy paper ([arxiv.org]). As I understand it, slow replacement, instantaneous replacement, and scan-and-copy all result in the same thing, atom for atom, so all three should yield the same result, and any line drawn by distance or speed will be arbitrary. I agree with a lot of this (the distance in metres is irrelevant, and I don't have a magic number of neurones per second). But consider what happens in their instantaneous case: the prosthetic neurones "passively observe" until someone flips a master switch. So they are not part of the same process before the switch, and nothing from the old process remains after it. Whether a process continued to run while the new parts were assembled is a physical fact about what occurred in the interim, and no inspection of the finished product will reveal it. Clas Weber answers them in a similar way, with immanent causation ([philarchive.org]).

2. No spares. Never keep a standby copy of a part just because that's how everything else gets made fault tolerant. A replica that only listens isn't part of the process (rule one), so switching to it after a failure would be survival by copy, which I reject. Assume instead that every node contributes, like in Duffau's patients, where other sites take part all along and take over more when a neighbour is lost (Edelman's degeneracy, [doi.org]). Consider the engineer who proudly reports that the failover was transparent, so that no user noticed a thing. In computing that's the goal. But can it be the goal for a mind? If you take a node out of a brain, you'd normally expect something to be missing, at least for a while. And if a node can die and nothing at all changes, then (I do hope we agree by this point) it wasn't contributing anything, so it was a spare. So the only fault tolerance that I can have is one where something degrades first and gets compensated afterwards, and I guess that this is what "graceful degradation" in the Ontology's strong form has to mean.

3. No fission. Take a brain that runs on two sites: A and B. Now, on one day the link between them two goes down (similar to e.g. a network partition). Site A reasons like this: Suppose B were still running, so then I'd hear from it (e.g. like ping for a health check in distributed software systems). If I don't hear from it, then B must be gone, and I carry on alone. On the other side of the cut, site B reasons the same about A. Each of them is being reasonable and both are wrong, and bc the link is down they can't even tell each other. So now there are two streams where there was one (distributed-systems people call this "split-brain"), and we're back at Parfit's question of which one is "me". The engineers' way out is called a quorum => only the side that still has the majority of the nodes may keep running, while the minority side goes dormant until it's connected again. With two equal sites that doesn't work, as neither has a majority (and two halves is how the human brain happens to be built btw => two nodes and no tie-breaker). For a brain I'd add that the minority side has to go dormant within one now, otherwise there'd be a moment with two, and that it can rejoin later.

Certainly all of this sounds like a nice-to-have, something for much later, but I do think that it must be an operational requirement. For instance, every piece that we replace and every bridge that we build is first of all a new node that has to become one with the rest, so the weak form is needed from the very first piece on. If we're not yet at a point where we can spread a whole brain over many locations, then we can at least start with one tract in an animal (as an example: cut it, bridge it with e.g. virtual axons, then check if the split-brain signs show up) and move up from there. The strong form is harder to justify to the same level, even though I think that it's the one that matters in the end => if we cannot distribute a brain in practice, then a mechanistic brain is still one object in one place, so a very durable single point of failure, which is "stop dying" and not yet a solution to death (day three of the Meditations). And if we cannot do it even in theory, then we have a problem with the whole idea of moving to a mechanistic substrate to make a mind truly resilient. What keeps me hopeful is that every brain already does it over 15 cm (!). As for the three rules, they seem reasonable to me, but I've only had one day for them, they aren't crisp in my head yet, and I don't really know how one would apply them in practice. So if you find the thought experiment that breaks one of them, write me!
