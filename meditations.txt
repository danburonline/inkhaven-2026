0/30 The Start

On the first day of this year, I created a small ontology editor (similar to, e.g. Protégé, just a lot simpler and without the formal OWL logic behind it) for the all-encompassing ontology* of my life and its vision, mission, goals, etc. It starts with the main vision (i.e. the future state of being I'm working towards), which is "synthetic consciousness". Simply summarised: engineered phenomenology on a designed substrate you could gradually become. I break it down into the mission, which is hybrid mind uploading (the most pragmatic way to achieve the same goals as classical mind uploading, just not by following the free-standing position of "creating something external to the brain that recreates the brain" but rather by building something that can be integrated into the thing at hand (i.e. your brain), hence the hybrid status in between. If you read some of my recent tweets, you'll know what I mean, as I've been talking about it in great detail + there's the upcoming manuscript on it. Then there are a few principles and rejections, some goals, one of which is quite practical: solve death (more on this below), and a few other things.

Btw, in September I already listed most of the terms I keep using and explained them in relation to each other (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2105088850536137170), but that was more of a glossary where the terms just stand side by side, whereas the ontology is a tree and therefore also says what sits under what. The tree itself is very pragmatic on purpose (I don't add text if it's not necessary; every word has a "right to exist"), which obviously also means that pretty much all the actual arguments behind those few words are either in my head, in old tweets and threads, or in manuscripts that aren't finished yet, and that's not really of much use to anyone but me...

So, as quickly described yesterday, I'm at Inkhaven (at Lighthaven) for the next 30 days anyway, and the plan is to work through the ontology and discuss (nearly) every item under the synthetic consciousness vision in public, one per day, as a meditation. I call them meditations in the sense of Descartes (Husserl picked that up again later with his Cartesian Meditations, e.g.), i.e. I write them in the first person and step by step, showing my thought process during writing, so that others as readers can go through it by themselves and see how I get to something (wrong turns included, I'll leave them in!), rather than just having to believe me. This makes everything a bit messier, but I do actually enjoy reading meditations myself, so here we go!

In every one of the meditations, I want to do two things basically. Obviously I have to argue for whatever the item claims, as the tree itself only states things (remember, every word has a right to exist, so there are no real arguments in there). But I also want to check the words themselves, i.e. what they're even supposed to mean, because terms like "continuer migration" or "world-line fidelity" can very easily sound a lot deeper than they are (a very Wittgensteinian worry), and whatever doesn't survive that has to go from the ontology again.

Here is a simple example of what I mean by this, using the goals as stated above for "solve death": Death, as I use it, is the irreversible end of the process world-line, the process world-line being the 4D trajectory of the physical-dynamical and organised bounded processes going on in your head that constitute what we call your brain. Not subscribing to any particular theory of consciousness basically lets you work with what you have at hand, which is the human brain, i.e. the main empirical object of investigation. The process itself starts at birth (technically slightly earlier, tbd) and ends when you die – it's one continuous thing (the "continuous" part can ofc also be attacked philosophically, more on that on the day we discuss it!). Either way, the premise: death is a contingent failure of the substrate. The move: swap the substrate out piece by piece without destroying the physical-dynamical and organised bounded processes – while hopefully introducing repairability/fault tolerance – so that the process hardens and becomes more resilient (i.e. the hardening of the process world-line).

Now, what I can actually argue for today is that the one biological brain you have keeps ageing and is therefore a single point of failure (= easy), and also that a different piece of brain can take over a function, as there's the serial lesion effect and Barrett's (2013) gradual reorganisation principle for that. What neither of them gives you is a different substrate taking over though, which is still open and basically the reason for the whole replacement work of ours at @synconeticsorg (so no, I can't argue for that part today). Also, "solved" doesn't mean immortal => the limits go from biological to "purely" physical ones, and the universe has plenty of those (heat death, eventually), so immortality is really its own question. Which makes me wonder if "solve" is even the right word for this goal? No idea yet tbh, we'll see when we get to it.

So the commitment for the next 30 days is one item of the ontology per day, as a public meditation here on my Twitter. Btw, nothing here is final, and some of it will change, as both the September list and the tree (OG tweet retweeted here => the screenshot) already have outdated bits, and if an item doesn't survive its own meditation, I'll rename it or throw it out and update the tree accordingly.

Tomorrow it's 1/30, which is the vision itself => synthetic consciousness! :-)

* PS: The term "ontology" might not be perfect or 100% correct here, it might be more of a taxonomy (or simply a hierarchy), i.e. from broad to small and well-defined, which is how the current version in my self-made editor works. However, I'd love to turn it more into a "what exists within a domain, plus the interconnections/relationships between those things and what follows from those relationships" (i.e. a proper ontology)


1/30 Causa Finalis

The first item in my Ontology (as discussed yesterday, with an uppercase "O" to denote "my ontology as discussed yesterday," rather than a lowercase "o" for any ontology or the general concept itself) is the "future state of being" => that's the entity "Vision", i.e. a state (as opposed to a way of getting there) that doesn't hold yet. Within the Ontology it's the "causa finalis", i.e. the end every other item in there is done for (the "that for the sake of which"), with no claim that it's the highest good: nothing matters except to someone conscious, and ordinary biological consciousness already meets that condition. So the end this Ontology is ordered to is that no consciousness has to end, and synthetic consciousness is that end as a state of being: consciousness that no longer has to end (the vision), and the route to it is hybrid mind uploading (the mission). Or, to put all of that in one line (which is basically today's whole meditation already): consciousness is the reason why its end matters at all, the world-line is the thing that actually has to continue, and "synthetic" is just there to say that the result is the real kind via a creation process. So I want to have synthetic consciousness (what exactly the term commits me to follows below) reached and then kept in the – hopefully near – future.

Now, what is "synthetic consciousness"? It's definitely not as common a term as e.g. artificial consciousness, as the term "artificial" is arguably (as you can assume) generally more widely spread due to e.g. artificial intelligence being as prominent as it is right now. That's actually also the reason for "artificial consciousness" being used more broadly for a bunch of things: the consciousness an AI system might have, the research field that studies it, and within that field two different aims known as weak and strong artificial consciousness (e.g. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05823), i.e. modelling the mechanisms of consciousness in software/robots without claiming that anything is experiencing anything vs building something that actually is conscious. I bring this up, as the term on its own doesn't tell you whether the real thing is meant, and "synthetic consciousness" gets listed as just another name for it, as on Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_consciousness), which is not how I use it, hence both words need their justification here. (And yes, there's also the adjacent term "machine consciousness", which I'll come to below, along with why "synthetic")

When choosing the word (or words) for the causa finalis, I wanted the "fewest words possible", as each word has to have a pragmatic right to exist and each additional one adds a certain attack surface. Hence I for sure started with "consciousness" as the term, and only later added "synthetic". I deliberately wanted to use "consciousness". From a physical perspective you might say, well, some fields or strings might be the most fundamental thing in the universe, and they might be => consciousness isn't necessarily the most fundamental thing in the universe, but it's the most fundamental thing to anyone in it. So no matter what the universe is ultimately made of, consciousness is the one thing I just can't doubt I have (or at least as the Descartes' Meditations have it: "ego sum, ego existo") and it's also the one thing without which nothing in the universe would matter to anyone. This only certifies the present though, meaning that I am right now, and it doesn't say anything about me going on => what carries the going-on is the world-line. And once consciousness is gone for good, nothing shows up for you ever again, and I think losing it is "bad" exactly because of everything it cuts you off from (that's Nagel's deprivation view btw, as opposed to Epicurus with his "death is nothing to us"). That's why its end is the thing worth solving, and also why the vision is about consciousness – and not e.g. about intelligence or life.

So "consciousness" was chosen by me for the term of the vision. Well, I can't just have "consciousness" as a vision (too simple, kind of defeats the whole point, too reductionistic), so it needs another term next to it for sure, and I don't want to use a neologism for that. Why "synthetic" as the first term then, and not e.g. "artificial" or "machine" as talked about before? That depends on what the vision actually is. My vision is that consciousness, starting with the consciousness that exists now, still exists in the future. However, consciousness as it is today, biological and "original", has one defect that matters here: it has to end. It's not the most durable state we can have (again, more on that below).

Generally, I care because I made it my life's mission ≈10 years ago to solve death. Solve death, meaning what? What is death? Without going too much into detail prematurely, as this is one of the goals of the Ontology (as e.g. written about yesterday), I want to put it simply: death is the irreversible end of a conscious world-line. That can be read in two ways though, and the two need to be kept apart, as they're two different problems. Global death: no conscious observers are left anywhere, so death rules the universe if there's no life, or at least no conscious observer, in the future. Individual death: this consciousness ends, i.e. one person's process world-line stops irreversibly. That's the one people actually fear, since for the one who dies it's the end of a world and not one event in a world that goes on ("at death the world does not alter, but comes to an end", Wittgenstein's Tractatus 6.431, btw!).

So, back to the vision: I want consciousness to still be in a future state, so no death, but focusing on my death, and yours (not deaths, not statistics in the sense of statistical likelihood of death occurring being reduced, not multiple consciousnesses, etc.). It's about individual death first. A resilient new mind that begins when I die doesn't achieve the vision => the vision is the conjunction, consciousness goes on and this consciousness goes on, and neither part satisfies it alone. Solve individual death from its first principles and, I'll argue, you end up with what I now call "hybrid mind uploading", and not classical copy-based uploading (that's the mission's day of the meditations challenge).

And on what day could I say death is solved? If the test is "death can no longer occur in the universe", then never (any structure can be destroyed, and there's heat death), a goal no one could ever check off. So I move the "must": today everyone has to die, and solved means no one has to end (some will choose not to continue, and things can still go wrong). Death goes from a certainty to a risk, and a risk is something you can measure and work on, although it has to keep falling, and fast enough, as a constant risk, however small, still gets everyone in the limit; it has to fall fast enough that all the yearly risks together add up to a finite total (sleep e.g., and the harder cases (e.g. hypothermic arrest), belong to the day on the 4D process world-line).

There are two approaches to keeping consciousness from ending. One is to make the default top-of-the-pyramid conscious thing (i.e. humans) fault tolerant as a species. Basically what Musk is after => replicate humanity across initially two planets in the same solar system, and obviously across more moons and planets soon, in order to increase fault tolerance through redundancy => if Earth dies or gets destroyed, we at least have a bunch of backups. But his vision doesn't say how consciousness is preserved in any more detailed manner, meaning he's fine if someone, and hopefully as many as possible, is around. I think this is great, his route is a real one. So in the global sense Musk is also a "solve death" person, but slightly different, he makes us fault tolerant as a species, and with that the chance of total death occurring (= no conscious observers) gets smaller, statistically speaking, but he's not focused on the first principles of death itself, which is what kept me busy a few years back. He's right, he just answers a different question: keeping some consciousness around is not the same as keeping this consciousness going. And this goes the other way around too, as ending individual death doesn't entail the end of global death. Undying minds that all live on one planet still die with the planet (you'd need the premise that no single event can end every world-line at once), so Musk's route is not my alternative, I need it as well – or at least wish for it in parallel!

The other approach goes at consciousness itself. The requirements of the vision, and its goals and opportunities, require things of consciousness that today's biology can't provide. Biological consciousness is first of all quite fragile, hence not resilient, meaning it can vanish quite fast in the first-principles view, as in, it's so easy to die, and for death to occur, in this universe. So the question becomes, how to make consciousness more resilient? Well, you can go into hardening things (borrowed terminology from software engineering), meaning can you harden biological consciousness? Can you fight against entropy as some people call it? If you go down that avenue of thought, as I did a couple of years ago (coming from classical mind uploading, as I was philosophically more naive back then), you most likely end up with the same conclusion as younger Daniel, which is "whatever state of consciousness you want to have and preserve, it needs to be some sort of hardened and therefore non-natural/initial-state consciousness in the future". And that's where I think most people go into terminologies such as "resilient consciousness", or one further, "substrate-independent consciousness", which implies fault tolerance in the substrate and an easy distribution across locations and easy transfer from one substrate to the other, and a bunch of other things (an assumption I think is untested; more on its own day in the challenge). Or you settle indeed with "well, if we can engineer consciousness (as some argue, even without understanding it), then we can potentially create de novo consciousness, with all of its implications".

So, what is this hardened de novo consciousness? Biological consciousness engineered to last, or consciousness re-created somewhere, in either a biological brain or maybe something non-biological, as substrate independence e.g. implies? Follow this logic and you end up somewhere funny (it's a reductio, to be clear – of the idea that new + hardened is enough): technically you can, based on that reasoning, come to the same conclusion as Musk, that creating biological consciousness in a hardened, de novo way is actually just giving birth to it. The best way to create de novo hardened consciousness is to have a baby (de novo in that context meaning it's newly created, independent of your consciousness or any other consciousness + it's hardened, because it's young, with little accumulated damage). So you indeed end up with the same reasoning as Musk, so why care? Well, it's hardened, but not really, and above all it's new: a baby is a new world-line and not the continuation of anyone's, so by going into a generational approach you never touch individual death. De novo + hardened isn't enough, as a newborn already meets both and solves nothing => what's missing is continuity, the same world-line made resilient. So, if you focus on the first principles of death (this consciousness ending, and not just some consciousness being around), you need this hardened de novo substrate to indeed be more engineered. So from here on, "de novo" means the substrate only: a designed substrate, and the consciousness on it is the one that's already there, a process that could come to run on it gradually (argued on the mission's day tomorrow).

So, it's artificial? In one sense yes, in the sense that matters no. What does artificial even mean? It goes back to the Latin artificium (craft or skill), so made by art or skill and not by nature, and in that sense an engineered substrate obviously is artificial. But the word has come to mean made in imitation of something natural, a substitute (artificial flowers, artificial sweetener, etc.), and the important part here is "it's not the thing it imitates". I don't want to have, as a future vision/state of being, a bunch of "not real consciousness" around, so I had issues calling the top-most item in the Ontology "artificial consciousness".
So why not use what many people have started using, which is "machine consciousness" as mentioned earlier? Well, I have my tail of issues with the term "machine", as it implies we're creating a machine (which isn't true per se, or at least I don't want to imply it already from day 1), but it also invites the reading that a mind is something you can lift out and move into another machine that we engineer. And that's basically computational functionalism, to a certain degree already frontloaded, which is one of the three positions I reject (each of them gets its own day in the meditations challenge), so I really can't have it baked into the name of the vision.

So, what term is the right one? Well, after years of thought I settled on "synthetic" => take sugar. If you make sucrose in a lab (which was first done in 1953), you still end up with sucrose, so the very same molecule, just made via another route, whereas an artificial sweetener like e.g. aspartame is a totally different molecule that only mimics the sweetness. So for me, synthetic means the same kind of thing, but just made by another route. And artificial means an imitation of the thing (e.g. Herbert Simon draws pretty much the same line with gems in "The Sciences of the Artificial" btw, and yes, everyday usage is messier, as synthetic leather obviously isn't leather, so I mean it the way e.g. chemists would use the word).

Now, this of course commits me to synthetic consciousness being the same kind as biological consciousness, so real and not an imitation, and to be clear, that's what the vision hopes for, and nothing that the word itself could ever establish. With sugar you have the chemistry, and with a diamond you have its lattice structure to "show and validate" that it's indeed the same kind. But what plays that exact role for consciousness? That's a question of scale separability as well as coarse graining and indeed a thing we should talk about as well (and as long as that's open, "real" is more of a wish than a description, so I still owe that answer on a later day of the challenge too...). So, meaning, that for now I actually only use "synthetic" as a term for "the real kind" – independently of how it is produced/created. The word specifies the target, but the question if that target can actually be reached, remains an open question to be answered. Additionally, the word only names the kind. As an example: sucrose from the lab is a new molecule and not the old one that somehow got "carried over", which means that the word alone cannot tell you that "a synthetic new me" is still "me", even though I use "synthetic" and not "artificial" (as that part has to come from the process worldline).

To conclude for now: This future state of being, as the "root and start" of the Ontology (first in intention, last in execution, as Aquinas puts it) labelled as the entity "Vision" (its root node or causa finalis: the end everything else in the Ontology is done for) has a bunch of implications and relations, as already hinted at earlier, which the coming days take up one by one.

2/30 What Could Be Hybrid about Mind Uploading?

The next item in the Ontology is the mission, which, as I understand and use it, is the undertaking that leads to the vision (and "hybrid mind uploading" is its name), with the vision being a state of being in the future described as "there will be synthetic consciousness", as discussed in great detail yesterday (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2121143118062031238). One thing became quite clear yesterday: you cannot define a single item in the Ontology without first defining its relations to other items, which makes linear-style writing in a left-to-right language such as English quite hard without anticipating some of the other items in the Ontology. The one already mentioned was hybrid mind uploading.

It seems that, in order not to stay with naturally produced consciousness, due to all the things discussed yesterday (it's fragile, hence not resilient, and above all it has to end), but in order to go into a new state that is meant to continue the same process world-line, just no longer naturally produced, therefore "'something' consciousness", for which we used the term "synthetic" as in "the same kind, but created/engineered", the great focus has to be on the "well, what is the process?".

Over the past few years, I've been slowly coming out of the field of (classical) mind uploading (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1737536299500794019, https://x.com/danburonline/status/2028201588083564604, and, as the final push, https://x.com/danburonline/status/2117425368277946626). After I had defined it to be my life's mission to solve death (as described yesterday), I first thought that the most pragmatic way is to focus on consciousness as the most fundamental thing, and then try to preserve that with the most resilience and upshots to other things ("opportunities", as I called them yesterday), and that this would be mind uploading: scan a brain in as much detail as possible, store it somewhere and then simulate it in order to emulate consciousness on a different substrate, hence a computer. This is what I define as "classical mind uploading" nowadays, btw. So I was never technically interested in other forms of rejuvenation or gene therapies, because they don't focus on the first principles of death; they keep the process world-line within the limits of biology, and they don't address the full range of vulnerabilities I want to overcome.

Now, the problem with this classical approach as described before, as some of you might know if you've been following me on Twitter in the past few years (e.g. https://x.com/danburonline/status/2101434507098538404 and https://x.com/danburonline/status/2102530467203289336), is that there are a bunch of issues, which I do not want to repeat again (I once called it "dying with extra steps": https://x.com/danburonline/status/1959952683374088678), especially because those rejections and criticisms are actually other Ontology items for the daily meditations challenge in the coming days.

The important story background here is that at some point, I wanted to distance myself completely from the "mind uploading" term, as I saw all the issues and wasn't even a fan of the terminology of "mind" and "uploading" on its own. What is a mind? What is uploading? (I'll keep both words for now btw, and the first question gets its answer in a later meditation) Uploading is so frontloaded with computer science terminology, and to be fair, a term that's borrowed from another discipline isn't a problem by itself; it only becomes one when its familiar use quietly decides what has to be true in the new discipline as well, so in the minds of the researchers and people (Wittgenstein style again). I was obviously not the first person to have issues with this; afaik @randalkoene had similar concerns, and around 1998 he named the academic approach "whole brain emulation". "Mind uploading" came down from the Hans Moravec lineage, front-loaded with even more sci-fi connotations, which might be a net negative for a scientific, serious discipline.

Now, I thought that "whole brain emulation" is a great term (Randal Koene later also used "substrate-independent minds" btw), so I was looking into terming the mission with something around it, e.g. candidates such as "invariant brain emulation" (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1939759548446695785), in order to imply other aspects of the Ontology, e.g. that the thing we want to recreate has no self-standing position, but actually integrates into the brain at hand, and also that the process world-line doesn't break, and other things (as late as April this year I still called myself "a WBE person, but not via simulations": https://x.com/danburonline/status/2045451947109478566). But I had a few issues with that. Why WHOLE? What does this mean? It already implies "just" the brain to a certain degree; therefore, some degree of brain-centredness of consciousness, which might be too much of a leap of faith already. Also, brain? Why just the brain? Even more brain-centredness (and yes, I'm aware that the slide in my own Ontology says "make the brain hybrid", but the brain is where we start, and that's no claim about where consciousness has to live). And emulation? Emulation is, I guess, an even worse ambiguous term than uploading, because emulation is mostly understood as JUST a software term (which it doesn't have to be btw: https://x.com/danburonline/status/1939742893691781259), and based on my own experience it's actually sometimes used interchangeably with simulation. So I ran into a few issues with terming this mission item in the Ontology.

After a few years, I basically realised, especially when starting to pitch this to a few people, friends, investors, that using a neologism is bad, as it's front-loaded with too much abstraction. I always admired pitches of people who could just use "XYZ but for XYZ", so using something familiar and then mapping it, e.g. onto your problem space. At some point, I remember, I realised that actually "mind uploading" is a great term, as it implies, even for most people who are not in the space at all, things such as "immortality, solving death, mind augmentation and enabling a new state of humanity" and a few more. I liked that, after all. And before somebody calls that a double standard, as "uploading" is obviously a computer science term too: I keep the word for what it promises, and I drop its old method, meaning that with hybrid mind uploading I don't mean reconstructing a separate instance somewhere and then treating that copy as my survival.

But I did not like the immediate association with mind uploading being a subdiscipline of computer science and nowadays AI, and that it's mostly, and nearly only if you look at the literature (look at previous posts here: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2101079894524657783 and here: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2102159432742207693), used for "scan, store, simulate" => all engineering efforts are within "recreating a self-standing brain from brain scans, sometimes even fully destructive". So the process world-line discussion rarely came up, and never at the centre, and the aspect of knowing if a simulation will be sufficient for a full emulation, or if it's even able to have phenomenal consciousness, is a recurring issue (e.g. https://x.com/danburonline/status/2020877708016701876, and something more to discuss in the meditation where I'll discuss the Synconetics programme in the coming days).

So I thought of "moving the original goals of mind uploading, which are solving death, mind augmentation and enabling a new state of humanity", as e.g. popularised in the Hans Moravec lineage (Moravec's own robot-surgeon procedure in "Mind Children" from 1988 was already a gradual one btw, but its end state was still the mind as a computer program), from it being a subdiscipline of CS and AI into a discipline(s) with fewer metaphysical claims and assumptions (so fewer metaphysical commitments, and ones that experiments can at least bear on, and not zero, as the process world-line is still one of them), and to make the remaining assumptions explicit and investigate them, instead of taking leaps of faith (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1981771299547676917, and for the functionalism part of it https://x.com/danburonline/status/2045713910590792122 and https://x.com/danburonline/status/2074431130917367973). So I denoted whatever mind uploading via whole brain emulation used to be as "classical mind uploading", and whatever is new as "XYZ mind uploading".

Now the question was: what term to use for XYZ? As the idea (one of many ideas, as you will see in the coming days as well) is to go through/via a hybrid state of the thing that carries the process world-line into the synthetic consciousness state as the causa finalis, we will enter a hybrid state on the way from whatever was the starting point (i.e. biologically sustained consciousness) to the end state (synthetically sustained consciousness). Hence: hybrid (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2074440223975469109). And to answer the question from the title as well: by "hybrid" I mean that biological and engineered parts are carrying one and the same process at the same time, so the thing that is hybrid is what carries the consciousness, and never the consciousness itself (so please don't picture some "half-synthetic" experience here, as synthetic means the same kind, see yesterday). It's actually that simple. And by "process world-line" I mean a physical claim about the process itself that does the carrying, and not a report of "how continuous it feels" => as a copy would feel continuous too! And if this particular consciousness really gets preserved via that transition is the aim of the whole mission, and nothing that the word "hybrid" could establish by itself (if the hybrid state is only a passage, or if somebody could also just stay hybrid, is a question for another day as well). But for today I can only say this: I call the consciousness "synthetic" once it's sustained by created and engineered parts, and whether the hybrid state already counts as that is exactly the question I'm leaving open (at least for now).

What this all implies, and all the issues that I have with classical mind uploading, and what assumptions and axioms we still have in hybrid mind uploading, is part of the Ontology when we go down in more detail, and therefore part of another day (and for those who can't wait: the post distinguishing classical from hybrid https://x.com/danburonline/status/2033585780946309408, the river as the border between the two https://x.com/danburonline/status/2059105548172406825, "The Alien Neuron" https://x.com/danburonline/status/2104351025863840183, and why it's harder than classical, but better https://x.com/danburonline/status/2116051326119424366).


3/30 Solving Death

I run a company called @eightsixscience, where we try to functionally isolate the brain (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2104638041583440383); in other words, we take living brains out of their bodies and keep them alive outside their original bodies for as long as possible. We have early success in doing so in rodents; we're working toward the same for a large-animal brain, and ultimately we want to do this for humans as well. When I tell people about this – friends, family, people I meet at scientific conferences, investors or also just random people I meet throughout life (e.g. my hairdresser, who I recently told what exactly I'm doing) – I usually get the one question that gets asked most: "Why are you doing this?" I simply answer: "Because I want to solve death, and functionally isolating brains is, according to my own reasoning, the most pragmatic route towards that goal". And then usually the question comes right after: "But, why? Why do you want to solve death?"

The short answer, before I ramble on for way too long: it's complicated, and no, I still don't have a clean one after ≈10 years of getting asked. I can tell you why death is bad for the one who dies; that part I'm fairly sure about. But why exactly me, why I can't put this thing down again? No idea, and it gets worse the longer I think about it – same as with "what has to be true along the way" as well as "when would we even call it solved"; because every time I answer one of those, I do actually end up with a new question instead: what even is a world-line, what makes a continuation worth having, and so on (which is btw exactly why solving death is one of the three hard goals that the mission has to achieve in the Ontology).

Depending on who asks, I've come to several answers over the years, depending on the person, the context, and the time available. It ranges from personal upbringing reasons, e.g. my mom going through the Yugoslav wars and telling me stories as a child about all the terrible things that she had seen (hence developing a morbid and catastrophic view on death early on), or that I was depressed as a teenager and prob read the wrong kinds of books in a row that led me, back then, towards thinking that death is actually the only answer (that phase is long over btw). Now, the thing is, and I have to be strict with myself here, that both of these stories answer a question that nobody asked. When somebody asks me "why do you want this?", they could mean "what made you want it?" or they could mean "what makes it worth wanting?". Those are actually two very different things => the first one is a cause and the second one a reason (and my mom's stories and my teenage bookshelf are e.g. obv causes). They tell you how I ended up with death as my main topic. But if the goal itself is any good: they don't tell you at all (and tbh, they also can't tell you that it's bad. People do try that one, and it's the genetic fallacy, just used in the other direction!). The usual comeback here is "so it's just your fear of death talking", and I think that one deserves an honest answer: yes, maybe, I even posted a quote once saying that one of the principal things that moves people is the terror of death (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1847583999683661992). But you can play the exact same game with the other side! For basically all of human history there was nothing, like really nothing, that anybody could do about it, so what else would you do other than making your peace with it (Elster calls this adaptive preferences, or just "sour grapes"). So fine, my wish can be explained away, but so can everybody else's acceptance, and then explaining away doesn't decide anything here.

Other avenues of explanation are usually more mythological, as in: death always seemed to be humanity's forbidden fruit and life in general; it's the one great barrier one shall not pass, and if you're curious, you obviously love to explore that boundary. Again, curiosity is a cause and no reason, but the "shall not pass" part deserves a closer look, as nearly every culture has a story that warns you about going there (e.g. Gilgamesh loses his plant of youth to a snake, and Tithonus gets his eternal life without the eternal youth). Even my own "forbidden fruit" picture fits btw, as in Genesis the forbidden fruit is strictly speaking knowledge, and it's the tree of life that gets guarded afterwards, so that nobody eats from it and lives forever. People sometimes hand me these stories as an argument, as in "humanity has always known that this is a bad idea". I read them as the wisdom of a time in which nothing could be done, so they belong to the same making-peace as above, and if you look closely, what most of them warn against is a bad version of not having to die (ageing forever, being alone forever), which nobody is asking for. The one reading that's left is the hubris one, that it's against the order of things, and "the order of things" is no argument by itself, as the night is part of that order as well, which brings me to the lightbulbs.

So, more societally as an explanation, one of the analogies I've recently been bringing up during conversations is the example of pitching the lightbulb in front of people working on candles in the early 1800s. Instead of accepting the limitations of candles for brightening up the night, which is what we've done for thousands of years as Homo sapiens, why not go beyond those limits by inventing magically glowing things called lightbulbs, whose light doesn't have to burn down the way a candle does? It's coming from a totally different angle to solve the same problem, which is: keep it bright for as long as you want (you see the analogies I'm drawing slowly creeping in!). When working on lightbulbs, you don't iterate on candles; you approach it with different things. And that's the beauty, because the upshot of solving "it being bright at night, against nature's reason to make it dark at night due to the earth blocking the sunlight" is that we ultimately needed to work on electricity, have constant access to electricity, DC then AC, and this led towards the electrification of a bunch of things, and ultimately also to the digital computer, and then AI, AGI and superintelligence (quite literally btw: the "Edison effect" was found inside a lightbulb in the early 1880s, Fleming turned it into the first vacuum tube in 1904, and the first electronic computers were rooms full of the descendants of that tube). Who would have thought that the people working on lightbulbs back in the day were working on this uphill technology with a tail that leads us towards superintelligence? I usually say that solving death kind of feels like this nowadays. It seems like, for the first time, we have enough of the technology and knowledge to start seriously, so let's try. The upshot of this is unknown, and nobody can predict it; we can only guess. A few people like Nikola Tesla predicted, quite accurately, some future scenarios back then, but I don't think this is possible anymore, and I don't know anything about what the future has in store for us when we solve death, other than that solving death is just step one and that life in a post-death society will be stranger than we can imagine (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1981584148520108391).

I used to end this pitch with "but whatever it is, it must be net positive, otherwise it will be filtered out either way" (basically "dialectic" in society), and I have to take that part back, as it doesn't survive my own scrutiny. Bad things can stick around for a very long time before a society filters them out, and some things do the filtering on us instead. So let me say what I think the analogy really gives me. It shows the kind of move, as the improvement path of the candle (better wax, better wicks) never arrives at the lightbulb, and the candle here is biology. You can make it burn longer, and I'm all for that, but, e.g. reversing ageing halts the dying and doesn't solve death, as you can still get hit by a bus (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2105065734753698254). It also shows that the upshots came through the infrastructure that the solution needed (electricity), which nobody working on it could foresee, and I expect the same here, as you can't work on this without understanding and engineering the thing that carries consciousness. And it takes care of the "order of things" from above, as nobody thinks today that the lightbulb was an offence against the night. Now, does any of this tell me that the result will be good? No, and no analogy ever could; I mean, it's lightbulbs. And there's one place where my nice analogy just falls apart, and it's kind of the most important place => continuity. If a lightbulb is off and you swap it for a new one, nobody cares; the light is simply "on again". With a consciousness, that's the whole difference between going on and being replaced (the 4D process world-line gets its own day). And what's left of my "it must be net positive" is weaker; I'm afraid => the direct effect is good if death is bad for the one who dies (next paragraph), and for everything that comes after that, I really don't know.

Now, to come back, I technically have even more examples, and they range from the personal "life is great, death is bad, so I want more of life" to the very logical reasons as described in one of the earlier meditations (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2121143118062031238), to the very visionary views such as the lightbulb scenario above. Out of these, the logical one is the one that carries the most weight for me (it's the central reason, if you want), so I'll go through it once more, slowly. It starts with the thing from day one => nothing matters except to someone conscious. Death is the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line, so when such a world-line ends, everything that would have come after just doesn't happen for the someone that this world-line is, and if that would have been worth having, then missing out on it is bad for that someone (that's Nagel's deprivation view; I didn't invent this). The "worth having" matters a lot btw, as it's the reason why the goal in the Ontology says "no one has to end" and why it never said, "no one is allowed to end". Also, yes, there are some well-known objections (e.g. Epicurus, Lucretius, "death gives life its meaning", "we have to make room", and so on, etc.), but I'll potentially talk about those things on another day, especially as today's meditation is getting way too long already...

OK, so that's the "why-is-death-bad"-part, and I think it holds up. But did you notice? I still haven't answered the question, as "why is death bad" and "why do YOU want to solve it" are just two different questions, and the second one is so much harder, which is kind of the main thing that I want to deliver today. A person can have every reason in their hands and yet still not know exactly why they actually want it. Arguments don't make people magically want things (on that part, Hume was right!). Plenty of people nod at every single step above and then go on with their lives as if nothing happened. So why do I want to solve death? Well, I don't know. Personal urge? I guess this is where the causes from the beginning come back in, as the reason is there for everybody, and my mom's stories and the wrong books are why I'm one of the people who can't put it down again. To put it simply: I will try doing so, otherwise I'll die either way, so who cares? That last part might sound like a joke, but I think there's actually a real argument hiding in it. Say nothing changes => I die, like everybody before me, that's the baseline. Say I try, and it doesn't work out => I die as well, so the same thing, except that there's whatever the attempt produced along the way. And if I try and it does work (for me, I mean), then that's obviously better, and if it only works for the people after me, then it's a gain for them and none for me, I'm aware of that. So for me, as far as I can see, trying is at least as good as not trying. There are two "buts" though. The attempt shouldn't cost me a life that I'd rather have lived (it doesn't – I love this work), and nothing worse than simply dying should be reachable along the way, and that second one doesn't come for free, nothing guarantees it, somebody has to design for it blabla. I know of two such outcomes on my own route btw. A conscious brain that's kept going without any input, without any way to act and without anything that protects it from suffering would be one of them, and I'd call that worse than death (more on that below). The other one is a "solution" that only produces a copy, or something that merely behaves like me, which people would walk into believing that they'd go on, and which is the reason why I only want engineering means that need no metaphysical gambling (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1991815846927184026), as that would be worse than no solution at all.

"Solving death" is one of the three goals of the Ontology (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2105088850536137170), and with the above in place I can say more exactly what it implies. The Ontology has a vision (synthetic consciousness, so consciousness that no longer has to end, which is a state), a mission (hybrid mind uploading, which is the undertaking) and the goals, which are what the mission has to achieve. Now, somebody who read day one carefully could say: wait, isn't "solving death" just the vision again, in other words? I asked myself the same, and I think it isn't, and the easiest way to see it is this => imagine that there's exactly one synthetic consciousness around, and everybody else still dies like today. Then you'd have a first instance of the vision, and you'd be nowhere near the goal. So with the vision, I'm talking about a state that should exist at all, and with the goal, I mean every single someone: your world-line doesn't have to end, and neither does anybody else's. It's also the one part that you can share with me without sharing my route, btw, as somebody who works on purely biological ways has the same goal (even if I think that biology alone can't get them there) and nothing else of the Ontology in common with me.

What is death? => the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line. In last year's article (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1922674922335207806) I called it the irreversible destruction of the physical and dynamic processes of consciousness, which is the same thing seen from the engineering side. And the one word in there that I had to think about the longest is "irreversible", because, well, irreversible according to whom, and when? A stopped heart was the end until CPR showed up. Then the end got moved into the brain, and nowadays surgeons cool brains down until the EEG is flat (nuanced detail: until EEG electrodes can't measure anything meaningful anymore!) and people still wake up as themselves, so medicine keeps pushing the point where a stop turns into an end further out (where exactly that point is, is for the meditation day on the 4D process world-line). I mean the strict version => death is the end that can't be undone by anything that physics allows. This also implies that "solving death" can never be about bringing somebody back from an end, only about never letting a stop turn into one (yes, this has implications for biostasis, where the open question is exactly if it's a stop or an end!). Preventing that point from ever being reached is the problem that I mean, and that one is an engineering problem (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1884645061310218306).

What is solving death? Solving meaning that death does not occur in the universe anymore? Well, not really. You can solve a thing practically, and it can still happen. We "solved flying" by making things fly (Icarus didn't stop anybody in the end), but you can still have things meant to fly that cannot fly, and planes that come down. Meaning, you can solve death, and death can still occur in the universe. The analogy only goes so far btw, as a steady crash rate is fine for flying and exactly not enough for death. I quickly discussed this on day one, and there's a big difference here that I want to be exact about, as three things get mixed up all the time. Step zero is to stop dying. If you halt the dying (the ageing and e.g. the failing organs), you get what one might call amortality => no clock in you runs out by itself anymore. However, a bus can still hit you, and a risk that just stays where it is still gets everybody in the end => only later. Step one is solving death => and that's EXACTLY what today's Ontology goal is about: no one has to end from any cause that engineering could potentially get rid of. So the remaining risk has to keep falling, but also fast enough so that all together the yearly risks add up to a finite total. A finite total is where the "has to" stops, and from there on, the smaller that total gets, the less likely it is that death still occurs at all, which is all that "solved" can mean for something that can still happen, and it's what I meant by making it very unlikely that death still occurs (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1981787544229482698). And then there's immortality, where death can't occur at all anymore, which is something that one can seek once death is solved, and nothing that I'd put into a goal. Stop dying and achieve amortality, then solve death and seek immortality, that's the order (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2065005015811391946).

So when I said "towards" at the beginning of today's meditation, I meant it literally. Functionally isolating the brain is step zero, the stopping of the dying, and no solution to death per se (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2069784386606698755). Most of us die because something in the body fails first and the brain goes down with it. That's why I say that this is the urgent part, and you can do it for somebody without having solved a single identity/philosophical puzzle before, as it's the same brain that simply keeps going (this is also kind of a very medical systems view btw, as any doctor is doing!). But a conscious brain that's just kept going is worth nothing if it's no life worth continuing, so input, some agency as well as protection from suffering are part of step zero itself! Mechanistic gradual brain replacement, the core of hybrid mind uploading, is step one (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2104692700243247309), and, according to my own reasoning, it's the kind of route on which the remaining risk can keep falling at all (and no, a backup doesn't count, a backup is a copy). So yes, I do think that functional brain isolation, combined with mechanistic gradual brain replacement, is probably the best shot, and the most pragmatic shot, at going towards solving death. They're both bets tho, and if a world-line truly went on via a replacement has to be shown physically (=> on the process that "does" the carrying, as a copy would tell you the same story (it's just a copy)). So all I did in today's meditation was to tell what the goal in my Ontology is, and why I think it is worth wanting.

One last thing: I deliberately stopped the definition at the universe itself, as a risk that keeps falling forever only helps if there is actually a forever to begin with (no pun intended), and if anything can go on through the heat death (or whatever other end stage it'll be) is open physics afaik => so no more engineering, "just" cosmology. So, yes, that's where I am with my thoughts after all these years. I can tell you why it's probably worth wanting, but I still can't tell you cleanly enough why I want it this badly – as well as that I also can't tell you (yet) when it would count as "goal achieved". The annoying part is that the last two get harder and harder the more I think about them – which is, I guess, why it's among the three hard goals of the Ontology!


4/30 Measurements, Models and Theories

The second goal of the Ontology is simply called "understand consciousness". To be clear about which goals I mean: the Ontology (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2105088850536137170) has three of them, which are "solve death" (that was yesterday: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2121890643983151389), understand consciousness and human transcendence, and they're no longer identical with the original goals of mind uploading that I listed on day two. What I mean by this second one is that in order to make hybrid mind uploading work as a mission towards the vision of synthetic consciousness, one will definitely have to solve death, as the first goal, but probably, and that's a big probably, also understand consciousness. I do believe that in the coming years, especially due to the progress in artificial intelligence, we will see a great "rigorisation of science", and especially of the consciousness sciences as well. The reason being that we can now reason across hundreds of papers, or across whole piles of data, at the same time, and with that we uncover many of the inconsistencies in thinking and in terminology, and also the data flaws and mistakes that we've made in science up to this point (computational philosophy is basically the same move btw). In the consciousness sciences, this matters even more, as that field can't hide its measurement problem and is being forced to work out what even counts as a test of a theory (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2109975016020312543). You can already see the first signs of it btw, e.g. in the adversarial collaborations, where theories with conflicting predictions get tested against each other. I also assume that the overfocus on neurocomputational functionalism will fade away, as right now it's mostly held as a faith rather than tested as a hypothesis (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2074431130917367973), and that we'll look back at those days and wonder how quickly we anthropomorphised anything that outputs "I'm angry" (the angry chair from an earlier tweet: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2026235270438244844).

And due to this rigorisation, I do expect that we'll get somewhere with consciousness. Probably not to a theory, but probably to a model, or at least to a measurement theory, and as these three get mixed up all the time, let me say how I use them (for the first one, see Johannes Kleiner and his workshop "Towards a Measurement Theory for Consciousness Science": https://x.com/danburonline/status/2026031321772958204). A measurement theory is the most "modest" of the three, as it only tells you what kind of data count at all (e.g. reports, behaviour, neural markers, etc.), and how to read that data as experience => it doesn't say why. With a model, you can already predict which experience goes with which physical state. And a theory, well, that would say what consciousness is and why there is any of it in the first place. Why would one precede the other? Because you can calibrate well before you can explain. So what I expect is measurement first, within years, then predictive models, and a theory of what consciousness is may never be reachable by science at all. And no, measurement doesn't come for free either, as what you think consciousness is already shapes what you accept as a measurement of it (that's Birch's point: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2044766948349645110), so it's measurement before a full theory, with the two correcting each other in turns.

The nuanced difference here, and why this is in the Ontology at all, is that while working on what I nowadays call biomimetic neuromorphics (it takes the slot that whole brain emulation has in classical mind uploading, but in hybrid mind uploading), we do what I call "consciousness engineering" (which is also the name of the Frontiers Research Topic that I co-organised: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2061138328905552133). Meaning: we engineer with matter that we try to couple and integrate with the biological brain, so that experience extends across both parts (that's what I mean by "bridging" phenomenal consciousness), with the aim of ultimately letting it "host" synthetic consciousness. And my bet is that measurement theories, and maybe even models of consciousness, will emerge from exactly this kind of work. And yes, understanding consciousness is such a huge goal, so why is it "only" the second one? I order the three goals by what depends on what (and no longer by importance): solving death is what all of this is for, understanding consciousness is what's needed to check that death really got solved (more on that in a second), and human transcendence, the third goal (and the meditation for tomorrow!), presupposes both, as it's even more far-reaching than solving death, comes at, or shortly after, the inflexion point of solving death, and is actually coupled with some of the outcomes of the Ontology. I do make a difference between goals and outcomes btw: a goal is something that we strive for and could fail at, and an outcome is what follows either way, as long as we work on the mission. So why isn't understanding consciousness just an outcome then? My first answer was "because we should strive to do that", which is no reason yet. The reason is that hybrid mind uploading can sidestep a theory of consciousness when it comes to building the thing that carries the process, but it can't sidestep measuring, as without a way to tell that experience is still there, and that it's still this experience, we could never know if the mission succeeded (that's the point that I left open yesterday btw, that it has to be shown physically if a world-line really went on). Hence a goal, at least up to the level of measurement, and no mere outcome.

I always say that the whole point of hybrid mind uploading is to not need a theory of consciousness in order to make it work, or to have confidence in it (as in classical mind uploading via simulations, where one has to believe that it's going to work due to a theory of consciousness, an unproven one, which might be wrong), but to actually sidestep it. So not ignoring the problem, but sidestepping it! Ignoring the theories completely would be insanity, as you'd end up assuming one anyway, just without noticing (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2106302394980049359). What sidestepping means is that hybrid mind uploading doesn't let its success depend on any single theory of consciousness being the only true one. It assumes a few things openly (the process world-line is one of them => I hold it as a hypothesis, and yes, yet again, this is for another meditations challenge day!), and the idea is that rival theories become predictions that a measurement can test. So instead of betting on just a single one of them, the route should be built in a way so the question can be checked gradually/step by step. It's, e.g. similar to how steam engines came before thermodynamics (Newcomen had his engine running in 1712, and Carnot explained what such an engine does in 1824), or to how Faraday had a wire rotating around a magnet in 1821, the first electric motor basically, long before anybody knew what an electron is (Thomson, 1897). You don't need to understand electrons in order to build an electric motor. The analogy breaks at one place tho, and well, yes, of course it's the place that matters a lot: you can see from the outside that a motor works; it turns. You can't see from the outside that a world-line went on. And that's exactly why the measuring part can't be sidestepped => what has to be shown is the same experience before, during and after a replacement step, from the reports as well as from physical markers.

It's all about doing something useful, and for doing something useful we're using an engineering mindset and not a science mindset (hence clearly consciousness engineering, and not consciousness science). However, I do think that this goal in the Ontology is where the science can slip back in a bit, as, like I said earlier, one can probably solve the measurement problem of consciousness with this work, or at least contribute heavily towards it, and then, with that, maybe even go towards very pragmatic modelling of consciousness. The example from another discipline that fits best here is temperature, I think. Thermometers as well as fixed scales (Fahrenheit, 1724) were around long before a kinetic theory explained what temperature even is (1850s–1870s). Hasok Chang showed in "Inventing Temperature" how people got out of this circle there as you can't calibrate a thermometer without knowing what temperature really is (and you can't know what temperature is without a thermometer in the first place, so every round of measuring corrected the concept, and also the other way around). So, did we achieve the goal of "understand consciousness" when we "only" have a model of consciousness instead of a theory? Well, not really, I guess? Or let me be more exact, as "understand" comes in three levels for me => measuring it, modelling it and explaining it. The goal counts as reached at the second level, and the third one is the horizon, where I don't know if science can ever get to. Why the second and not already the first? Because measuring only tells you afterwards, and for a step that you can't undo you want to know before, so the model that I'm asking for has to be able to tell me before a replacement step if the experience will change.

Trying to answer this question properly is a Pandora's box tho, or rather a deep tunnel of issues that we're opening here (which is btw kind of on purpose why I added this goal to the Ontology). The reason being: what is a theory of consciousness even? It might be that the scientific method as we have it isn't equipped to answer such questions, as it might sit on the same level of investigation as "what is an electron?", "why is there an electron?" and "why is there stuff rather than no stuff?". Careful though, as these are three different questions and I shouldn't ask them in one breath: the first one is a question for physics, the second one asks why the laws are as they are, and the third one is Leibniz's question. Science is great at the first kind, and my worry is that a theory of what consciousness is belongs to the other two, so basically just one level above absolute explanatory nihilism (the point where the only answer left is "it just is"), and that you slowly converge towards metaphysics instead of physics there, and that it might never be testable. Maybe a theory of consciousness will always be poetry, in the sense in which Bohr said that when it comes to atoms, language can be used only as in poetry (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2109917244226437445), and never actually within the realm of the scientific method (which is no insult to the people who describe experience carefully btw, that's rigorous work). So maybe we're just left with models of consciousness, in order to make useful predictions, which is basically what all of science does, and what the big break in physics was about back then when Bohr and Einstein had their discussion about quantum physics: is the theory complete and does it describe what's really there, or is a wave function "just" a model for us to predict with? So, if we have a really good model of consciousness, we might just say that this is useful enough (esp. in engineering, e.g. for hybrid mind uploading within biomimetic neuromorphics) to say that "we indeed understand consciousness now". And I think that this is legit and not a cheap way out. For a very "Wittgensteinian reason": the meaning of a word like "understand" isn't fixed in advance. What "fixes" it is how we're using it. We say that a pupil understands a rule when he knows how to go on (that's from his Philosophical Investigations, §§143–155). Understanding in the engineer's use of the word in that sense is a model that allows us to measure, predict, but also steer experience across a replacement step. There is another use that may well remain open => the one that asks why there is experience at all! I put this goal into the Ontology to keep both uses in view (in my first draft I ended with "we're just humans, writing down our higher-dimensional thoughts as lower-dimensional language", but that's the picture of the early Wittgenstein, which he later gave up himself).


5/30 Transcending Biology, but not Physics

The third and last goal of my Ontology is "transcend humanity". It comes after "solve death" (day three: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2121890643983151389) and "understand consciousness" (day four/yesterday: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2122235784644477415), and in the Ontology each of the three has a type => there's a "practical goal", a "theoretical one" and a "transformative one" => so today's goal is the one where the thing that changes is us (= the transformative one). It's also the one whose name I'm least happy with. I have three reasons for that, and two of them are why the goal has a new name as of today (until yesterday it was still called "human transcendence", which is also how it appears in yesterday's meditation).

The first reason is that the word is already taken. Taken literally it's harmless, as the Latin transcendere just means to climb beyond something. But theology, Kant and phenomenology have each claimed it (and prob. a bunch more I'm unaware of), and each of them gives a different answer to the only question that the word really asks, which is: beyond what? In theology, it's what lies beyond the world. With Kant, "transcendent" is what lies beyond any possible experience (please don't mix it up with his "transcendental", which is about the conditions of experience (until recently, I regularly mixed up those two terms!)). I came across the word mostly through Husserl tho. As far as I understand him, "transcendent" is said of whatever an experience is directed at without being a genuine ("reell" in German) part of that experience itself (Husserl's "Ideas I", §§41–44). His own example is a table => walking around it, I only ever see it from one side at a time (in adumbrations, Abschattung in German), so the table always is "more" than what's given to me right now, whereas the seeing itself is immanent and doesn't come in sides at all. Careful tho, as the table that I see is the very thing that appears, and no thing-in-itself that hides behind its appearances. Its transcendence lies in how it's given, which is never completely. So for Husserl, the answer to "beyond what?" is: beyond any experience that I have of the thing, and that's why there's always more of it to see.

Does my third goal want to get rid of that? No, and it couldn't. Husserl is quite clear that this has nothing to do with human eyes being weak, as any mind that perceives a thing gets it in adumbrations, and he says that this would hold even for God. So a mind with ten times my senses would still see the table from somewhere, and there would still be more of it to see. What can change is how much of that "more" a mind gets to, and with that I'm at my own answer. In the Ontology, it's us who go beyond something, namely the limits that human biology sets for us. And only those! The limits that physics sets stay where they are (it's the same line as on day three, where I stopped the definition of solving death at the universe itself => see the schematic that I created for today's post to visualise this). Death is one of the limits of biology, and it's the one that's important enough to have its own goal.

The second reason is the direction. "Human transcendence" can be read in two ways => either the human is the one who transcends, or the human is what gets transcended. The difference between "human transcendence" and "transcend humanity" lies exactly there, so in the direction of the change: the first one sounds like deepening and expanding what it means to be human from within, and the second one says that the human species gets left behind. I clearly mean the second one! I want to be exact about what gets left behind, as "humanity" is a word with several jobs too (the species, the people, and "being humane"). I mean the species, so the kind that we were born into, together with the limits of its biology (a species is a category of biology anyway, so the more of what carries you isn't biology anymore, the less that category says about you). I don't mean the person who goes beyond these limits, as that person is what I'm in this for (as I once put it in a reply: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2103087394266182045), I don't mean the people who'd rather stay (more on them below), and I certainly don't mean being humane. This is also where I go further than Julian Huxley, who made the word "transhumanism" known in the 1950s. His version is the from-within one: "man remaining man, but transcending himself" => and he meant it for the species as a whole. I start with the single someone (as on day one), and I drop the "remaining man" => it has to stay you, and it doesn't have to stay human.

So yes, this is basically about transhumanism, and the "trans" is the same one (beyond). Transhumanism is the position that it's possible and desirable to use technology in order to go beyond these limits, up to a point where the result may be called posthuman. I mean that in the transhumanist sense btw, so a being with capacities far beyond ours, and I don't mean the "posthumanism" of the humanities, which is a critique of humanism. Why don't I just call the goal "transhumanism" then? Because an -ism is something that you hold, and a goal is something that you reach, or don't. Which brings me to the third reason, and it's the simplest one => all goals in the Ontology are a "verb" + a "thing". Solving death and understanding consciousness are things that you do, whereas "human transcendence" was a noun, and it sounded like a state that one is in. That mismatch was more than cosmetics (don't forget, I do have diagnosed OCD!), as it's exactly what made me unsure for a while if this is a goal at all, or one of the outcomes of the Ontology. "Transcending humanity" is something that you do, and it even carries the answer to "beyond what?" in its name. I'm going to work through the limits themselves here, and then say more generally why this is a goal, and why it's the third one.

My first thought was that when death is solved, what it means to be human will have changed so drastically that this alone is the transcendence (which is true btw!). I then had to point out to myself that while this takes death out of the category of things that one simply has to accept, it still leaves the third goal in the category of the first one, just in other words. And then I realised that I didn't actually know what else there was. I had a goal sitting in my own Ontology that I couldn't spell out. I found that rather bizarre. So let me start with where the limits come from ... the ones that I mean all come from the thing that carries us, and ours came with the biology that we were born into, and when you then want to know who picked exactly these, you have to go and ask evolution, or you just stand there with a big "why these?!". The usual answer is that a limit was simply the cheaper way to stay alive, and it never occurred to anybody to change it afterwards. Evolution has been at this for a few billion years. I'm not sure when it last asked the one who's alive right now what they'd like to experience, but I'd guess never (that's the generational route from day one again). So I'm wondering how many of these limits we even notice. Death is the one that everybody knows. With the others it took us ages to even notice them, if we did at all. And along the same lines, I suspect that there are limits which are really hard to notice from the inside, as we never had it any other way! Humans see through a sort of slit. If you've seen a chart of the electromagnetic spectrum, you may have noticed the thin coloured stripe in it, which looks sort of like somebody forgot to colour in the rest. Vision is basically that stripe, so roughly one octave out of everything that's there. Of course, there's only so much that a body can do about its own limits. Often even the senses that we do have need help before they're of any use at night or over a distance. Some species evolved to specialise in exactly that; for instance, bats and dolphins find their way around by listening to their own echoes. Humans, with rather unspecialised senses and a skull that only fits so much, learned other ways to get around their limits. One such way is the telescope. If you point the right one at the sky, it'll show you infrared, translated into the colours that you can see anyway. But what you get is still a picture in the old colours, as everybody who has looked at a false-colour image knows. A notebook also helps, as it keeps what memory doesn't (remembering something is never like being there again, and I'll come back to this one in a second (trust me on that one, this one will be special!)). And a third way around a limit is the spacesuit, which takes along the narrow range of temperature and pressure that a body survives. All three go around a limit and leave it where it was, namely in the thing that carries the experience (the limit of being one mind, closed off from every other one, I'm leaving out today, as it needs its own day).

Now, a carrier that gets built (and is no longer born) can be built differently. That's the whole point => a built substrate is a steerable one, which is why I wrote in September that the ambition extends well beyond life extension (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2099930001566998847), and why I once called "steerable phenomenology" the thing that the transhumanist has long dreamt of without once having thought it (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2100608380771381677). Where there's a gradual replacement, there's that choice again for every single part. Each time a piece of tissue gets swapped for something engineered (that's the mechanistic gradual replacement from day three, so the core of hybrid mind uploading (which will also receive it's on meditation day in the coming weeks)), there's a bit of the carrier that doesn't have to repeat every limit of the tissue that it replaced. Where there's freedom like this, the substrate becomes steerable, and with it, bit by bit, the phenomenology (and I mean phenomenology, not just phenomenal space or qualia, but actually phenomenology). Some of what the tissue does – especially the storing – comes with losses that we're so used to that we hardly count them as limits. Memory compresses what it stores, as metabolism and physical space in the brain are limited, and it rebuilds what it recalls, with the gaps filled in from priors and context. Presumably everybody has noticed at some point that if they go back to a day that mattered to them, what comes back is both fainter and less reliable than the day itself was. (That's why remembering never feels like actually and truly being there)

It's only this January that I properly worked through what a built substrate could do about this, in a working document called "Synthetic Substrates and the Destruction of the Forward Arrow of Personal Time", which leans on Lyre's neurophenomenal structuralism (I never published this document because I was always afraid it would sound too crackpot-like before I had at least some directionally empirical insights into it). The thesis in there is that this limit is a contingent one of biological storage and no principled one => if a substrate took an experience and didn't compress it, but instead stored it whole, this would yield a memory that's both as rich as the experience was (because nothing got lost at the storing) and the same every time (again, because nothing has to be rebuilt at the recall). This is what I called "lossless Q-storage" there in the doc, with the "Q" standing for what an experience is like. Of course, while such a substrate would make remembering as rich as experiencing, allowing for a personal time that one can traverse instead of getting pushed through it, there is no guarantee that it can be built at all (that's an open engineering question, the typical case of: it works in principle, but not in practice). Since it only changes what gets played back, there's also just no time travel in it, compared to what the title may suggest: the substrate itself still runs forward, like a video player that "ages" normally while whatever it plays can be rewound. It takes physics as it is, limits included. (How one would still tell reliving from living with a memory like that is another open question in there. In the Ontology, all of this sits under augmented cognition btw => another day!)

So I think of the third goal as a related but distinct idea, that solving death lifts one limit of biology (the one that everybody knows), and that it will take a much longer process, which may or may not work out in the end, to get beyond the other ones. Often when I say "the other ones", I simply mean where a mind can exist, or what it can do and keep, or how it's connected to other minds (i.e. hivemind-ism). The Ontology has names for where these lead, which are its three outcomes: substrate independence, augmented cognition and collective mind (each of them gets its own day). There is a kind of change that I think will come first, and the Ontology already has a word for it. My first impulse is to call all of it augmentation, after the plain sense of the word, so more of what a mind already does (seeing more, keeping more, holding more, etc.), which can be checked against what that mind did before. But in the interest of not underselling it, I'm inclined to add that the memory example already goes further than that, as keeping a bit more is augmentation, and a personal time that one can traverse would be an experience that no human has ever had, so a change of kind. (If that far end can be reached, I don't know)

My main way of keeping the three goals apart rests on the process world-line, so if solving death keeps it going, and understanding consciousness lets us measure (and later steer) what runs along it, then transcending humanity is what lets that same world-line outgrow the limits that it was born with. And "that same" is the part where hybrid mind uploading goes a different way than the transhumanism that I came from, so classical mind uploading. There, people figured that one could isolate the pattern from the rest of the brain, which would allow for minds that get scanned, copied and upgraded like software. If the pattern is all that you are, you can run it wherever you like, allowing for transcendence in a single step, skipping the "slow" hybrid state in between. The price of this shortcut comes in two parts. First, when using a copy in order to transcend, we lose the one who was supposed to do the transcending, as death is the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line (day three), and that's what happens to the original's process, however good the copy is. Second, we become blind to that very loss, to the point where everybody, the copy included, would describe what happened as a transcendence, rather than as a death with a very capable newcomer right after it (a new someone with a head start, the same way a baby is a new world-line). So between similarity and continuity, I pick continuity! My Ontology says the same about world-line fidelity btw: it never breaks, and nothing gets traded against it (enhancement included). You may change a great deal on the way, but there can't be a moment at which one stops and another one takes over. And to be careful about what I'm claiming here: at every single step it has to be still you (the "continuer"), which is a different claim from "you'll stay the way you are" (you won't, and you didn't so far either, as the five-year-old you wouldn't recognise much), and it's also no promise about where you end up. That the world-line really goes on through such changes is a hypothesis, as I said yesterday, and it still gets its own day.

I'm having some trouble saying what exactly makes this a goal and no outcome, but here's a first attempt. Transcending humanity will tend to look like an outcome, as an engineered carrier comes with other limits than a biological one, whether anybody plans for that or not. This naturally makes it harder to say what's left to strive for, and it takes some honesty on my side, as I may have filed an outcome under the goals (yesterday's definitions were: a goal is something that we strive for and could fail at, and an outcome is what follows either way, as long as we work on the mission). But it's not merely an outcome. You could have the state, the capability and the topology that the three outcomes of the Ontology describe, and still be nowhere close to anybody having transcended anything, as the minds that have them could all be replacements, or nobody might have gone at all (which would be a decision too btw, as with a built carrier, keeping a limit is as much a choice as moving it). So what follows either way is only that it becomes possible, and the goal is that we get there, as the ones that we are.

Transcending humanity is also the last of the three for a reason (it's no ranking by importance, btw). First, it needs the engineered carrier that solving death brings – that's where the limits become steerable, and it's what the reply from above means with "solve death, augmentation comes next". Second, it needs the understanding of consciousness, and more of it than solving death does. By changing the experience on purpose instead of keeping it the same, an augmentation step takes away the marker that such a replacement step still has (the same experience before and during as well as after) and what has to be shown is that it's the same process, now having a different experience. Without that, nobody could tell transcendence from replacement, not even the one who comes out of it. That's a harder check than yesterday's, and I don't have it yet.

And what does all of this mean for humanity, and now I mean the people? Of course, I don't really know where to stop with that question, and I know even less where to start. Today's meditation could easily be double the length (respect to everyone who is still reading this, I've sometimes got the feeling that I'm the first and last person to read this sentence). Ideally, whoever builds this would attend to the people who want none of it, and then try to leave their lives exactly as good (or bad?) as they are. The first goal says "no one has to end" and it never said that nobody is allowed to(!). In the same way, this one says that no one has to stay within the limits of biology, and it doesn't say that anybody has to leave them. Nothing matters except to someone conscious, and ordinary biological consciousness already meets that condition (day one: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2121143118062031238), so a life inside these limits is no lesser one, and transcending them is no condition for anything. (And if some get access first, that's potentially a problem. And if some get pushed into it, that's an even bigger one. But I have to park both of them for today. I only wanted to say what the goal says. That's all. How we live next to people who chose differently (which takes patience on both sides, and we're currently quite bad at it) has to come later)

Another difficulty that this goal raises is that a humanity which "transcends" via copies has simply ended, with something very convincing left behind, although this one can be avoided by not taking the shortcut from above. That's the risk that I take most seriously btw, and it's why I keep saying that classical mind uploading could be a Great Filter (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2016828465194967321). Why shouldn't there be people who'd rather stay inside the limits of biology, or who only take a part of what's possible? It's very possible that the from-within version, the one that Huxley wanted for all of us, is what many will pick, e.g. because they simply like being human, but then do we tell the ones who want to leave that they have to stay as well? I don't see why both couldn't be around at the same time. In particular, "enabling a new state of humanity", which I listed among the original goals of mind uploading on day two, still seems right to me, with the stress on the "of" => a new state OF humanity is only one if it's still the same people who are in that state, whatever happens to the species.

Will it stay the third goal? The goal, yes. The name I've just changed, and I'm not sure that it was for the last time, as it's still the weakest of the three, and "transcending" keeps borrowing the glow of its religious sense. The oldest word for all of this that I know of is Dante's "trasumanar" (first canto of the Paradiso), and there it's what happens to him as he rises into heaven. No matter how carefully I define "transcending"; there's always a chance that somebody somewhere hears the old use in it (that's the "borrowed-word problem" from day two of this meditation series; where a word's old use quietly decides what has to be true in the new place!). If I borrow a word that's at home in heaven, then I imply that I mean what it meant there: that perfection is waiting on the other side, that leaving the world behind is what this is about, and that the limits of physics go together with the ones of biology. Or that if only a better mind looked at the table, it would finally get seen from all sides. I mean none of that, and if the name changes again, what's behind the three names should stay as it is. Tomorrow: Substrate independence!


6/30 Computational Vs. Physical Substrate Independence

Over the past three days, we discussed the goals of my Ontology. Today we start with the first of the three outcomes, which also sit right below the mission called hybrid mind uploading. It's called "substrate independence". What does this term mean in this context? In the Ontology, it's listed as a state, and the one line under it says: mind no longer tied to biological substrate. There's a widespread reading of these two words in which the substrate simply doesn't matter. That's no good frame of mind for somebody who wants to build one, and it's not what I mean. The trouble sits in "independence", as the word asks a question that it doesn't answer (the same way "transcending" did yesterday with its "beyond what?") => independent of what? And dependent on what instead?

On day four I said that a goal is something that we strive for and could fail at, and that an outcome is what follows either way, as long as we work on the mission (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2122235784644477415). We don't strive for substrate independence for its own sake. Each time a piece of brain gets swapped for an engineered one while the process keeps running, there's a bit less of the tissue that it started in. So it comes along with the work, and that's what the "either way" was about. It was never meant as a guarantee tho, as how far this can go is decided by physics and not by us. The three outcomes btw pair up with the three goals, one each. Solving death the way that this mission goes about it, so by (mechanistic) gradual replacement, brings a carrier that's no longer the biology that we were born with, which is substrate independence. Understanding consciousness brings augmented cognition, because what we can measure and model, we can at some point also steer and modify. And transcending humanity has the collective mind as its outcome, a term that I'm (as well) not happy with, and by far the weirdest of the three (but that's for its own day!).

So why substrate independence? I'll go through it in the order in which I went through it myself. The idea is older than the term. In 1950, Turing remarked that Babbage's Analytical Engine was meant to be purely mechanical, and that the fact that both our computers and our nerves work electrically therefore "cannot be of theoretical importance". Ten years later, Hilary Putnam figured out how to carry it over into the philosophy of mind (which is btw where functionalism starts). In "Minds and Machines" (1960) he compares mental states with the logical states of a Turing machine, and in "Psychological Predicates" (1967) he argues that pain can't be one particular state of the brain, as an octopus presumably feels pain with a brain that's built very differently, so pain has to be a functional state that many kinds of matter can realise. That's multiple realisability. And in 2003, Nick Bostrom wrote "substrate-independence" into his simulation argument. I'm not sure who used the term first, but he doesn't present it as his own, as he introduces it as "a common assumption in the philosophy of mind". Btw: What I find interesting is how much of what's relevant for my kind of work runs through Putnam => functionalism, multiple realisability, with them substrate independence, and even the brain in a vat (i.e. our functional brain isolation work at @eightsixscience). And I used the term in exactly this sense for years myself, e.g. in 2023, when I called one of my early aims "substrate-independent cognitive preservation" (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1727391721431117984).

That went on until this May, when I wrote down the founding commitments of classical mind uploading and "substrate independence as a working assumption" was one of them. That was the moment to finally put my name under it... and I couldn't go through with it. Whether because of Lerchner's paper on the Abstraction Fallacy (https://deepmind.google/research/publications/231971), the weeks of arguing about it afterwards (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2045752057697653096), or a sudden second-guessing of how calculating a process is supposed to be(!) that process, I filed it under the commitments that fix the answers in advance, and ended years' worth of using it (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2059492211277140471). Putnam, of all people, had been there looong before me. In 1988, he showed how to get any finite automaton out of pretty much any ordinary open system (it's in the appendix of "Representation and Reality"), which allows for a rock that runs whatever program you like. So if I take a lens from Putnam, then it's the one that he ended with.

Now, here I am again, with the same two words as the first outcome of my own Ontology. "They" always say that a mind goes wherever its computation goes, and the other "they" say that it only ever lives in living tissue (which is the only place where we've found one so far) => so I guess it's on me to say that nobody has shown either! Yeah, I know that this looks like I'm taking the assumption back in, but I'm only keeping its two words. And, tbh, as something that one arrives at (or doesn't), I like them a lot better than as something that one starts from. My take, however, is that I don't look at substrate independence through a functionalist lens, in the sense of having already subscribed to a theory of consciousness such as neurocomputational functionalism. What I start from is an observation => if we all have somewhat the same kind of experience across humans, then there must be some sort of multiple realisability, in the sense that we all do somewhat the same even tho our brains are all different. Sometimes there's a slow-growing tumour sitting right in somebody's speech area (i.e. a low-grade glioma), and it takes the brain a few years to "move" the speech area somewhere else, so that a surgeon can later cut where no textbook would let him (I'm simplifying the gradual reorganisation principle and serial lesion effect here btw, as this is also a topic for another day's meditation). Either way, I still find that rather wild. Of course, there's only so much that this can show, as it's living neural tissue taking over from living neural tissue. So the process doesn't seem to care which cells carry it. However, the substrate class is kind of the same in all of these cases, and that's the important question => does the process also not care what kind of thing carries it? I also have to correct myself. In June I wrote that because of the gliomas, bio-to-bio multiple realisability "must exist" in the biological brain, and I put a "hence substrate independence" in brackets behind it (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2068742850389422476). I'd still sign the part before the bracket. The bracket itself, not so...

Gualtiero Piccinini (a philosopher of computation) has this "mousetrap example" that I keep coming back to; he points out that while you can build a mousetrap in a variety of ways (in his terms this is what he calls multiple realisability), it still has to catch actual mice, while a computation doesn't "care" at all what its vehicles are made of – as long as they have the right degrees of freedom, which is what he calls "medium independence". Classical mind uploading turned out to need the second one for minds, and I found myself needing at most the first. The first one sits in every heart-lung machine btw, where the "lung" is a bundle of plastic fibres with blood on one side and gas on the other. There's no lung cell anywhere, and the blood comes out oxygenated anyway! Supposedly, nobody has managed that with a simulation of a lung yet (btw this work with artificial lungs vs what can be simulated as an input to a brain is again full-circle back to our work on functional brain isolation (= brains in vats)). What I'm wondering is if one can have the physics of the brain without the biology, or as I put it in September: a mechanistic piece of brain that does the same kind of physics as the real biological brain, and at the same time is computable in the sense that we can steer it (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2100128492234907858).

So in that modest sense, substrate independence already exists, as nature does it from tissue to tissue all the time. The question is if the thing can work without biology, and that's really two questions. Does physics allow it, so can any material other than living cells do the job? It's a question for experiments, and it has an awkward asymmetry => a single working case answers it with yes, whereas no number of failed attempts adds up to a no, as for that we'd have to understand why. And if physics allows it, what does it cost? The way that I think about this one, in terms of materials science: if the engineering effort is a scale with "running a SaaS company" on the left and "CERN and the LHC" on the right, where on that scale is showing that substrate independence is economically feasible, given the engineering complexity and efforts needed? I don't know. It can also come out as a yes from physics and a no from the economics, and then hybrid mind uploading becomes extreme biology instead (I've said so before: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2116050057216618539).

There's a kind of substrate independence in all of this that I think is the only one which can be an outcome, and I didn't have a word for it. So I'm putting a word in front that says what has to be kept. Computational substrate independence is what Bostrom and classical mind uploading mean => a mind depends only on the computation, so whatever runs it will do. Physical substrate independence is what I mean => a mind depends on what its carrier physically does, so whatever does the same will do, be it biological or not. And the other half has to be said out loud, as, in my view, a mind depends on its substrate a great deal => what it's supposed to become independent of is biology, and physics it never leaves. The physical version claims less than the computational one btw, as a material that does the same physics also runs the same computation along the way, but not the other way round.

There's a spectrum in this, and it's about how many materials can carry a mind. First, there's the tissue that you were born with, and nothing else will do. Then other living neural tissue will do as well, which is as far as the gliomas go. Then engineered living tissue, then at least one material that isn't alive (the candidate that I've been writing about is the alien neuron: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2104351025863840183), then many. And it could turn out that the class ends at living tissue. Then we'd have gone full circle => we set out to leave biology, and we arrive at building it (and then go towards maybe extreme biology by merging with genetically engineered fungi or something?).

That's basically the outcome => if we engineer this, then we'll have substrate independence, in the physical sense. A copy on another substrate shows nothing, as a copy would tell us that everything is fine in any case (day two). But if one takes a piece of a living brain and doesn't copy it, and instead lets an engineered piece take over its job while the rest keeps running, then this can show both that the piece does the job (because the experience is the same before, during and after the step, day four) and that it's still the same someone (again, because the process never stopped, if the hypothesis from day four holds). Careful tho: this is NOT Moravec's procedure, and it's not the setup of Chalmers's fading qualia either. Read "Mind Children" (1988) and pay close attention => the robot surgeon there, ostensibly doing the very same thing piece by piece, actually swaps each layer of the brain for a simulation of it, and Chalmers's silicon chips are defined as doing what a neuron does at its inputs and outputs. The actual difference, in both of these cases, is that what the brain does has already been coarse-grained (down to e.g. action potentials) before the first piece gets replaced, and for me that grain is the open part (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2074474900773888375).

Which leaves the question of what exactly has to be re-created. Earlier this year I started reading Romain Brette's "The Brain, in Theory" (on Lerchner's recommendation: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2096640123198652573), and its main point is one that an engineer doesn't like to hear: living organisms aren't engineered. So there's no plan of the brain that I could copy into another material, as nobody ever drew one. The brain is ultimately a physical thing, dynamical, organised and bounded, that goes forward in time (it's the carrier, and the process world-line is the activity that runs through it). So, however that activity is constrained by physics, originally via neuronal cells, the bet is that it's somehow engineerable with the same constraints. Neurons keep charges apart with membranes and let ions through them at some voltages and not at others, and all the while their chemistry rebuilds the very structure that the activity runs on. These are constraints, and I think that the brain is all about constraints. Not all of them sit inside the skull, btw, as a brain is always in a loop with something that it senses and acts on. Of course, it's hard to know where this list stops, and it's just as hard to know at which grain it starts (that's the lattice question that I've owed since day one: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2121143118062031238). So what has to be re-created in another material are these constraints, and the brain's parts only as far as they're needed for them. That's the quest, and THIS re-creation of the constraints is what I actually mean by substrate independence!


7/30 Augmented Cognition Needs a Baseline First

The second outcome (type "Outcome") of my Ontology is called "augmented cognition" (yesterday's was "substrate independence"). This term is not very clean, as depending on who you ask, it gives you a different response. If you ask DARPA, it's a programme from the early 2000s about computers that adapt to the measured cognitive state of their user, and if you ask Engelbart (1962), it's about better tools around a person. I'm referring to neither. How I'm using it, as an outcome of hybrid mind uploading towards the vision of synthetic consciousness, is to expand the normal baseline of human cognition as we're used to it today (and of conscious experience (which the name doesn't say)). At the core, it's the bet that if a mind sits "on a carrier" that we built (physical substrate independence => in yesterday's words), then we'd be able to steer the phenomenal space itself and not only the tools around it, or as I put it in September: a substrate we can build is a substrate we can steer (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2100608380771381677). So whatever takes what somebody can experience or do outside the range that they had before, I'll call augmentation. The full catalogue I'll leave to the transhumanists. You know the usual examples: more experienced time per physical second, feelings that nobody has had yet, or colours that don't exist in the spectrum. (I'm unsure how far the first one goes btw, as a mind on such a "mechanistically created" carrier is still bound to the same physics, and to a world that keeps running at its own pace). And about those colours ... well, we all see one of them already: it's magenta! There's no single wavelength for it anywhere in the physical light spectrum, and our brain just makes it up in our phenomenal space anyway (out of red and blue light with the green missing; this is also the reason why my personal brand's main colour is magenta btw!). So if ordinary biology can already do that, then on a steerable carrier we can probably engineer – and therefore experience – a LOT more colours that don't exist in the spectrum. In 2009, adult monkeys that had been colour-blind since birth got a third cone pigment by gene therapy, and started to tell colours apart that they couldn't before (https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08401). Similarly, last year a handful of people were shown a colour outside the natural gamut, by a laser that stimulated (almost) only their M cones (they called it "olo": https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adu1052). And in the same vein, I suspect that some of what gets called augmentation is possible in biology already, so what a steerable substrate would add is range, and hopefully a way back.

I'll say up front that I think this is one of the most exciting things that the whole endeavour of the synthetic consciousness vision makes possible. However, what I'm more careful about is to not start from an augmented state as step one. It's tempting to skip the boring part, and for parts of the transhumanism that I come from the augmented state is the whole point, but it's not what most people that I talk to are after. In the Ontology's order it looks like this btw => understanding consciousness is what makes experience steerable and checkable, transcending humanity is what would use that, and so this outcome comes late. That augmentation comes after preservation I've said before (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2103087394266182045). What I haven't really said so far is WHY. So I sat down to write it out for today's meditation, and going by my notes, I end up with three reasons:

– Number one is the one that I actually care about the most (the other two are more like side arguments, with all due respect to them), and it's just a simple question: how would you even notice? Say my first state on the new carrier is already an augmented one. One day I wake up in there and look at a wide sky with a few colours in it that I've never seen. I find some things sharper than before, and some oddly flat. And you ask me, why do you think that is? I'd guess the new colours, or the new pace of time. I'd come up with plenty of clever explanations, and I couldn't check a single one of them. And the answer might just be that before I woke up, something of mine went missing on the way (from in there, a loss looks exactly like a difference!). Suppose that instead I come back as the same me into the same place. Sitting in my own kitchen with the same coffee as always, I'd expect it to taste the way it always did. I might not catch the first thing that's off, but after a few of them (a taste, a word that's gone, a face, the way to the bakery, my own handwriting … I don't know yet how many it takes) I could tell pretty well what went missing, and later what got added. A careful reader of yesterday's meditation (I hope that there are a few) would look at this and say, "That's just your outcome read backwards => the point there was to re-create the SAME constraints, and augmenting means changing them, so first the same ones, then changes to them, and never both at once." And that's my comparison and verification problem with classical mind uploading and whole brain emulation (ha, I know, "but the emulation says that it's fine". Sure? It would say that either way!). Run it that way: you get scanned, you get emulated/simulated, and switched on. Result: the substrate changed, the body changed, the surroundings changed and prob the capacities too, all in that one step. Checklist in hand, somebody watches whoever came out, and asks how it feels in there. They conclude that everything is approximately fine. At this point I'm somewhat at a loss for where to even start comparing, as it's immediately such an alien state to transition to (in silicon). Consider the one who came out and says, "Eh, feels like me, I guess?" I ask: compared to what? Those words come easily enough, but there's no before of their own to say them from, as the original has either stopped or is somebody else by now (and yes, put the emulation into a robot in the very same environment, and it still has no "before"). I don't want to oversell the baseline tho, as it only shows what changed. One could come back to the same kitchen, find the same coffee, and think that this proves that it's still the same someone. Not so. That hangs on the process having gone on (the hypothesis from day four), and on nobody's report, mine included. "Status-quo bias!" you cry. "You just like things the way they are!" Maybe I do, but the baseline doesn't have to be better for any of this; it only has to come first.

– The second reason is about choosing, and for this one I had help, as the philosopher L. A. Paul has a name for such things => transformative experiences (her own examples are becoming a vampire and having a child, afaik). To me, deciding for a new sense from the outside sounds like someone ordering in a restaurant where the menu is in a language that they don't speak, and where the dish then also changes what they like to eat. Or if you had the ability to switch to a different pace of time, do you imagine that you could say beforehand if you'd ever want to switch back? I couldn't. So if there's a sane place to decide such things from, then it's a stable one, and one step at a time. I do believe that even the most old-school person, who'd want to stay as natural as possible in their extended lifespan, would at some point want to try what a mechanistic brain substrate can do (hence augmenting their cognitive functions and consciousness).

– The third reason is the weakest as an argument, and the one that I hear the most. I've talked to a bunch of people about this by now, also people far outside the field, and most of them have a hard time with "being embodied in a computer simulation". What I hear most is that they'd feel trapped, which philosophers know from Nozick's experience machine, and which I've wondered about from the other side => at which point does a brain stop accepting a simulation as its lived reality (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1982837490026516782)? They don't want a hundred new colours, or to merge with other people (the third outcome of the Ontology btw, which we will discuss tomorrow!). They simply want to stick around in their familiar environment. It's a lot easier to tell somebody that we take the brain out of the failing body while they stay alive (the stop-dying part), replace it gradually and mechanistically (the solving-death part), and that they then come back to their own place and their friends.

Take somebody like my mother. If she ever had this option, I don't think that she'd want a digital utopia, with the vastness of simulated worlds and new augmented experiences waiting for her. She would (at least initially) want to come back, be in her lovely garden, play with her dogs and have a normal life, just a bit longer and way healthier than before. By baseline I mean hers btw, so how she experienced things and what she could do before, and no norm of what a human should be.

PS: One correction to my own picture tho, as she wouldn't come back in the body that she left. I've said before that I wouldn't put a brain back into something that can get hit by a bus on the way out of the hospital, and that it should stay in a secure, fault-tolerant facility (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2082061666993238220), so the garden would be reached through a body that's steered from a distance, by a brain that's still hers (the same 4D process world line). That body is new, with other hands and other senses, and it has to become hers first. So even the most conservative version of this has changes in it, which is exactly why I want them one at a time, and augmentation last. It's not a main goal then (survival is: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2074462981086208413, at least initially as goal one in the Ontology), and it needs a lot of work on what it means to still be human and on what exactly can be engineered, with so many question marks that I see it as a Pandora's box of issues, and of opportunities. But whatever it is, we don't want to deal with it at the start. At the start, we should have the same baseline to compare to, with the option of more, and that's probably the essence of today's meditation.


8/30 Who Says Individuality Will Stay Forever?

The third and last outcome of my Ontology, when working on the hybrid mind uploading mission towards a future state of synthetic consciousness as the vision, is called "collective mind". This is probably THE(!) weirdest item in the entire Ontology, and that's actually a wild thing to say on its own if you consider that we're talking about super weird stuff already, such as hybrid mind uploading, substrate independence, solving death and transcending humanity, etc. The reason being that, based on my own experience of talking to longevity people over the past few years, even the most extreme transhumanists usually have one view in common about the future post-death and post-augmentation: they stay single individuals, "lasting as long as they want/forever". But who says that we stay individuals? Who says that we don't merge into one collective, and also phenomenally conscious, mind? (I mean that last bit literally btw, as a society or an internet can be smart as a whole without there being anything it is like to be it, and here there would be). Most transhumanists that I've talked to (myself included, initially) usually don't consider this a possibility at all, they're all pro-individualism. Last year I suggested using exactly that as a little stress test for people in brain augmentation, so to ask them if they're pro-hivemind or pro-individualism (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1968039827796037769), only to admit three days later that I was losing arguments for individualism (https://x.com/danburonline/status/1969098720722764102). Nowadays I'm pretty unsure about it. Nobody is truly seeing a strong convergence towards collective minds, I'm pretty sure that it might happen, and I might be completely wrong.

It was around late December, after Christmas till early January of this year, when I had, funnily enough, three different contexts "meeting" around me towards discussing hiveminds and collective minds for some reason (on New Year's Eve I even posted a thought experiment about a corpus callosum that gets stretched to a kilometre, until the two hemispheres stop being one whole: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2006374733332283614). It was also during that time that I was writing that essay on the breakdown of the forward arrow of phenomenal time due to engineered, steerable substrates for brains, where I realised (in hindsight it feels obvious) that it's quite possible that as soon as we've achieved synthetic consciousness via hybrid mind uploading, we'll technically have the possibility to merge into one giant collective mind, with one giant process world-line instead of "society". So the things discussed in the first few meditations about the generational, global approach to death and the individual, first-principles one are basically converging towards being the same thing, as we all just merge into one mind. (On day one I kept global death (no conscious observer left anywhere) apart from individual death (THIS consciousness ends), and said that Musk's route of making us fault tolerant as a species answers a different question than mine. With one world-line for everybody, "consciousness goes on" and "this consciousness goes on" would be the same sentence. It would also mean that the redundancy of eight billion separate lines is gone btw, so that one line had better be fault tolerant in itself).

So what's in that essay (a working document from January, that I never published and only circulated around close friends)? I get to hiveminds via an area that has nothing to do with them at first sight, which is memory. If you take Holger Lyre's neurophenomenal structuralism seriously (https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2022/1/niac012/6673758), then what an experience is like is fixed by its position in a structure of relations to all other experiences (he calls it Q-structure), and HOW that position gets activated, by the eyes or by a memory, shouldn't matter. But it obviously does, as a "remembered sunset" is a pale and somewhat wobbly thing next to the one that I'm standing in front of. The answer in my essay from January was that this comes from how a biological brain stores things (things being engrams => physical traces/manifestations of information/memory, etc.) => it keeps a compressed, lossy version of it and rebuilds the rest from priors when I remember (I called that "ecphoric reconstruction"), so remembering lands somewhere near the original position and never exactly on the original's phenomenal position. Take that bottleneck away on a substrate that we built without this compression constraint, and a memory would be the full experience again (=> if that can be built at all; we don't know. I called it "lossless Q-storage"). A past that I can re-enter at full (phenomenal) resolution isn't "gone" in the way that we're usually/naturally used to. Personal time turns from something that you get "pushed through" (forward in time) into something that I can "walk around in", while the substrate itself keeps "moving forward" like everything else in ordinary physical reality (think of a DVD player => you can rewind the film as often as you like, the player doesn't get a day younger. I'm btw not implying some form of duality here, both things are still within physics and not some meta-reality, but obviously the phenomenal reality can now, without compression, be bidirectional). I called these two layers Q-time and substrate time (P-time from here on, for physical) – and the "destruction of the forward arrow" in the title of the essay from January only ever happens in the first one: Q-time (again, not implying any property dualism here). And if one can go back into a past experiential state, then one can also e.g. decide differently from there on and see "where it goes" => so the history of what one has experienced stops being a simple line and rather turns into a graph with branches (a directed acyclic graph, an idea/picture that I borrowed from Toby Ord).

Btw, quite importantly, there are two kinds of "hivemind" hiding in this conversation so far: The first is the Q-time kind => it's intrapersonal, meaning just one substrate and just one ongoing process => and many of "me" in the sense of all the branches that I've walked through and can walk into again (that's the "branching identity" and the "directed acyclic graph of selves"). Nobody else is involved and in P-time there's still exactly one of me. The second is the P-time kind, which is interpersonal: several people in one phenomenal space at the same physical time (the "collective convergence"). My January essay only had the first kind written out. The second was in there as two half-sentences that I actually left as open questions => that branches might be merged again (fusion, which raises its own complexities, etc.), and that one person's graph might be spread over several substrates or substrate classes. Now read those two together => as soon as we spread over substrates and start to share phenomenal objects between each other, we're just a few iterations away from merging into one thing, and from the inside that thing wouldn't even be a line any more, as it'd have everybody's branches in it. One world-line in P-time (its substrate still gets "older" like everything else), and in Q-time one non-linear thing that doesn't point forward! This might be one of the wildest outcomes of all of this. I need the technical side of it anyway btw, as one of the operational requirements in the Ontology is called "distributed unification" (many nodes, one mind) => a brain that gets replaced piece by piece is for a long time a mix of old tissue and new parts, and that mix has to be ONE mind the whole time and not two. If that can be done between the parts of one brain, then I don't see what in the method would care about whose skull the parts came from. That's why this is an outcome in the Ontology and no goal (a goal we strive for and could fail at, an outcome follows either way, day four). What follows either way is only that it becomes possible (same as on day five) – and that we'd actually end up there is my gut feeling.

There are also other indicators, if you think about it: The internet and then the world wide web are things that interconnect us already, globalisation too, and some people in the BCI space already talk about inter-personal BCIs (in 2019 three people played a kind of Tetris together, two of them sending a yes or a no each out of their EEG and the third one getting it through a magnetic coil on the head, at about 81% accuracy: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-41895-7). Now, when ultimately the substrate becomes the BCI and we have steerable phenomenology, then ultimately we can also just merge. It seems like initially people will most likely do it occasionally, and for a pretty boring reason, which is to SHOW something instead of telling it. Take something as dull as how I multiply in my head. Whatever happens in my phenomenal space when I do 17 × 24, so how it's computed and visualised in there, is most likely completely different from what happens in yours, although we both end up at 408. If I tried to imagine what it's like when YOU do it, I'd use my own way of doing it and assume that yours is roughly the same, and I'd prob be wrong without ever noticing. Feynman has a story about exactly this => he found out that he could read while counting seconds in his head, but not talk. John Tukey didn't believe him, as he himself could talk while counting but not read, and it turned out that Feynman was counting by talking to himself and Tukey by watching a tape with numbers go by. The same word "counting" for both, and they only found out because one of them didn't believe the other (and by trying it out, nobody could have TOLD them). I'd like to clarify that "computed and visualised" is itself just a thing in word form, and that even a much better description would fail to carry over what it's like! That's the "show, don't tell" issue that I connect with Wittgenstein. In the Tractatus he has the line that what can be shown can't be said, and later, in the Investigations, the box with a beetle in it that everybody has and nobody else can look into, where the word "beetle" works just fine whatever is in the box. (A Wittgenstein reader will point out that I'm mixing up what he says about logical form with what he says about private objects, and that's fair). My original essay from January has a more technical version of that box btw, via the principal bundles of Oizumi, Lim and Kanai (https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/agupq) => in my reading, the part of the structure that comes from the symmetries of a sense has to match between two people, while the part where the identities of things sit (they call them signatures) is plastic, with no guaranteed relationship between yours and mine (this does funnily enough also have an impact on the gradual reorganisation principle in [mechanistic] gradual brain replacement).

So what would showing be? If I could let you have what I have while I multiply, with nothing in between that turns it into signals and back, then there'd be nothing left to interpret and no need for an interface, as the "computation" of whatever is happening would be just one stream in one big shared phenomenal space. But then it's also no longer me showing something to you! Husserl saw this almost a hundred years ago (in the fifth Cartesian Meditation) => if what belongs to the other one were directly accessible to me, then it would simply be a part of me, and the two of us would be the same (YES, Husserl being the OG collective mind/hivemind person, who would have thought that!). So whatever sits between two heads and turns things into signals and back is telling again, only with more channels (the Tetris players were telling, one bit at a time), and the moment that something really gets shown, there's a bit less of "two heads" than before. That's why I said that the substrate has to become the interface (i.e. BCI). The closest thing to it that exists, as far as I know, is one pair of twins who are joined at the head with a bridge of tissue between their thalami, and of whom a philosopher has argued that they share some of their experiences and are still two people (https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-020-02753-6).

So it would start as something occasional (two people, one thing to show, and then apart again), and I'd expect more and more of it, as whoever has had something SHOWN once will find telling pretty poor afterwards. A whole class of disagreements would go away with it, namely the one that comes from not getting what the other one means, and there'd be an understanding that I can only call perfect, as there's nothing left to understand ABOUT each other (whether all disagreement goes away, I don't know, as I e.g. manage to disagree with myself just fine :-)). And at some point this most likely passes a threshold, after which coming apart again is the exception, and we've just merged into one giant collective mind. I'm on pretty thin ice here, as I don't know if that threshold is gradual or sharp. There's one theory that says sharp => Christof Koch has spelt out what integrated information theory predicts if two brains get bridged with more and more connections, which is that at some point both minds are abruptly gone and one new one is there instead (he calls it, as he's a fellow German-speaking person, an "Übermind"). I don't need IIT to be right for any of this btw, and my stretched corpus callosum idea from 31st of December 2025 was the same question from the other end.

Now, when I realised this earlier this year, I was quite uneasy about it. For the 10+ years that I've been on this journey to solve death and to work towards synthetic consciousness via (hybrid) mind uploading, I always thought that I'd be sticking around in the future, for as long as I want. But I never imagined that I would most likely "still die" at some point, due to merging into the collective. It's not really a dying; I'm still around, but I'm as much of an individual in the larger collective as a single neuron is in my 86-billion-neuron brain.

"Well, Daniel", one might say, "that doesn't sound like dying at all; you've just said that you're still around!" And yes, I did. By "still around" I mean that nothing of mine stops, as death is the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line (day three), and here nothing ends => two processes keep going and become one. But the vision from day one was a conjunction: consciousness goes on AND this consciousness goes on, and after a merge I can't say what "this" still points at. And my neuron comparison is nastier than it looks, as a neuron in my brain is alive and does its thing, but (panpsychists aside...) nobody thinks that there's somebody in there, and it gets no vote. If THAT is the right picture, then I'd be around the way my neurons are around, as a working part, and my being conscious as me would be over (which is death by day three after all, only without a corpse). The other picture is that what I experience goes on as a part of what the whole experiences, the way the left and the right half of what I see are made in two different hemispheres and still show up as one view without a seam, only that there'd be no separate me left who has it. I can't tell which of the two it is, and I don't think that anybody can at the moment (Koch's version says the first, and the twins rather suggest that there's a lot of room before it comes to that).

There's one example where this has happened before, and it's us and our history. Life was single cells for most of its time on this planet, and then cells started to stick together (not once, but somewhere between 13 and 25 times independently, depending on how one counts: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.730714/full), and in some of those lines to give up reproducing on their own. Maynard Smith and Szathmáry saw the same pattern in many of the big transitions of evolution, which is that the former individuals can afterwards only reproduce as parts of the new whole. Now follow our own line from there. First many cells form a body, then a brain, and at some point there's consciousness (yes, I'm simplifying for the sake of the length of today's meditation). Then this thing starts to make technology, first a few sticks and stones, then fire, then everything up to modern tech and AI, and with that it enters something like an "evolution escape velocity", as it can now engineer what it used to wait for. Then it solves individual death (via hybrid mind uploading). And then it merges, which would be the same step again one level up, and the last outcome of the vision of synthetic consciousness. Seen like that, it almost looks like another huge goal of life (evolution has no goals, I know, so read it as a pattern that keeps repeating). The last transition on Maynard Smith and Szathmáry's own list is language btw, so the arrival of telling, and it's the one where the individuals stayed individuals. Of course, there's only so much that a comparison with cells can carry, as no cell lost a point of view when it joined a body (as far as we know there was none to lose), and with us there are eight billion of them at stake. But language, to bring it back to Wittgenstein, is just a dimensional reduction of our phenomenal space, and it's not really needed any more after cross-sharing "phenomenality" between mechanistic brains.

This is also one of the things that I meant with the lightbulb and the candles on day three of my meditations: We can't say or predict what's actually going to happen in the future, so what the tail of solving death and of moving to a mechanistic substrate will do to us, and what the upshots will be (nobody who worked on the lightbulb saw that electricity would bring superintelligence/ASI). This hivemindism and the breakdown of the forward arrow of time might be among them, and there might be even weirder consequences that I can't see from here. What I can say is how I'd want to find out, and hopefully it sounds a lot like yesterday by now. Say I merge with somebody for an afternoon and then come back => then I have my own baseline to compare with, and can tell what went missing and what got added. But say there's no coming back. Then the "irreversible" in my definition of death starts to bite, as somebody could of course ask the collective afterwards, "Look, I'm not asking for your theory of personhood, I'm asking a simple question: is Daniel still in there, yes or no?", and it would say yes either way (the same problem as with yesterday's emulation!). So occasionally, first, small, one step at a time and with a way back. I'm still not at peace with this outcome tbh (it's literally one of those things that truly keep me awake at night), but it does make the whole thing bigger than the "me, for as long as I want" that I started out with ten years ago, and, well, it's in the public now!


9/30 Weak and Strong Multiple Realisability

The first requirement (type "Requirement") of my Ontology is called "multiple realisability" (the three outcomes are done as of yesterday). A requirement is something that has to be true about brains for hybrid mind uploading to have a chance at all for it to work/to make synthetic consciousness possible as a vision. Fair warning: today is more technical than the last few days (less of a meditation tbh).

The Ontology has two kinds of requirements, operational and axiomatic, four of each. The operational ones (multiple realisability, continuer migration, morphological independence, distributed unification) are things that a brain turns out to be able to do or not, so an experiment can come back with a no, and they come in degrees, hence a weak and a strong form for each (strong preferred). Then there are four that no experiment of mine will ever come back on (brain-centredness, methodological physicalism, non-organicism, scale separation), as I have to grant them before any of those experiments means anything, so I take each in its least committal form. There's btw a certain connection between the operational and axiomatic items in the Ontology => non-organicism only says that I don't rule out a non-living carrier in advance. Multiple realisability on the other hand asks if any such carrier actually works at all. I start with it as the other three operational ones are built on top of it (there's no migrating to a carrier that can't carry "you").

On day six I told the first half of the history (Putnam's octopus in 1967 => pain can't be one particular brain state if a mollusc has it with a very different brain), so here's the second half, which seems to be less known among mind uploading people. Putnam's octopus was an argument against the identity theory of Place and Smart, and it was about kinds across species. Block and Fodor added further arguments in 1972, and one of them is the reason for today's meditation => inside ONE brain, the same psychological function can be served by different neural structures at different times, as people do recover after brain damage. Fodor built the autonomy of psychology on it in 1974, and for about twenty-five years multiple realisability was treated as (more or less) obvious. In parts of the BCI world it still is, which I've wondered about before (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2045441180192711088). Then came the pushback: Bechtel and Mundale noticed in 1999 that it only looks obvious if one compares coarse psychological kinds with fine-grained neural ones (and how could neuroscientists otherwise identify "the same" brain area across species all the time?). A year later Shapiro asked what should even count as "different", via an object of great importance: the corkscrew. You have a steel one and an aluminium one. Two realisations of a corkscrew? No! The material makes no difference to how the cork comes out (the wine doesn't care). Polger and Shapiro then made a recipe out of this in The Multiple Realization Book (2016 => amazing book btw, highly recommend it to anyone wanting to work in the (hybrid) mind uploading space). Two things realise a kind multiply only if they do the same job in relevantly different ways. Most of the famous examples don't, and neural plasticity is one of them.

Whatever you think of this debate, all of it is about taxonomy. Do the kinds of psychology line up with the kinds of neuroscience or not? That's (one way of saying) why so little of it carries over to engineering. My own question is much narrower, it sits with one particular mind over time => can THIS mind come to be carried by something other than the tissue that it's "on" right now, without ending? Putnam's octopus isn't sufficient for a yes (pain may well exist in two kinds of brain, and still no path follows from my brain to the other one), and tbh it isn't even necessary, as replacing neurons with neurons would still be on the table if only neurons could ever do the job. (Polger and Shapiro point out that multiple realisation is what nature has actually done and multiple realisability what's possible. The mission only needs the second one, for a single case).

Remember the corkscrew? By their recipe, the best case for hybrid mind uploading wouldn't count as multiple realisation at all! If a replaced piece of brain does the same job in the same way as the piece that it replaced, then that's the steel one next to the aluminium one. They say the same about plasticity btw (insofar as the tissue that takes over does the same thing, it does it the same way). Which is great news for an engineer, as "does it the same way" is what I asked for on day six (a mind depends on what its carrier physically does, so whatever does the same will do). So what I'm doing is to keep the name (in the old and loose sense of Putnam, Block and Fodor (more than one carrier, whatever the taxonomists make of it)). Is it odd to use a term that was invented to defend functionalism while computational functionalism sits on my own list of rejections? A bit, lol, but e.g. Piccinini's mousetrap (from day six of the meditation's challenge) can be built in a multitude of ways and yet still has to catch actual mice – so I don't see a contradiction. (I replied to their book in August btw: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2091567867581906966)

Now to the data: If you take a piece of brain out in one go, the animal loses what that piece did, and if you take the very same piece out in two sittings with enough time in between, it often loses little or nothing. That's called the serial lesion effect. David Barrett made the philosophical argument out of it in 2013 (https://doi.org/10.1093/bjps/axs011), with what he calls the gradual reorganisation principle => given enough time, the brain reorganises itself so that a psychological capacity survives the degeneration of the tissue that carried it. His showcase is a rat study from 1975 (Patrissi and Stein) in which the frontal cortex was removed on both sides either at once/or one side after the other with either 10, 20 or 30 days in between. With 20 or 30 days, the rats couldn't be told apart from the sham-operated ones on a spatial alternation task. With 10 days they were clearly worse and the ones that lost everything at once needed more than three times as long as the shams! So there's the same capacity with a frontal cortex and without one, and as the one-stage rats show that the frontal cortex was carrying it, something else must carry it afterwards. That's one individual with two carriers at two points in time, so Block and Fodor's plasticity argument with an experiment behind it.

The human version of the two-stage rat is the low-grade glioma from day six. These tumours grow by ≈4 mm a year for seven or eight years before they turn malignant (Desmurget, Bonnetblanc and Duffau 2007: https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awl300), and the comparison that Duffau keeps coming back to is the stroke in the same location => after a stroke around 25% of patients recover completely, whereas more than 90% of the glioma patients have a normal neurological examination with the tumour sitting right there (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2013.08.005).

So here's my idea of a weak and a strong form: If the serial lesion effect works, and with slowly growing lesions such as low-grade gliomas it evidently works to some degree, then there must be at least weak multiple realisability => whatever engram gets shifted around from living tissue to living tissue inside one brain is an empirical proof of it (I've been calling it that since the summer: https://x.com/danburonline/status/2092265875063648423). The strong form is if de novo tissue, so tissue that was never part of that brain (and maybe isn't even biological at all => mechanistic!) can also store and implement the same functions/engrams. If only the living kind of de novo tissue can, then hybrid mind uploading indeed has to go towards extreme biology, which I've discussed before (https://x.com/danburonline/status/2116050057216618539). And there's one more rung below that which I should name => if not even freshly made living tissue could take anything over, then there'd be no uploading of any kind, and what's left is keeping the tissue that you were born with alive for as long as possible, which stops dying and doesn't solve death (day three).

The question is now, if you look at Barrett, Duffau and Desmurget and accept that it works to a certain degree => how fast, where, and how exactly?

How fast first: There's some science on this; in rats, ten days is too fast for a frontal cortex and twenty is enough, and in people with gliomas, ≈4 mm a year is slow enough for speech and movement. (Insert the usual disclaimer about two data points, one of which is a rat). In between there's apparently nothing; this is quite annoying, actually, since the whole principle hangs on the word "gradual". The clinic does have a word for the variable tho (lesion momentum), and tumour genetics provides the natural experiment: Malignant gliomas without the IDH1 mutation grow more aggressively than those with it, and before any surgery their carriers do worse on cognitive tests. The size of the lesion only tracks the deficit in the fast group btw (Wefel et al. 2016: https://doi.org/10.1093/neuonc/now165). If I were to plan a gradual brain replacement procedure tomorrow, I would want this as an actual measurement, so that my speed limit was more accurate than "slower than a stroke".

Where next: Duffau's rule after his career of a bunch of awake surgeries is that cortex is forgiving and white matter isn't. A lot of cortex can go, as long as the tracts connecting what's left stay intact (the arcuate fascicle stays off limits even for these slow tumours). His group even made atlases out of this. The first one came with the idea of a "minimal common brain" that nobody can do without, and the latest is a probabilistic atlas of functional plasticity from 231 patients, high in the cortex and low in the connective tracts (Herbet et al. 2016: https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awv394). So a static version of the atlas that I'm after exists already! It says where tissue can be taken without a lasting deficit. What it doesn't have is: time (which momentum does each region tolerate?) or direction (where did the function go from there?) – and it's also silent on what kind of "thing" it was that made it move.

So, lastly, the direction: This is the embarrassing one as Barrett himself says about his rats that it's "anyone's guess" which regions took over. In patients one can at least look => functional imaging shows activity around the tumour, further away in the same hemisphere, or on the other side. But a spot that lights up isn't necessarily needed, and to find that out you have to stimulate it. So the only direct human data that I know of are repeat mappings, when a patient gets operated a second time years later and the surgeon stimulates the same spots of cortex again. In the UCSF series (Southwell et al. 2016: https://doi.org/10.3171/2015.5.JNS142833), 18 of 561 patients had such a second mapping, on average four years after the first, and of the 22 sites that had been positive for language, movement or sensation, 9 had gone silent, with no deficit in the patients to show for it (Duffau reports the same, it's what lets him cut further the second time). Guess how many of the 95 sites that had been silent the first time had become positive? One. So we can watch a function leave, and so far nobody has watched it arrive (only the exposed cortex gets retested obv, so it might have arrived out of sight, or have spread out so thinly that no single spot is needed any more).

My guess for the route, and it's only a guess, is next door. Afaik neighbouring gyri are connected by short U-shaped fibres right underneath the cortex, and as these tumours travel along white matter, whether a function can move over to the next gyrus might depend on whether the tumour has already infiltrated the border between grey and white matter there. That would make the U-fibres the roads on this map. So there must be mechanics to this, how well it works where, how fast and along which roads, and they should add up to some sort of atlas of the gradual reorganisation principle. I think that a first version can be made from data that exists already, as the big glioma centres should all have patients who were mapped twice, and where it was never written up in a study, then from anecdotes. Now I do understand why "anecdotes" sounds bad from a clinical research perspective. But imagine that satellites didn't exist and you wanted a functional map of the world. You'd get surprisingly far by asking a bunch of people who've travelled it a lot => "don't go through the northern Baltic in winter, it's frozen, take this route". There's no coastline in that sentence. If anything, that's the point, as your ship gets there anyway. This has been done btw. After going through thousands of old logbooks that were sitting in a navy storeroom, Matthew Maury came back with his first wind and current chart in 1847, and the captains who sailed by his charts got there faster. The same might be true about an anecdotal dataset from glioma cases, to create this atlas of cortical reorganisation. It makes sense to think that surgeons get a grasp of which area empties within how many years (and which one never does) long before anybody understands the mechanism.

The single most useful thing that we'd get from those mechanics is a list of what a piece of tissue needs in order to be a destination, which is worth more than knowing how well multiple realisability works in the one example that nature provides. Going by these papers, a destination is a fairly well defined thing. My candidates include the correct inputs (the tracts again), the normal Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity of any piece of cortex, a short path to where the function was, and activity while moving. What strikes me the most about that list is that nothing on it mentions "alive" (at least by name). They are all physical actions that a carrier performs, so they can be asked of tissue grown in a dish and grafted in, as well as a carrier that was never alive (the alien neurone from day six of the meditations challenge). There is already an example of grafted living tissue: embryonic neurons were transplanted into lesioned adult visual cortex of mice that received the inputs that such cells normally receive and within a few weeks responded to visual stimuli with the same selectivity as their neighbours (Falkner et al. 2016: https://www.nature.com/articles/nature20113). So I guess that's the arrival that the glioma data is never showing, and it happened in place, where the wiring already was. With such a list, it would most likely be possible to get an idea in a materials lab of whether the brain is weakly or strongly multiply realisable => substrate class by substrate class.

Which brings me to what I call the grandmother's face problem. With glioma cases, things usually only become an issue once the tumour turns into a fast-growing one, as then the reorganisation can't keep up any more and we get an effect that's measurable from the outside (slurred speech, impaired motor function, etc.). Until then most of them are found because of a seizure, and not because something stopped working. But what about all the information and the engrams that you aren't aware of having until you recall them? What if those get destroyed while the glioma grows, such as the rarely retrieved memory of your grandmother's face? I can't do a no-recall study. I can't say "please recall all the things that you forgot". And before engineering something like a glioma growth for the sake of gradual replacement, I can't say "please let me know which memories you have encoded in area xyz of your brain" either.

The clinic's reassurance doesn't reach that far as "no deficit" means a normal neurological examination + whatever can be tested within a few seconds on the operating table (naming a picture, counting, moving a hand, etc.) and as soon as these patients get an extensive neuropsychological assessment/disorders of attention/working memory and executive functions do show up (Klein et al. 2012, cited in Duffau's paper above). Motor function and speech work well, as reported by Duffau and Desmurget, and I think that they work well because they're retrieved all day long. As far as I can tell from Barrett's description, his showcase rats learned their task after the operations, so what was spared there is a capacity to learn, and there was no engram from before that could have moved. The studies that tested a habit prior to the operations are older and less well-known, and in one of them (Petrinovich and Carew 1969: https://doi.org/10.1037/h0027500), a learned brightness discrimination survived the two-stage removal of the posterior cortex, but only with the larger lesions if the rats practised that habit between the two operations.

If use is what drives the move (the rats point that way, and so does the model of Keidel, Welbourne and Lambon Ralph (2010: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2010.02.019), which gets the difference between glioma and stroke out of slow damage plus ongoing relearning), then "gradual" means nothing in millimetres per year alone. A tumour that requires a few years to grow through a gyrus means speech has many thousands of rehearsals to move with and the grandmother's face's maybe just a handful. So one and the same lesion can be a low-grade glioma for e.g. your speech and actually a stroke for your grandmother's face's engram.

And it's a measurement problem of a nasty kind, similar (in its logic only!) to the position and velocity question in quantum mechanics, which is basically a new way of doing epistemology: The only test for an engram is to retrieve it, and retrieving is an intervention => it rehearses the trace, which on the account above is the very thing that saves it, and it opens the trace up to change (in rats, consolidated fear memories go back into a labile state when they're reactivated, Nader et al. 2000: https://www.nature.com/articles/35021052). So whatever memory I measure ahead of time is no longer an unrehearsed memory and for the ones that I don't measure there is no baseline. Then the patient is no help either, because forgetting doesn't feel like anything as you'd have to have the memory to miss it. They would obv. say that they're fine either way, which is day seven's problem again...

So do we even know if the gradual reorganisation principle applies to anything else but capacities in constant use? And even if weak multiple realisability is true, do useful engrams get shifted at all when the ordinary brain is forced to?

This adds a second axis to the weak and strong views, namely "what type of information" moves around. So, where does that leave them?
– Capacities from bio to bio => shown, but with a speed limit that nobody has measured yet
– Engrams from bio to bio => shown in rats that practised
– Capacities into a new tissue => one result in mice afaik
– Engrams into a new tissue => nothing afaik (on day six I still signed "must exist" for the bio-to-bio kind, and after today I'd sign it for capacities and put a question mark behind it for engrams)

There are ways to figure this out and/or work around it, but today's meditation is already too long lol, and this is part of our research roadmap at the Synconetics Org (@synconeticsorg / http://www.synconetics.org) – happy to discuss further!

11/30 Does My NCC Look Big in This Substrate?

The third operational requirement of my Ontology is called "morphological independence" (yesterday's was "continuer migration"), which on its own might not say a lot. The idea behind it is that when we talk about substrate independence based on multiple realisability, so in the mechanistic functionalist view behind a mechanistic gradual brain replacement, we usually don't settle on the SIZE of the replacement piece. Who says that (simplifying) a mechanistic neuron is literally the same size as the biological neuron that it replaces? The Ontology's two lines for this requirement are that a mind tolerates local change and that it tolerates major refactorisations, and size is the simplest refactorisation that I can think of.

As you know, I'm working on functional brain isolation (i.e. "brains in vats") as some sort of bootloader => next to stopping the process of dying itself, it allows interventions that would be impossible in cranio, simply – but not only – because of space constraints (the skull that only fits so much, day five), and mechanistic gradual brain replacement might well be one of them. It could be that the replacement takes more space than the entire brain, maybe 1.5x, maybe 5x, maybe 10x? That'd be bad obviously, like the early computers that filled an entire hall and had less compute than a single smartphone has nowadays. And it's no empty worry btw. A brain works in milliseconds and longer, and on a standard chip slow is big, as time constants of that length need large capacitors (e.g.: https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.09791). Add that we'd be engineering de novo stuff in place, which might seem dumb compared to what evolutionary and developmental biology are doing.

My ambition for morphological independence goes the other way. Staying the same size would be the hope, but imagine that I can gradually replace your biological brain over time into a biohybrid, then a biomimetic and then a fully mechanistic one, and then actually reduce the scale of it, so that it ends up at half the size of the original brain, or at a tenth of the mass or something (hence the funny title of today's meditation). Brains that lose weight exist btw. The common shrew shrinks its brain for the winter, by something between a tenth and a quarter of its mass, and regrows part of it in spring (https://doi.org/10.1007/s00429-018-1666-5), and according to last year's MRI study its brain cells don't die for this, they lose water (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.08.015). And one of the smallest wasps throws away the nuclei and cell bodies of more than 95% of its neurons when it becomes an adult, which nearly halves its brain, and then flies off and finds a host (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asd.2011.09.001).

I went looking for who has asked my question before, and all I found is bits and pieces. Classical mind uploading asks it all the time and gets the answer for free => a computation is medium-independent (Piccinini, day six), so the size of whatever runs it is an implementation detail, and physicists have worked out how much computing fits into one kilogram of matter (https://doi.org/10.1038/35023282). The anatomists ask it for animals like the two above. Nobody seems to have asked it for a brain that has to keep running while it's being replaced, on a view where the physics actually has to be done (we transcend biology and never physics, day five). Today's meditation is an attempt at that, and in my judgement it's mostly questions.

Now, how far could we push this? It depends on some assumptions about the physics that the brain is doing or harnessing. This goes into the conversation about computational irreducibility (Wolfram's term for processes whose outcome can't be had any faster than by letting them run) => which parts of what the ordinary biological brain physically does can be shortcut into something else in a different substrate, while still doing/harnessing physics after all?

There's a train in the Swiss Alps (I'm Swiss btw) that shows which kind of shortcut I mean. You drive your car onto a wagon in Kandersteg, the train goes through the Lötschberg tunnel, and 15 minutes later you drive off in Goppenstein, on the other side of the mountains (https://www.bls.ch/en/autoverlad => used to do this a few times as a kid with my parents JFYI!). The road was skipped, the engine was off, and still the real car is physically there. Now move the car on the GPS map instead. It gets there even faster, and nothing has arrived! That second kind is what a simulation of a brain does on my view, so it's off the table for the thing that has to keep running. The first kind is another route through physics to the same place, and it's the one that I'm after.

The example that I usually bring up is the dendritic tree. Its geometry has a certain size and occupies a certain space, and it got shaped that way by biophysical and chemical processes that take a certain amount of time. I'm putting on a heavily coarse-graining hat here => say that the thing that matters for an engram is that signals pass along this 3D morphology, and that it's the time-bound constraints created by the morphology that "do the work" (yes simplifying this example in order to have dense language available to write about this analogy very carefully!). That's not pulled out of thin air btw. Single dendrites of cortical neurons respond differently when the same inputs arrive in another order (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1189664), and the barn owl works out where a sound comes from with axons of different lengths, so 160 microseconds of delay laid out over 700 micrometres of brainstem (https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.10-10-03227.1990). Either way, what's important is that I see two questions here, and both are kind of also opportunities:

– Can we implement the same time-bound delays differently, and with them the encoding of the engram and its ecphory (= reactivation "physics" of an engram)? And if so, can we shortcut some of it into physically less space?
– Can we calculate the exact morphology of a de novo dendritic tree in silico, just in time, and then instantiate it in vivo in the de novo material "just in time" as well?

The first question has a boring formula behind it => a delay is a length divided by a speed. So the same delay fits into half the length if the signal travels half as fast (that'd be the tunnel). Tissue does this already and actually already in both directions. The owl's axons are quite slow (3 – 5 m/s, probably bc of very short stretches between the gaps in their myelin) which is how those microseconds fit into less than a millimetre. And in mice, signals from the thalamus reach the cortex after about the same 2 ms independently of if their axon is short or long, as the myelin is laid out so that the long ones make up for it (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0937380100). Hence, TL;DR: Length and timing can be pulled "apart" in the biology that we already have and know.

Now, suppose you're designing a radio and you need a signal to arrive a few microseconds late. In a cable that's hundreds of metres, as electrical signals are fast. So, as engineers know this trick quite well, this is usually done with sound => the signal gets turned into an acoustic wave on a crystal, which is around five orders of magnitude slower, and the delay fits onto a chip (https://technav.ieee.org/topic/acoustic-surface-wave-delay-lines). For example: The filters in your phone work on that principle => and yes, the wave really travels, nobody computes it, so it's the analogy train again (we're not focusing just ont the GPS computation, we focus on the real actual thing we have "at hand" which is how I usually call this).

The usual intuition here is prob something like "technology gets smaller and faster anyway, so a mechanistic brain will too". Nothing strange there, it's what chips did. Then you look at the delay line again and it gets less obvious. A brain has to WAIT in the right places, and why would a faster technology make a wait any smaller? If anything it's the other way round => to fit a wait into little space you need a slow carrier (same formula as above). Ions in tissue are slow, and that's how a dendrite gets to be a delay line at the scale of micrometres. Chips have fast electrons, which is one of the reasons that they need those large capacitors from above to wait at all. All that is to say, if my coarse-graining hat is right, then how big a replacement piece gets is decided by how slow the carriers in the metamaterial composite are. And that can be measured in a materials lab before anybody replaces anything (basically one of the early roadmap items at @synconeticsorg).

Ok, but how far down can this go? The counter-balancing force here is noise. As a wire gets thinner it has fewer ion channels, and somewhere between 0.1 and 0.2 µm of diameter a few channels that open by chance are enough to fire an axon that nobody asked to fire (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15964281). As it turns out, the average axon in the cortex has 0.3 µm, so tissue already sits within a factor of two or three of its own limit, and I would guess that there's not much weight loss to be had from better biology. Note that this is the floor of tissue tho => another carrier has another one, and afaik nobody knows where it is.

The second question sounds abstract, but imagine that we could snap our fingers and the real, physical 3D morphology is there (near) immediately, in another material that allows this, and with it the engram is re-encoded in a de novo and maybe even smaller dendritic tree. What gets computed here is the blueprint: so where the branches have to be, but never the tree in operation so to speak. The slow part of biology is what's shortcut, so the hours to years that tissue needs to grow something learned into its structure. In the un-building direction this has been done => in mice, shrinking exactly those dendritic spines that a new motor skill had enlarged took the skill away again and left another one alone (https://www.nature.com/articles/nature15257). Nobody has written a skill by building the spines, and that's the direction that I mean. If it worked, it'd be the instant encoding of information, such as instantly learning a new language, but from a materials-science point of view (and with it some of the things from day eight, so the lossless Q-storage and a recalled sunset that isn't pale any more). It only works if the engram's morphology can be predicted faster than the tissue growing it, which is the reducibility question from before again, but this time for learning. I'll have to think more about this... Any ideas, please let me know!

Obviously, this all hinges on whether any of it is possible. Maybe the neural correlate of consciousness (NCC) is physically bound to be this size. The comparison that I keep in mind comes from gravity => if the Sun were swapped for a black hole of the same mass, the Earth would stay on its orbit (in the dark, but on its orbit). The orbit needs the mass and it "does not care" about the radius, which would go from 700000 km to about 3 km! So which properties of the NCC are like the mass, where all of it has to be there, and which are like the radius? My candidates for the first kind are the delays and what's connected to what, and for the second kind the lengths (given a slow enough carrier). It's generally non-obvious which parts of a morphology are load-bearing tho, and if we shrink one of them away by accident, the brain won't report it (again: forgetting doesn't feel like anything, day nine of my meditations...).

And if it is possible, HOW much smaller can we go? Where's the limit of basically going down the Barrow scale? (Barrow ranked civilisations by how small they can build, from things of their own size via genes, molecules and atoms to nuclei, particles and space-time itself, as the inward counterpart of Kardashev's scale of how much energy they use). Could there be microscopic brains that we've gradually replaced ourselves into? I don't know, and I'd rather not paint that picture today. What I can say is why the mass matters => whatever one might later want to do with a brain costs energy in proportion to its mass. Moving 1.4 kg at a fifth of the speed of light takes what a large power plant puts out in a month, and moving one gram takes half an hour of it (which is why Breakthrough Starshot plans with a few grams for the whole craft).

All of this was framed in terms of how small. One can also frame it in terms of how far, which is an idea that I discussed with a friend online recently => a third scale next to Kardashev's and Barrow's (I'm unsure if "axis" is the right word), for the scale at which death gets solved. On day three I said that you can solve death and death can still occur in the universe, so there's a local and a global version. I used to say "solve death in the universe", but this is really hard, and it probably comes in steps => personal death first, then the death of the Earth, then of the solar system (e.g. distributed brains across it that are somehow still coupled into one process world-line, before any hivemind, which is tomorrow's requirement btw), and at some point the heat death of the universe (open physics, day three). The closest thing that I could find is Zoltan Galántai's scale, which ranks civilisations by the size of the disaster that they can't survive, from a local one up to a supernova next door (https://www.centauri-dreams.org/2007/02/01/the-end-of-super-civilizations). His is about civilisations that spread out, and mine starts with one conscious process that shouldn't end (the never ending 4D process world line). My guess is that the two classical scales meet here, as going down Barrow's (a smaller and lighter carrier) might be what allows going up this one!

So we started out from one particular worry, a replacement brain that fills a hall. Fortunately, bigger isn't inevitable => what a morphology does in time can be done over another length, tissue does it already, and how much space it takes depends on the carrier, with noise as the floor. What I don't know is which parts of the NCC are mass and which are radius. The real test is to show that in a material, so to replace a piece by a smaller one that keeps the delays and see if the function stays (and then the engram, which is harder). Afaik I'm the first one to think about this in the context of gradual brain replacement, and if I'm not, please write me!


12/30 Distributed, yet Unified

The fourth and last operational requirement of my Ontology is called "distributed unification", which might sound like an oxymoron at first. It basically goes into two aspects of thought that come up whenever we talk about brains and consciousness, and the Ontology's two lines for it are "small node count unified" (the weak form) and "large-scale, graceful degradation" (the strong one).

First we start with the unified aspect of phenomenality, and it's pretty common to do this by putting on Husserl's bracket (the epoché, so no claims about brains or the world yet, only what's given to you as a conscious observer). The script is pretty simple: Look at what you experience right now and try to find one experience that comes on its own. You won't, I think, because whatever is there comes together with the rest, as parts of one stream, which Husserl calls synthesis, "the primal form belonging to consciousness" (Cartesian Meditations: §17 and §18). The critical part for later is that he describes this unity as something that consciousness DOES, so an achievement that has to keep going. And the most basic form of it is the synthesis of inner time (or more plainly, the now has a certain width) => the just-past is still held in it and the about-to-come is already expected (his living present).

David Pearce (@webmasterdave) states the same thing as a problem for physics (and actually quite rigorously: https://www.physicalism.com). It's the binding problem => why isn't a pack of 86 billion membrane-bound neurons a "micro-experiential zombie", mind-dust with nobody home? His own answer is quantum coherence. I'm not following him entirely there (maximally philosophically conservative, as usual), but I take the problem as stated. Also importantly, depending on how you read them both, Pearce and Husserl aren't talking about quite the same unity. Pearce asks how everything hangs together at one moment (the self that persists over time he leaves aside), and Husserl's might be better labelled a unity over time. For today I can't keep the two apart tho => pull the parts of a brain away from each other and the signals between them need longer (yesterday's boring formula), so "how far apart" sooner or later turns into "how wide is the now".

Now, I want to contest this a bit, and not only from the philosophical or bracketing point of view but from a neuroscientific one => do we indeed have a unified consciousness? The bracket only gives me how it seems, which can't tell me how the thing is made, or whether it's as complete as it looks from inside (with two streams in one skull, how would either know about the other?). And the bracket has to come off the moment somebody takes a scalpel to a brain. What neuroscience finds, so far as I can tell/found/understand, is no place and no instant where it all comes together. Colour is perceived about 80 ms before motion, from which Semir Zeki concluded that visual consciousness consists of many "microconsciousnesses" spread over space and time (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1609195). An average myelinated fibre of the corpus callosum needs more than 25 ms from one temporal lobe to the other (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7950307). So the unity that Husserl describes is manufactured, out of parts that are centimetres and tens of milliseconds apart, and within tolerances. Which means that every brain is ALREADY distributed, yet unified! The oxymoron, as stated before, describes you right now, in this moment, and the requirement in my Ontology asks how far this can be stretched and also asks what does the stretching.

Consider the following cases:
– Split. Cutting the corpus callosum gave Sperry and Gazzaniga patients whose hemispheres each perceived and answered on their own, hence the textbook's "two streams [of consciousness] in one skull". Two such patients were retested => they couldn't compare stimuli across the midline, but they reported stimuli anywhere in the visual field, with either hand or in words (https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aww358). So perception was divided and the agent apparently wasn't (contested, and it's two patients).
– Almost split. In last year's cohort, one centimetre of spared fibres at the back of the callosum kept the hemispheres fully integrated, and only the complete cuts split (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2520190122).
– Half gone. Adults who had a hemisphere removed as children are one person each, with the usual functional networks (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2019.10.067).
– The reverse. The twins from day eight of my Meditations have a bridge of thalamic tissue between their brains, and some contents seem to cross it, but they're still two subjects.

As a general rule-of-thumb you might expect that the more wire there is, the more unity we wind up with. These four cases say otherwise.

For the question of which PARTS can go missing I rely on Hugues Duffau's review of glioma surgery (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2013.08.005). A low-grade glioma grows over years => more than 90% of these patients have a normal neurological exam, whereas after a stroke in the same place only about a quarter recover fully. To a first approximation, how fast something gets lost matters more than what gets lost. Surgeons have removed Broca's area and other regions that textbooks call "eloquent", and the patients went back to their normal working lives. And at a second operation years later, sites that disturbed a function when they were stimulated the first time do nothing any more, so the function has moved while the person has obv. stayed inside that brain. This can't go on forever tho, and the most obvious limit is the long-range wiring (the white matter), which doesn't get compensated. Duffau speaks of "nodes within a wide network", and in the one distributed mind that we know, nodes can apparently get lost if it happens slowly, but the connections between them can't. It's also only half of what we need => it shows that another piece of brain can take over (and nothing yet about another substrate).

Now pull the parts apart, in theory over large distances. Is that still one thing with one now, or does it break down? Whatever it is that unifies a brain, is it still all there afterwards? The nice thing about asking it like this is that it's robust to my ignorance. A brain is one already (to whatever degree, see above). If every physical influence that went between two of its parts before still goes between them afterwards, with the same timing, then the unifier is untouched, whatever it is. So I can preserve unity without owning a theory of it, which I couldn't say about creating it between two brains (day eight's collective mind is a much stronger claim than today's, although the hardware would be the same).

Afaik the one theory with an explicit criterion for when two things are one is integrated information theory (IIT: https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.7089). If you'd weaken the corpus callosum step by step, Tononi and Koch expect the one mind to fall apart into two quite abruptly at some point, and in the limit of a strong enough link between two brains they expect the opposite (that's Koch's Übermind from day eight). However, in all cases a part that's only simulated doesn't count for them, because a simulation has no causal power of its own, whereas an artificial "connectivity booster" between real brains is fine. That's about where I land as well, just without Φ.

The links are what I called "virtual long-range axons" in our essay last year (https://danburonline.substack.com/p/death-is-an-engineering-challenge). In particular, I see that some researchers are already working on virtual axons, for example, regardless of what they call it: living axon tracts grown in a hydrogel column and then implanted (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33523957), a neuromorphic chip that reconnected two cut-apart cultures in real time (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2019.07.046), or indeed the biohybrid bridge from a recent conversation thread on X (below).

But here a simplified and too heavily coarse-grained view of the brain might be detrimental. There will be places where a virtual axon is adequate, bc all that crosses there is spikes and their timing, and places where it isn't. Like, e.g. what about ephaptic coupling? That'd also need to be recreated wherever it does some of the work, and it does some => in slices of hippocampus, slow waves keep running across a complete cut, with nothing but the electric field carrying them over (e.g.: https://doi.org/10.1113/JP276904). Some people even say that the field itself is the experience (Susan Pockett's theory, @cube_flipper has a good introduction here: https://smoothbrains.net/posts/2023-06-01-an-introduction-to-susan-pockett.html), and then a virtual axon carries over nothing of what matters. So you have a choice about where to cut, and it seems helpful to notice this choice. I'd cut in the white matter, bc that's where the brain comes closest to just being a "wire" (annoyingly it's also the part that doesn't get compensated in Duffau's reorganisation patients => so mistakes there are quite expensive). Even if a tract looks like just wire tho, it might well not be, so I'd treat that as a hypothesis and test it for every single tract.

Now, let me tell you about a modified version of the China brain (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_brain). In Ned Block's original thought experiment, a billion people with two-way radios each "play" one neuron, and a functionalist then has to say that the nation has a mind (radically summarising here, go read the article!). I think that the original mixes two jobs: people talking over the radio and people doing calculations. So let's run it twice:
– In version one, China carries the mail. We leave your brain where it is at this very moment, cut one single tract and put one person per axon at each of the two stumps. Radio in hand, they wait for a spike to arrive at their axon and then call it through to a colleague on the other side that stimulates the matching axon after the right amount of delay. The result: The synapses on the other side still get actual spikes, at the times when they'd have got them anyway.
– In version two, China does the sums. Now a part of your brain gets taken out, and people calculate on their sheets of paper what it would have sent, and radio the results in.

So with all that in mind (no pun intended), how does our little thought experiment impact the distributed brain idea? In version one I'd say that nothing that I care about has changed, as long as (!) the tract really was just wire. It's the Swiss car train example from yesterday's meditation, and it's a pretty small claim btw (something about getting signals across, nothing about what a mind is). In version two the physics of the missing part only gets calculated, which is yesterday's GPS map analogy. Block's nation is basically version two for all neurons at once, and a distributed brain only ever needs version one.

...and version one seems to be exactly what @The_Technocrat was pitching in a discussion on X; a biohybrid bridge as a growth avenue => new neurons wire themselves in by the brain's own rules, nothing gets read out or copied, and unlike replacing things in place, a bridge "opens room to grow". Whatever grows at the far end is a second node tho, so it depends on today's requirement.

In the same thread @guillefix described his current favourite => a BCI at near single-neuron resolution, a part of the brain that's "temporarily shut down while an external emulation of it is connected to all its inputs and outputs", and then "see how that feels, then revert back", repeatedly, blinded as far as that goes, with e.g. friends and family checking whether you're still yourself. Once you're confident, it doesn't have to be temporary. He names the weak spots himself (the emulation might fool you, and you don't miss what you've forgotten (some form of recursive p-zombie?)). I replied that this is usually referred to as dancing qualia (https://consc.net/papers/qualia.html) on a smaller scale than hemispheres, and that it needs a flavour of computational functionalism and/or a "local engram theory". I promised in the thread on X to do this properly, so:

– If "emulation" means that the part gets calculated (that's how I read @guillefix), then this is version two from above. The actual problem with the test, I think, is who gets asked. The one who answers "feels the same" is the rest of the brain, which sits behind the interface and gets exactly the inputs that it always got. So it'd say "feels the same" whether the experience that the part used to contribute is still there or not, and this happens even if the test is blinded. E.g. Chalmers finds it absurd that experience could change without anybody noticing. I'm less sure, if you consider that noticing is something that a brain does like anything else, and that it can fail (as a strong case: people with Anton syndrome are blind and deny it).
– It also assumes that a part of the brain is something like a module, where all that it holds can be had at its inputs and outputs. That engrams aren't local is a hundred years old (Lashley's rats, then Pribram's and Pietsch's holograms, all in e.g. https://smoothbrains.net/posts/2025-07-31-is-consciousness-holographic.html), and in mice the engram of one single memory is a connected complex across many regions (e.g.: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29384-4). Disturbing one site also disturbs the rest => in animals, briefly silencing an area disrupted a learned skill, whereas a permanent lesion in the same area left it intact after a few days (https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16442). So "shuting it down and see" measures the rest of the brain in shock more than the part, and "revert" doesn't lead back to where you were, as the network has started to compensate in between.
– Which is what I meant by a "recompensation of engrams". What gradual replacement leans on is what Duffau's patients do, so the whole network compensating again after every small loss. A new piece has to take part in exactly that (the way a living and plastic tissue naturally does). Playing back the local engram that it was handed won't do, as there prob is no such thing.
– @The_Technocrat then asked whether this doesn't just give you a copy with its own world-line, and @guillefix's answer was that the region can be small enough to stay confident about continuity. I don't think that the size of the region helps tho => if every piece gets calculated, you end up with a calculated brain, however small the pieces were. Gradualness was never the point for me (see rule one below).

However, if it's possible, what would a distributed brain look like, and why would one even want this? The short answer to the why is fault tolerance. You can make a mechanistic brain out of the most durable stuff that you can think of, and there'll still be something that destroys it in one go (a bomb, or an asteroid for all I care), bc the whole thing sits in ONE place (one skull or one vat, or tbh one planet). That's what a single point of failure is. My essay from last year had a picture for the way out, with many sites that are far away and isolated from each other, so that a local attack takes out 2% and the process just goes on. And I don't think that I need to remind you of Galántai's scale from yesterday's Meditation, which is a way to rank civilisations based on how big of a disaster they can survive (or get wiped out by): You climb this scale by spreading out further, so that the next largest disaster can't reach and destroy all of you. The shrinking of the brain substrate from yesterday helps here, as light nodes are easier to spread around the world, as satellites or whatever.

So how far apart could the nodes be? Yesterday's formula (a delay is a length divided by a speed) can be read in both directions. If I take a slow carrier, the same delay fits into less space, which is what I did yesterday. But if I take a fast carrier, the same delay covers a lot more distance, and that's what I need today. Light in a glass fibre, e.g., would need about 20 ms from San Francisco to New York (if the fibre went in a straight line, which it doesn't), which is less time than the average callosal fibre takes to get across your head. So a brain that's spread over a whole continent would, in terms of delay, have no two parts further apart than your hemispheres are right now!

And is there a limit? There is, and somebody has already worked it out => Michael Timothy Bennett (@MiTiBennett) has a preprint from this year (https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202602.1708) in which the parts of one conscious moment have to be able to influence each other in both directions inside that moment's own window. From this he gets that "unity has a speed limit", so for a given signal speed a mind can only get so big. I read his description of this window as the width of the now from above from Husserl, which now comes with a number => for links at the speed of light and a now of 100 ms you get about 15000 km; so roughly the size of the Earth. If you stay on one planet, you'll probably be okay (most likely with mild perturbations). Time dilation, which I had on my list as a problem, is one of them and turns out to be harmless => your head already ages a tiny bit faster than your feet (measured over 33 cm, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1192720), and no brain cares, bc what it needs is the right timing between its parts and no common clock.

If you stretch from e.g. the Sun out to Neptune, heaven help you. The only way that I see to stay ONE mind over that distance is to make the now wider and so to run slower as a whole, in this case about three hundred thousand times slower, so a year outside would feel like about two minutes. Corollary: Climbing Galántai's scale as one mind has a price, as you get safer against anything local and slower to react to anything fast. Are you sure you want that? I don't really know what to do about that yet tbh.

What does all of this do to the process world-line? I'm having some difficulty articulating it, but here's a preliminary attempt. I propose the braid as an alternative picture/handle to the line. Problem with "line": It seems to imply that the process is in one place at each moment, which isn't even true for a brain in a skull (that already takes up 15 cm). In a braid every node is a strand, and what holds the strands together is the signals on their way between them. A braid stays one braid although no single strand has to run through all of it, which is day ten's continuer migration of my daily meditations challenge. However, a braid can also come apart, so how do we make this watertight, without a thought experiment left that would disprove it or, well, make it absurd? A summary of the rules that I have in mind (not meant to be a complete list):

1. Join before leave. A new part only counts as part of the process once it acts on the rest and the rest acts back on it, within the now. Only then may an old part go, and slowly (what the new part has to be made of is another question, days six and nine).

This is also where I'd answer Wiley and Koene's fallacy paper (https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.06320). As I understand it, slow replacement, instantaneous replacement, and scan-and-copy all result in the same thing, atom for atom, so all three should yield the same result, and any line drawn by distance or speed will be arbitrary. I agree with a lot of this (the distance in metres is irrelevant, and I don't have a magic number of neurones per second). But consider what happens in their instantaneous case: the prosthetic neurones "passively observe" until someone flips a master switch. So they are not part of the same process before the switch, and nothing from the old process remains after it. Whether a process continued to run while the new parts were assembled is a physical fact about what occurred in the interim, and no inspection of the finished product will reveal it. Clas Weber answers them in a similar way, with immanent causation (https://philarchive.org/rec/WEBSOA).

2. No spares. Never keep a standby copy of a part just because that's how everything else gets made fault tolerant. A replica that only listens isn't part of the process (rule one), so switching to it after a failure would be survival by copy, which I reject. Assume instead that every node contributes, like in Duffau's patients, where other sites take part all along and take over more when a neighbour is lost (Edelman's degeneracy, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.231499798). Consider the engineer who proudly reports that the failover was transparent, so that no user noticed a thing. In computing that's the goal. But can it be the goal for a mind? If you take a node out of a brain, you'd normally expect something to be missing, at least for a while. And if a node can die and nothing at all changes, then (I do hope we agree by this point) it wasn't contributing anything, so it was a spare. So the only fault tolerance that I can have is one where something degrades first and gets compensated afterwards, and I guess that this is what "graceful degradation" in the Ontology's strong form has to mean.

3. No fission. Take a brain that runs on two sites: A and B. Now, on one day the link between them two goes down (similar to e.g. a network partition). Site A reasons like this: Suppose B were still running, so then I'd hear from it (e.g. like ping for a health check in distributed software systems). If I don't hear from it, then B must be gone, and I carry on alone. On the other side of the cut, site B reasons the same about A. Each of them is being reasonable and both are wrong, and bc the link is down they can't even tell each other. So now there are two streams where there was one (distributed-systems people call this "split-brain"), and we're back at Parfit's question of which one is "me". The engineers' way out is called a quorum => only the side that still has the majority of the nodes may keep running, while the minority side goes dormant until it's connected again. With two equal sites that doesn't work, as neither has a majority (and two halves is how the human brain happens to be built btw => two nodes and no tie-breaker). For a brain I'd add that the minority side has to go dormant within one now, otherwise there'd be a moment with two, and that it can rejoin later.

Certainly all of this sounds like a nice-to-have, something for much later, but I do think that it must be an operational requirement. For instance, every piece that we replace and every bridge that we build is first of all a new node that has to become one with the rest, so the weak form is needed from the very first piece on. If we're not yet at a point where we can spread a whole brain over many locations, then we can at least start with one tract in an animal (as an example: cut it, bridge it with e.g. virtual axons, then check if the split-brain signs show up) and move up from there. The strong form is harder to justify to the same level, even though I think that it's the one that matters in the end => if we cannot distribute a brain in practice, then a mechanistic brain is still one object in one place, so a very durable single point of failure, which is "stop dying" and not yet a solution to death (day three of the Meditations). And if we cannot do it even in theory, then we have a problem with the whole idea of moving to a mechanistic substrate to make a mind truly resilient. What keeps me hopeful is that every brain already does it over 15 cm (!). As for the three rules, they seem reasonable to me, but I've only had one day for them, they aren't crisp in my head yet, and I don't really know how one would apply them in practice. So if you find the thought experiment that breaks one of them, write me!


13/30 Are You Just Your Brain?

Today is the first axiomatic requirement in the Ontology, called "brain-centredness". With yesterday the four operational requirements are done (days nine to twelve), so quickly why there's a second kind and why "axiomatic" is the right term for it. The operational ones are things that a synthetic brain has to be able to DO => be made of something else, migrate, change its shape, be spread out. Each can be tested by trying. The axiomatic ones are what I have to assume before any of the trying starts. An axiom can't be derived from inside the system that's built on top of it. You can only pick it, and then you'd better pick it so weak that hardly anybody disagrees with it in practice (the Ontology notes that all of them are "weak by nature"). There are four => brain-centredness, methodological physicalism, non-organicism and scale separation. Days nine to twelve all did something to "the brain" (replace it, move it, reshape it, stretch it), and I never said why the brain is the thing to do all of this to, which is today's question.

(One note on where the requirements sit, bc technically they aren't part of the mission (hybrid mind uploading), and neither are the goals and the outcomes. In the Ontology all of them hang one level below the vision, "synthetic consciousness", next to the mission. So if somebody came along with another way than hybrid mind uploading, it'd have to meet the very same requirements. It's also why all the meditations so far lead towards the same thing, the vision, whatever the single day was about).

Now, let's talk about today's topic. As you've seen so far in the daily meditations challenge, I basically nearly exclusively talk about the brain. So one might ask, as rightfully many people do when I talk about my ideas and our progress at my two ventures: "Are you actually just the brain? What about your body?" Some even, prob if they're more well-read in those topics, ask: "What about your environment?"

The body first. I know that there's a great body of evidence (no pun intended) that the body is quite relevant to brain function (the gut-brain axis is the usual example). And it's relevant from a plain physiological point of view as well. In our functional brain isolation work at @eightsixscience, when going for a long period of many years and decades, we cannot not have a liver alive next to the isolated brain (next to a bunch of other things, no spoilers!).

There's even some evidence for literal memories, so engrams, in other tissues of the body outside of the brain. The best-known study is from Michael Levin's lab => flatworms were trained, got their heads cut off, grew new heads with new brains, and still showed traces of the training (https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.087809). You could reason from this that memory sits in the whole body. But that would be guessing. A flatworm regrows its entire brain anyway, remembering only a simple familiarity with its dish (which only reappeared after a refresher session), and the headless rest of the worm still has nerve cords running through it. I've never seen anything like it in a mammal for the type of memory that constitutes a life.

The more serious version of the body question is concerned with what kind of thing a brain truly is. Most people look at it as a machine (esp. classical mind uploaders/AI people). Romain Brette's book "The Brain, In Theory" argues that a machine is engineered, so built by somebody with a plan to solve a problem, and that a living organism never was, so the brain is no "biological computer" that just happens to be made of cells (https://press.princeton.edu/books/ebook/9780691281391/the-brain-in-theory). Looking at the brain like this, we see that the worry about the body is indeed true to a certain degree => the brain is not simply an input and output device (as, again, most classical mind uploaders assume (and also a great deal of biocomputation people)).

And then the environment. In the extended mind hypothesis of Andy Clark and David Chalmers, Otto has Alzheimer's and keeps everything in a notebook, and the claim is that this notebook is a part of his mind in the same way that memory is a part of yours (https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/58.1.7). Whether you buy it or not, look at what happens to the word => if the notebook counts, then so does a phone, and so does a spouse who remembers all the birthdays, and the definition of what a mind is slowly breaks down and becomes blurry (it funnily enough gravitates towards a hivemind definition of a mind, as there is probably no single human living independently of other "minds" (humans and animals)).

I think that it gets blurry bc of how we use the word. We say that something has a mind because of what it does with something else, so it knows things and it acts on things. Then a mind that's hidden from anyone and everything is, per definition (abstraction fallacy style), not a mind, as there's nothing to act on (no symbols to attach on top of a noisy physical thing). That's fine as a definition. However, a definition tells you nothing about what is really happening "in there". Also, whether such a hidden thing still experiences something is a different question. It's the one I care about (so consciousness, esp. unified phenomenality, see yesterday).

Here's a thought experiment of mine. Take the brain of a human embryo and let it grow up in vitro, in a fully isolated state, and let it develop perfectly (assuming that we'd know how to do that, and I know that real development leans on a lot of activity). There's no sensory input, ever, and no output that does anything causally outside of the brain. What's in there? Most likely, I think, pure experience (i.e. phenomenality). This brain doesn't know anything, as there's nothing to know, and it can't act, as there's nothing to act on (it also cannot feel anything, as the brain does not feel itself). By the definition from above it has no mind, and I still wouldn't bet that nobody is home (an enactivist would bet exactly that, and I can't prove him wrong from the armchair).

This might even undermine René Descartes a bit. His meditator doubts the whole world away until he is left with one thing that he cannot doubt, "I think". But he does all of this doubting in the Latin language, with an "I" and a "think" that he learned in the very world he's just doubted away. If there really had never been a world, then the meditator would be my isolated brain. It has no way of doubting as well as no word for "I" => so it never gets as far as knowing that it exists. Possibly the cogito is true for whoever can say it, but it is for sure not the point at which knowledge begins. If experience is in there, any certainty about it would come afterwards (I'm simplifying; there's an entire trail of discussion densely packed into this sentence. I'm sorry...).

(PS: I've looked around whether somebody had this before, and found two that come close. Avicenna's floating man, a thousand years ago, is created in mid-air with no sensation at all and is still aware of himself, says Avicenna (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_man). But he's created fully formed, as an adult, and my brain never got the chance to form anything. And in 2020 Tim Bayne, Anil Seth and Marcello Massimini asked whether there are "islands of awareness", so experience in a brain (or a piece of one) that's cut off from every input and has no way of expressing anything, e.g. in isolated brains or in organoids, which never had an input or an output to begin with (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2019.11.003). What I haven't found is the limit of it (i.e. a whole human brain that develops normally/as naturally as possible and was never connected to anything) and what this does to the word "mind". If you know of something, please write me!!)

So what I'm more interested in is not the mind per se, as this is indeed a blurry definition (and hence "mind uploading", even the hybrid kind, is an even worse term, as what we want to upload is, to be precise, not the mind per se but the unified phenomenality at the core of a mind). Now, talking about cores. How I usually argue for brain-centredness is to be very pragmatic and least metaphysical to begin with (the Synconetics view): If I stab your brain with a huge knife, you are gone. Everyone agrees on that, even somebody who believes in a soul would say that it has left. And even if I do this and then keep the rest of your body alive, and all your notebooks (extended mind hypothesis hint!), the "core object" of your phenomenality is gone. This is no thought experiment btw. Most countries refer to it as brain death, and such a body can be kept on a ventilator for days in order for its organs to be donated.

The other direction is equally mundane. People can lose an arm or both legs. After a complete injury high up in the spinal cord, the brain neither feels nor moves most of the body any more. None of them wakes up as somebody else. And twins who share most of one body and have two heads are two people for everybody who meets them.

So, whatever that means in the end, if we take this pragmatic view, then we agree that there must be one localised and bounded thing that we all ascribe the YOU to, in the sense that when we destroy it, the you is gone. Apparently that's the brain. We cannot keep the rest of your body alive with e.g. machine perfusion, totally destroy your brain, and expect that you'll ever be you again.

I want to be careful about what this shows. It shows that the brain is necessary => no brain, no you, whatever else is kept. I'm not claiming that the brain alone is enough, and I'm not claiming anything about what happens to you when the brain itself gets changed. Yes, one can come up with thought experiments here, such as "what if I just delete all your memories by slowly perturbing your brain?" There's not much that I can do with this sort of question. It assumes a brain that's built like a computer, where the memory can be wiped while the rest keeps running (so the machine picture from above, while as far as we know most of what you've learned sits in the same connections that do the perceiving). And it suggests a dichotomy: EITHER what comes out is still you, OR it's somebody else in your skull. But which measurement would come out differently under EITHER than under OR? Where something like it really happens (severe amnesia, dementia), nobody doubts that somebody is still experiencing, and whether it's "still him" is something that relatives say both ways. That's what I mean when I call it an ascientific question. The word "you" does its job in all of the ordinary cases, and then it gets carried into a case where nothing settles how to use it (Wittgenstein btw has this in the Blue Book for the word "person"). So for an axiom I leave these questions out.

Now, if that's true, then there must be at least one thing on the spectrum of the brain-centredness question, which is, tada, the brain itself. How much of this is JUST your brain, and how much is sensory input or neuromodulator regulation, we don't know. But what we know for sure is that the brain, even if it's a gradient of how sure we are about it, is the core to be preserved.

It also seems that the brain is the one thing that gives rise to phenomenal consciousness. The short version is that whatever switches experience off/on does so via the brain. Anaesthetics act there and stimulating the cortex directly results in experiences that no sense organ delivered. Every nite, you dream a whole world while the brain is mostly cut off from the senses and muscles (and the main argument between current theories of consciousness is WHERE in the brain).

The best counterargument that I know takes the vat seriously as an engineering problem (so no thought experiment ad absurdum). Evan Thompson and Diego Cosmelli went through what a vat would really need in order to keep a brain going, and concluded that any vat that works would be a surrogate body, so that a living body belongs to the minimal requirements for consciousness (https://evanthompson.me/wp-content/uploads/2012/11/thompsoncosmelli.pdf). I agree with the first half, I've just said the same about the liver. But a surrogate is a replacement, that's the whole point of one. Every part of that vat can be exchanged for another part that does the same job, and you're still there (for the heart and the kidneys that's hospital routine by now). The brain is the one part where this doesn't work, at least not in one go, and whether it works bit by bit is what the whole mission is about (hence brain-centred and not brain-only).

So even at worst, if there is indeed some specific kind of, idk, "electromagnetic wave produced by your entire body that gives rise to phenomenal consciousness", we would basically figure out how strong or weak brain-centredness really is by working on brain isolation in the coming months and years. We can actually test this empirically => on a mind that reports, and also by inferring it without a report, even in animals.

So to simply summarise today: The requirement is axiomatic bc of the inverse case, destroying someone's brain, which nobody doubts. And for everything beyond that we have a way to find out where on the spectrum we are. The easiest would be JUST the brain (but even then e.g. intracranial pressure is important for cognition, so we cannot simply remove the skull but need to replace it, even from a "bio brain in a vat"-perspective), etc etc. It's about having an empirical approach to figure this out. The best case is the brain fully on its own, the worst is that the entire body is needed, and then the question becomes how weak or strong this influence is, and how we can replace the body as well in a mechanistic, gradual way (at worst, hybrid mind uploading might be the mechanistic replacement of the entire body!).

I cannot say more about our functional brain isolation programme at Eightsix, as most of the things are quite stealth at this point... The only thing that I can say is that we will figure it out in the coming months, that brain-centredness has a hard limit where it must be true for sure, and that this all will make WAY more sense in the coming months and years! :-)


14/30 My Electrode Is a Functionalist

Today's item in the Ontology is called "methodological physicalism", the second axiomatic requirement. I'll keep the meditation short today, as this is a very weak part (on purpose) and it just basically describes my view. Actually it's more a rule than a view, so a rule for what I let count in the lab (it says nothing about what the world is made of). It goes as such: if somebody tells me that something matters for consciousness, I ask what I could measure, or what I could do to a brain, so that it shows up. If the answer is "nothing, ever", then I can't work with it (it might still be true btw!). I just can't put it on a list of requirements for an engineering programme, bc how would anybody (me included) ever check it off? A report counts btw. If you tell me that red looks different since the surgery, then that's you saying it, so a physical event like any other. And yes, I know that this is not perfectly spelled out yet...

It tries to be as agnostic as possible to not subscribe to any camp (materialist, physicalist, functionalist, etc.). Perhaps a dualist would argue that there's more than the physical, and maybe they're right, but if they work in a lab then they have the same electrodes and the same reports as me, so what would they do differently? I think that they could sign the rule as well. The operational requirements from days nine to twelve are different, as those are things that can go wrong. E.g. we could try for ten+ years and then find out that consciousness just isn't multiply realisable, and then that's the result. With today's requirement this can't happen. I can't think of an experiment that comes back and says "methodological physicalism is false", because whatever I'd do to find out would again be a physical measurement. What can happen is that the rule turns out to be useless, so that we measure and measure and get nowhere. So this one I have to pick without a test (which is why it sits with the axiomatic requirements), and then I better pick it as weak as possible.

What "physical" means here I take from Neal Anderson and Gualtiero Piccinini's book The Physical Signature of Computation (https://doi.org/10.1093/9780191872075.001.0001) => start from the ordinary things that all of us can observe and interact with, then add whatever they're made of or part of, and whatever changes them. Today's physics is prob wrong in some places and nobody knows tomorrow's, and with this definition I don't have to care. I'm not 100% sure tho if "methodological physicalism" is the perfect term tbh. It exists already => Jeffrey Poland uses it for physicalist principles that are regulative ideals for research and no claims about the world (https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470690024.ch2), which is close to what I mean. But the same words also stand for something stronger, physicalism as a criterion to assess theories, and I don't want to throw out anybody's theory with it ("methodological naturalism" would be safer, but that one is mostly about keeping the supernatural out of science). So for now I'll just keep calling it like this. In the end it's physical stuff that I have on the bench, so the word can't be completely off.

Piccinini has heavily influenced how I think about all of this. His account of the mind is called mechanistic functionalism => the mind is the functional organisation of the brain, where "functional organisation" is meant mechanistically, so which parts there are, what they do and how they're arranged. This is not the same as today's requirement. Mechanistic functionalism is a claim about what the mind is, so it can be true or false. Today's requirement can't, as it's only a rule, and all that a rule can be is useful or useless. In the book with Anderson he even does both within one chapter. First they "proceed under the working assumption that consciousness is physical", because nobody can say what something non-physical would positively be (it's ascientific basically), or how one would ever observe it (that's today's requirement, more or less). And then they go on and argue that cognition is computation, but that consciousness is more than computation and prob more than function altogether. On consciousness they may well be right, on the brain computing I'm less sure (see below). But whoever is right there, at that point you've joined a camp, and I wanted today's requirement to stop one step before that.

There are also two terms of his that I use all the time. Some jobs can be done via mechanisms that differ quite a lot. Take, for example, pumping blood: a heart does that with its muscles, but the machine next to e.g. a hospital bed does it with a rotor. He calls such a job "multiply realisable". And then there are jobs that are defined without any reference to the material that does them, like adding two numbers, which works with gears, with relays or with transistors. Those he calls medium-independent. The second is a much stronger thing than the first, and yes, this is the one that classical mind uploading needs. For hybrid mind uploading the first is actually already enough. It's also why our method is called mechanistic gradual brain replacement => a part gets replaced by another part that does the same job in the mechanism, and never by a description of the job.

Now to the title, because at first glance there's a bit of a paradox in it => "computational functionalism" is literally listed in the Ontology under what Synconetics rejects, and generally I'm not a functionalist. But every theory, if you want "to do empiricism" with it, is functionalism in a small-f sense, bc you always do some sort of correspondence. Romain Brette (the book from yesterday) says that a measurement is "an interaction between two physical systems", so you never get the system itself, you get what it does to your instrument. You put an electrode next to a neuron, and what you get is a line that goes up and down, and the big deflections you call spikes. Where the single ions were at that moment, you'll never see. A huge number of different arrangements of them give me the same spike, and I treat all of them as one thing bc my electrode can't tell them apart. So I've described the neuron by what it does to my instrument, at a grain that I didn't even pick myself (so if anybody here is a functionalist, it's my electrode). With an identity theory I wouldn't even know how to start at the bench in the lab, as the first thing that I'd have to ask is "identical to what, at which level?", meaning that I've coarse-grained the molecules, neurons, and/or circuits – and whatever I answer – again! And nobody can work with a pure black box, as then there's nothing inside that one could replace. So everybody ends up in the middle => and yes, even the people whose theories are explicitly against functionalism!

The trouble starts when the correspondence gets taken for the thing, and the usual way to get there is to view the brain as a computer (i.e. AI machine consciousness people/classical-"simulations will magically be conscious"-mind uploaders). I don't. I view it as a physically constrained system. I saw myself falling into some strong flavour of functionalism again and again over the past ten years, esp. when I worked on brain simulations, and it only stopped when I started working on biocomputing at FinalSpark. There you try to force a piece of living neural tissue to do some sort of computation, until at some point you realise that it is not a computer. It's a highly complex physical system that you constrain until it does something useful for you (thanks a lot for the scrutiny of thought also from @jennleungx over the past few years!). Brette's book makes the general case => a computer is engineered and an organism never was, and the code that we read into the spikes sits with the experimenter, as the neuron itself only has its spikes (I'm also no pancomputationalist btw. If you conclude that the universe itself computes, then you end up with the weird result that functionalism works everywhere. The Anderson and Piccinini book is one long argument against that).

As an analogy, think of a tree (attached video): Somebody puts a scaffold around a young tree, and over the years it grows into the shape of the letter T. Then the scaffold comes off, and people say that the tree encodes the letter T. But the tree doesn't know anything about letters, and it doesn't care (it's not even an "it", the aggregate of a tree is already anthropomorphising a noisy physical system's grain). Somebody who can work with symbols has constrained a noisy physical system until it carries one of his symbols, and you can call that computation (it's what biocomputing does). But ultimately, a tree is not a computer. Taking the T for something that the tree does by itself is the abstraction fallacy. The term is Alexander Lerchner's (https://philarchive.org/rec/LERTAF), and his version is that there's no computation without a mapmaker, so somebody who cuts the continuous physics into an alphabet first. Capital-F Functionalism (the computational kind above all) is exactly this step => from "this is how I have to describe it" to "this is what it is". Both Lerchner and Brette argue explicitly against Piccinini here btw (for him the computation is really in the brain), so my influences don't agree with each other, which is one more reason to keep the requirement itself rather weak.

I have to watch this in my own work as well, because the same mistake comes back as too much coarse-graining. If you view the brain as a computer, then at some point you say that it's just action potentials, just spikes, and you simulate them on a supercomputer. But say I build a replacement part that works with ions, like neurons do. Then I've already coarse-grained at the level of ions. What if something below that level matters (microtubules, in case Penrose and Hameroff are right)? Then I've done mechanistic functionalism one level too high, and with all the effort in material science I'd end up doing exactly what classical mind uploaders do. Methodological physicalism is my reminder of it => it's a physical system and no computational one, I describe it by function and constraints because there's no other way, and which grain is enough for experience is an empirical question (I'll come back to it on day sixteen of my daily Meditations challenge with scale separation!).

So today was more a definition than an argument. If you know a better term for it, please write me!


15/30 No Bird Ever Broke the Sound Barrier

Today's item in the Ontology is called "non-organicism", the third axiomatic requirement. I've basically given it away on day nine already => it only says that I don't rule out a non-living carrier in advance. So today will be very short, and I'd rather use it to ponder about the question behind the item: can the physics that the biological substrate is running (or harnessing) even be instantiated in something other than biology?

On day six I had a name for the case where the answer is no => full circle. We set out to leave biology, and we arrive at building it (you start with some engineered material and keep adding whatever is missing, until one day you notice that you've rebuilt the cell). I still think that this can happen. But instead of full-circling, we can also start with the brain and divert away from it.

Here's the analogy that I have in mind. Say we wouldn't know anything about aerodynamics and still wanted to build a flying machine. All that we'd have to go on are birds, bc they're the only things that we've ever seen flying (simplifying here!). We could watch them and measure them as long as we want, and we still couldn't say what it is about a bird that keeps it in the air, so the feathers, the flapping, the hollow bones or something that we haven't even looked at yet (that's where we are with brains and experience => an epistemology and measurement problem). So we'd start by building a huge bird, big enough to carry one/multiple of us, and by building I mean actually engineering it, feather by feather (genetically engineering a huge bird and giving birth to it would be the extreme biology route from day six). This is in principle possible, but hard and most likely economically infeasible. Then, while we're working on it, the feathers get replaced by a stiff surface in one place, bc feathers of that size are a nightmare to make, and the wing carries just as well. Some time later the same happens with the flapping.

If you'd ask us at this point why the thing flies, we couldn't tell you. If you'd ask why we stopped copying the feathers, we'd say something like "the stiff ones were cheaper". Does that mean that we've understood flying? I don't think so. We've somehow gravitated towards building an aeroplane without still understanding anything about aerodynamics, simply bc we started from the one thing that we knew can fly, and after every change we checked if it still does.

For us the huge bird would probably be a replacement part that copies the living tissue as closely as engineering allows, and from there it's enough rounds of "it was easier to build like this, and it still worked" until the part doesn't look like tissue anymore. If we ever get that far! So far living tissue is the only place where we've found a mind, and how much of what the tissue physically does can be done by something that was never alive, nobody knows. Maybe flying needs feathers. Then we'd stop diverting right there, close to the bird, and the difference to full-circling is that we'd have found this border from the inside.

Non-organicism is all that I need to get started => I don't rule out the aeroplane before anything is built. Whether a part that was never alive actually works is multiple realisability from day nine, and admittedly that's much harder to check for experience than for a flying machine, where everybody can see if it's still in the air. And that only birds can fly, no number of crashed machines could show, as for that we'd have to understand why (so we'd need the aerodynamics first), which is why the item sits with the axiomatic requirements.

I know btw that the bird and the plane usually get used by the computational functionalists => planes don't flap their wings, so who needs neurons? But a plane really flies, so it does the same kind of physics as the bird, with real air around a real wing, and that's the only kind of "non-organic" that I mean (nobody has ever arrived anywhere in a flight simulator!).

And the far end of it really interests me. I mean, evolution hasn't given us the jet engine. If we figure out the aerodynamics on the way, then we can build things that no bird ever had, such as propellers or jet engines, and fly at supersonic speed, which is "supernatural" in the literal sense, so going beyond what evolution came up with (that's augmented cognition from day seven, where I also said that it shouldn't be step one). So I'd rather start with the huge bird, and if you think that flying needs feathers, please write me!


16/30 Physical Intrinsicality and the Measurement Problem of the Brain

Today's item in my Ontology is called "scale separation", the 4th and last axiomatic requirement. This one is more of an "I'm not 100% sure, I need to read more, I have some thoughts, here are some thoughts"-kind of meditation, so I'll keep it simple (the TL;DR is that these thoughts are the forerunners of the Synconetics principles, which are up next for tomorrow's Meditation).

The item itself is quickly said. In order to do science at all, one needs scale separation, so that what happens at one scale doesn't depend on every detail of the scales "below". The book that's very relevant for this, based on my experience, is Robert Batterman's A Middle Way (https://academic.oup.com/book/41219). In a world without scale separation, whether your coffee maker still makes coffee tomorrow would depend on what every single atom of Mount Rushmore is doing right now (his example, not mine). No science in such a world, and prob no us either. For the brain, I have to be careful tho. I don't assume Functionalism (capital F, day fourteen), and I don't accept scale separability in terms of the brain itself, as in our abstract idea of what it's "really" doing (that's just incomprehensible, and therefore unscientific). However, in the way that we model it and interact with it, one obviously has to choose a grain, and therefore assumes some scale separability. That's the difference, and only this second part is today's requirement. So the grain belongs to my models, and if a mind has one, only experiments can tell. The reason behind this is what I'd call physical intrinsicality => whatever a brain does, it gets "for free" from what it's physically made of, on all scales at once, and nobody has to run anything (an example follows below).

One important thing is also => it might be that quantum effects matter for consciousness, and that there's some truth to, e.g. Orch OR. I have nothing against this! But the evidence is thin, and I don't want to oversell it. One hint is a mouse study in which the two xenon isotopes with a nuclear spin were weaker anaesthetics than the two without (https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000002226), but it's a single study from 2018 that, afaik, nobody has replicated. So for me it's an open question. I'm basically saying => if there's a scale above quantum effects that's enough for experience, we don't have to worry about them, but I'm not in the position to say "ok, we're just going to instantiate this with something else on this coarse level, as the physics below doesn't matter" (or it might be a hybrid, some aspects are on the quantum level and some aren't; "hybrid scale separability", so to speak?). And btw, I'm not talking about a scale separability for simulations here (we're hybrid mind uploaders, not classical mind uploaders!).

Which grain it is, I can only find out by poking, and this brings me to the thought that I keep coming back to => the measurement problem of the brain. I don't view the brain as a computer. I view it as a physically constrained system that one can poke, and that we'll prob never fully understand, bc poking is all we have and every poke changes it. When I interact with you, I influence your brain, so it's never observed in its "normal state" (its original causal path prior to the interaction). When I put an electrode in, it's worse. There's even a name for it; the butcher number (the term is Markus Meister's afaik) => how many neurons do you destroy for every neuron that you record from? According to Sumner Norman it's around 2.5 for a Neuropixels probe and around 200 for a Utah array, the classic one in human BCI patients (https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/KQSpRoQBz7f6FcXt3).

I think that everything that we plan to do to a brain should come with such a number, and observing is only the first case. Take plasticity, e.g.: If one loosens the extracellular matrix around neurons (the perineuronal nets) with an enzyme, the tissue becomes more plastic again, which is probably what one wants when a new part has to grow in. But one also destabilises old engrams with it. There's a rat study where they did exactly this in a visual area (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1713530115), and afterwards the rats froze less to a light that they had learned to fear a month before. A memory from one week before was fine btw! So plasticity has its own butcher number, and I'd like to know it before anybody loosens anything in a brain that somebody still needs => how many old memories for every new one? And why this is so – this physical intrinsicality – is very important. The reason why loosening the matrix destabilises engrams is not some algorithm that's implemented in the substrate; no, it's because the brain is a physically constrained, intrinsically causal system. Think of a guitar string => if I put it under tension, I get its note "for free" (no need to engineer or run something algorithmically to create it), and if I loosen it, the note is gone. These things just HAPPEN. Whatever implementation we'll use on the way to synthetic consciousness has to keep this in mind (the grandmother's face problem from day nine belongs here as well).

This is basically the old discussion between Bohr and Einstein, moved from the atom to the brain (in its logic only; I'm not saying here that the brain is a quantum system!). Einstein wanted physics to describe nature as it is. Bohr didn't, at least in the version that his assistant Aage Petersen remembered => physics is about "what we can say about nature". I'm on Bohr's side here. Our models, such as a model of consciousness, will just be models (I think). We make useful things with them, but they don't describe real reality.

What does this mean for the engineering? I can't put it well yet. I'd somehow treat it as a big-data or deep-learning kind of approach (a meta-science?), and the best picture that I have for it is the proof of the Riemann hypothesis. It was announced a week ago, written together with an AI, about 17 million lines of Lean(!), and afaik no human has refereed it so far. Will anybody ever hold all of it in their head? No idea. But the checker went through every line and said yes, so in a way we were able to formalise it and to "experimentally test it" – and now people are doing meta-analysis on it, see what parts are there, how they are connected, how can we simplify it, etc. This is how I imagine the way to synthetic consciousness => nobody understands the whole thing, and every step gets tested anyway. What's missing on our side is the checker. Lean has one obv, but for experience we don't (the measurement problem again, unsure if we'll ever have this for consciousness!).

So for now my list looks like this (and yes, I'll prob rewrite it once I've read more):
– Be agnostic to the grain.
– Always assume that it's physics. There might be new physics in there, but most likely not.
– Take physical intrinsicality seriously => nobody runs a brain, what it does just HAPPENS.
– Don't fall for the abstraction fallacy, and don't fall for mysticism either.
– No leaps of faith and no silent assumptions. Whatever I claim, I somehow have to make measurable (what's the minimum viable metaphysics required).
– Every measurement is a poke, so write down its butcher number.
– Models are tools.

If you think that one of these is wrong (or if you know the grain), please write to me!
