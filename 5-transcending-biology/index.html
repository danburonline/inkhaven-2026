Transcending Biology, but not Physics

16 November 2026·3,809 words

Originally posted on XReplies and discussion happen there.Open on X →

The third and last goal of my Ontology is "transcend humanity". It comes after "solve death" (day three: [Nov 2026]) and "understand consciousness" (day four/yesterday: [Nov 2026]), and in the Ontology each of the three has a type => there's a "practical goal", a "theoretical one" and a "transformative one" => so today's goal is the one where the thing that changes is us (= the transformative one). It's also the one whose name I'm least happy with. I have three reasons for that, and two of them are why the goal has a new name as of today (until yesterday it was still called "human transcendence", which is also how it appears in yesterday's meditation).

The first reason is that the word is already taken. Taken literally it's harmless, as the Latin transcendere just means to climb beyond something. But theology, Kant and phenomenology have each claimed it (and prob. a bunch more I'm unaware of), and each of them gives a different answer to the only question that the word really asks, which is: beyond what? In theology, it's what lies beyond the world. With Kant, "transcendent" is what lies beyond any possible experience (please don't mix it up with his "transcendental", which is about the conditions of experience (until recently, I regularly mixed up those two terms!)). I came across the word mostly through Husserl tho. As far as I understand him, "transcendent" is said of whatever an experience is directed at without being a genuine ("reell" in German) part of that experience itself (Husserl's "Ideas I", §§41–44). His own example is a table => walking around it, I only ever see it from one side at a time (in adumbrations, Abschattung in German), so the table always is "more" than what's given to me right now, whereas the seeing itself is immanent and doesn't come in sides at all. Careful tho, as the table that I see is the very thing that appears, and no thing-in-itself that hides behind its appearances. Its transcendence lies in how it's given, which is never completely. So for Husserl, the answer to "beyond what?" is: beyond any experience that I have of the thing, and that's why there's always more of it to see.

Does my third goal want to get rid of that? No, and it couldn't. Husserl is quite clear that this has nothing to do with human eyes being weak, as any mind that perceives a thing gets it in adumbrations, and he says that this would hold even for God. So a mind with ten times my senses would still see the table from somewhere, and there would still be more of it to see. What can change is how much of that "more" a mind gets to, and with that I'm at my own answer. In the Ontology, it's us who go beyond something, namely the limits that human biology sets for us. And only those! The limits that physics sets stay where they are (it's the same line as on day three, where I stopped the definition of solving death at the universe itself => see the schematic that I created for today's post to visualise this). Death is one of the limits of biology, and it's the one that's important enough to have its own goal.

The second reason is the direction. "Human transcendence" can be read in two ways => either the human is the one who transcends, or the human is what gets transcended. The difference between "human transcendence" and "transcend humanity" lies exactly there, so in the direction of the change: the first one sounds like deepening and expanding what it means to be human from within, and the second one says that the human species gets left behind. I clearly mean the second one! I want to be exact about what gets left behind, as "humanity" is a word with several jobs too (the species, the people, and "being humane"). I mean the species, so the kind that we were born into, together with the limits of its biology (a species is a category of biology anyway, so the more of what carries you isn't biology anymore, the less that category says about you). I don't mean the person who goes beyond these limits, as that person is what I'm in this for (as I once put it in a reply: [Sep 2026]), I don't mean the people who'd rather stay (more on them below), and I certainly don't mean being humane. This is also where I go further than Julian Huxley, who made the word "transhumanism" known in the 1950s. His version is the from-within one: "man remaining man, but transcending himself" => and he meant it for the species as a whole. I start with the single someone (as on day one), and I drop the "remaining man" => it has to stay you, and it doesn't have to stay human.

So yes, this is basically about transhumanism, and the "trans" is the same one (beyond). Transhumanism is the position that it's possible and desirable to use technology in order to go beyond these limits, up to a point where the result may be called posthuman. I mean that in the transhumanist sense btw, so a being with capacities far beyond ours, and I don't mean the "posthumanism" of the humanities, which is a critique of humanism. Why don't I just call the goal "transhumanism" then? Because an -ism is something that you hold, and a goal is something that you reach, or don't. Which brings me to the third reason, and it's the simplest one => all goals in the Ontology are a "verb" + a "thing". Solving death and understanding consciousness are things that you do, whereas "human transcendence" was a noun, and it sounded like a state that one is in. That mismatch was more than cosmetics (don't forget, I do have diagnosed OCD!), as it's exactly what made me unsure for a while if this is a goal at all, or one of the outcomes of the Ontology. "Transcending humanity" is something that you do, and it even carries the answer to "beyond what?" in its name. I'm going to work through the limits themselves here, and then say more generally why this is a goal, and why it's the third one.

My first thought was that when death is solved, what it means to be human will have changed so drastically that this alone is the transcendence (which is true btw!). I then had to point out to myself that while this takes death out of the category of things that one simply has to accept, it still leaves the third goal in the category of the first one, just in other words. And then I realised that I didn't actually know what else there was. I had a goal sitting in my own Ontology that I couldn't spell out. I found that rather bizarre. So let me start with where the limits come from ... the ones that I mean all come from the thing that carries us, and ours came with the biology that we were born into, and when you then want to know who picked exactly these, you have to go and ask evolution, or you just stand there with a big "why these?!". The usual answer is that a limit was simply the cheaper way to stay alive, and it never occurred to anybody to change it afterwards. Evolution has been at this for a few billion years. I'm not sure when it last asked the one who's alive right now what they'd like to experience, but I'd guess never (that's the generational route from day one again). So I'm wondering how many of these limits we even notice. Death is the one that everybody knows. With the others it took us ages to even notice them, if we did at all. And along the same lines, I suspect that there are limits which are really hard to notice from the inside, as we never had it any other way! Humans see through a sort of slit. If you've seen a chart of the electromagnetic spectrum, you may have noticed the thin coloured stripe in it, which looks sort of like somebody forgot to colour in the rest. Vision is basically that stripe, so roughly one octave out of everything that's there. Of course, there's only so much that a body can do about its own limits. Often even the senses that we do have need help before they're of any use at night or over a distance. Some species evolved to specialise in exactly that; for instance, bats and dolphins find their way around by listening to their own echoes. Humans, with rather unspecialised senses and a skull that only fits so much, learned other ways to get around their limits. One such way is the telescope. If you point the right one at the sky, it'll show you infrared, translated into the colours that you can see anyway. But what you get is still a picture in the old colours, as everybody who has looked at a false-colour image knows. A notebook also helps, as it keeps what memory doesn't (remembering something is never like being there again, and I'll come back to this one in a second (trust me on that one, this one will be special!)). And a third way around a limit is the spacesuit, which takes along the narrow range of temperature and pressure that a body survives. All three go around a limit and leave it where it was, namely in the thing that carries the experience (the limit of being one mind, closed off from every other one, I'm leaving out today, as it needs its own day).

Now, a carrier that gets built (and is no longer born) can be built differently. That's the whole point => a built substrate is a steerable one, which is why I wrote in September that the ambition extends well beyond life extension ([Sep 2026]), and why I once called "steerable phenomenology" the thing that the transhumanist has long dreamt of without once having thought it ([Sep 2026]). Where there's a gradual replacement, there's that choice again for every single part. Each time a piece of tissue gets swapped for something engineered (that's the mechanistic gradual replacement from day three, so the core of hybrid mind uploading (which will also receive it's on meditation day in the coming weeks)), there's a bit of the carrier that doesn't have to repeat every limit of the tissue that it replaced. Where there's freedom like this, the substrate becomes steerable, and with it, bit by bit, the phenomenology (and I mean phenomenology, not just phenomenal space or qualia, but actually phenomenology). Some of what the tissue does – especially the storing – comes with losses that we're so used to that we hardly count them as limits. Memory compresses what it stores, as metabolism and physical space in the brain are limited, and it rebuilds what it recalls, with the gaps filled in from priors and context. Presumably everybody has noticed at some point that if they go back to a day that mattered to them, what comes back is both fainter and less reliable than the day itself was. (That's why remembering never feels like actually and truly being there)

It's only this January that I properly worked through what a built substrate could do about this, in a working document called "Synthetic Substrates and the Destruction of the Forward Arrow of Personal Time", which leans on Lyre's neurophenomenal structuralism (I never published this document because I was always afraid it would sound too crackpot-like before I had at least some directionally empirical insights into it). The thesis in there is that this limit is a contingent one of biological storage and no principled one => if a substrate took an experience and didn't compress it, but instead stored it whole, this would yield a memory that's both as rich as the experience was (because nothing got lost at the storing) and the same every time (again, because nothing has to be rebuilt at the recall). This is what I called "lossless Q-storage" there in the doc, with the "Q" standing for what an experience is like. Of course, while such a substrate would make remembering as rich as experiencing, allowing for a personal time that one can traverse instead of getting pushed through it, there is no guarantee that it can be built at all (that's an open engineering question, the typical case of: it works in principle, but not in practice). Since it only changes what gets played back, there's also just no time travel in it, compared to what the title may suggest: the substrate itself still runs forward, like a video player that "ages" normally while whatever it plays can be rewound. It takes physics as it is, limits included. (How one would still tell reliving from living with a memory like that is another open question in there. In the Ontology, all of this sits under augmented cognition btw => another day!)

So I think of the third goal as a related but distinct idea, that solving death lifts one limit of biology (the one that everybody knows), and that it will take a much longer process, which may or may not work out in the end, to get beyond the other ones. Often when I say "the other ones", I simply mean where a mind can exist, or what it can do and keep, or how it's connected to other minds (i.e. hivemind-ism). The Ontology has names for where these lead, which are its three outcomes: substrate independence, augmented cognition and collective mind (each of them gets its own day). There is a kind of change that I think will come first, and the Ontology already has a word for it. My first impulse is to call all of it augmentation, after the plain sense of the word, so more of what a mind already does (seeing more, keeping more, holding more, etc.), which can be checked against what that mind did before. But in the interest of not underselling it, I'm inclined to add that the memory example already goes further than that, as keeping a bit more is augmentation, and a personal time that one can traverse would be an experience that no human has ever had, so a change of kind. (If that far end can be reached, I don't know)

My main way of keeping the three goals apart rests on the process world-line, so if solving death keeps it going, and understanding consciousness lets us measure (and later steer) what runs along it, then transcending humanity is what lets that same world-line outgrow the limits that it was born with. And "that same" is the part where hybrid mind uploading goes a different way than the transhumanism that I came from, so classical mind uploading. There, people figured that one could isolate the pattern from the rest of the brain, which would allow for minds that get scanned, copied and upgraded like software. If the pattern is all that you are, you can run it wherever you like, allowing for transcendence in a single step, skipping the "slow" hybrid state in between. The price of this shortcut comes in two parts. First, when using a copy in order to transcend, we lose the one who was supposed to do the transcending, as death is the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line (day three), and that's what happens to the original's process, however good the copy is. Second, we become blind to that very loss, to the point where everybody, the copy included, would describe what happened as a transcendence, rather than as a death with a very capable newcomer right after it (a new someone with a head start, the same way a baby is a new world-line). So between similarity and continuity, I pick continuity! My Ontology says the same about world-line fidelity btw: it never breaks, and nothing gets traded against it (enhancement included). You may change a great deal on the way, but there can't be a moment at which one stops and another one takes over. And to be careful about what I'm claiming here: at every single step it has to be still you (the "continuer"), which is a different claim from "you'll stay the way you are" (you won't, and you didn't so far either, as the five-year-old you wouldn't recognise much), and it's also no promise about where you end up. That the world-line really goes on through such changes is a hypothesis, as I said yesterday, and it still gets its own day.

I'm having some trouble saying what exactly makes this a goal and no outcome, but here's a first attempt. Transcending humanity will tend to look like an outcome, as an engineered carrier comes with other limits than a biological one, whether anybody plans for that or not. This naturally makes it harder to say what's left to strive for, and it takes some honesty on my side, as I may have filed an outcome under the goals (yesterday's definitions were: a goal is something that we strive for and could fail at, and an outcome is what follows either way, as long as we work on the mission). But it's not merely an outcome. You could have the state, the capability and the topology that the three outcomes of the Ontology describe, and still be nowhere close to anybody having transcended anything, as the minds that have them could all be replacements, or nobody might have gone at all (which would be a decision too btw, as with a built carrier, keeping a limit is as much a choice as moving it). So what follows either way is only that it becomes possible, and the goal is that we get there, as the ones that we are.

Transcending humanity is also the last of the three for a reason (it's no ranking by importance, btw). First, it needs the engineered carrier that solving death brings – that's where the limits become steerable, and it's what the reply from above means with "solve death, augmentation comes next". Second, it needs the understanding of consciousness, and more of it than solving death does. By changing the experience on purpose instead of keeping it the same, an augmentation step takes away the marker that such a replacement step still has (the same experience before and during as well as after) and what has to be shown is that it's the same process, now having a different experience. Without that, nobody could tell transcendence from replacement, not even the one who comes out of it. That's a harder check than yesterday's, and I don't have it yet.

And what does all of this mean for humanity, and now I mean the people? Of course, I don't really know where to stop with that question, and I know even less where to start. Today's meditation could easily be double the length (respect to everyone who is still reading this, I've sometimes got the feeling that I'm the first and last person to read this sentence). Ideally, whoever builds this would attend to the people who want none of it, and then try to leave their lives exactly as good (or bad?) as they are. The first goal says "no one has to end" and it never said that nobody is allowed to(!). In the same way, this one says that no one has to stay within the limits of biology, and it doesn't say that anybody has to leave them. Nothing matters except to someone conscious, and ordinary biological consciousness already meets that condition (day one: [Nov 2026]), so a life inside these limits is no lesser one, and transcending them is no condition for anything. (And if some get access first, that's potentially a problem. And if some get pushed into it, that's an even bigger one. But I have to park both of them for today. I only wanted to say what the goal says. That's all. How we live next to people who chose differently (which takes patience on both sides, and we're currently quite bad at it) has to come later)

Another difficulty that this goal raises is that a humanity which "transcends" via copies has simply ended, with something very convincing left behind, although this one can be avoided by not taking the shortcut from above. That's the risk that I take most seriously btw, and it's why I keep saying that classical mind uploading could be a Great Filter ([Jan 2026]). Why shouldn't there be people who'd rather stay inside the limits of biology, or who only take a part of what's possible? It's very possible that the from-within version, the one that Huxley wanted for all of us, is what many will pick, e.g. because they simply like being human, but then do we tell the ones who want to leave that they have to stay as well? I don't see why both couldn't be around at the same time. In particular, "enabling a new state of humanity", which I listed among the original goals of mind uploading on day two, still seems right to me, with the stress on the "of" => a new state OF humanity is only one if it's still the same people who are in that state, whatever happens to the species.

Will it stay the third goal? The goal, yes. The name I've just changed, and I'm not sure that it was for the last time, as it's still the weakest of the three, and "transcending" keeps borrowing the glow of its religious sense. The oldest word for all of this that I know of is Dante's "trasumanar" (first canto of the Paradiso), and there it's what happens to him as he rises into heaven. No matter how carefully I define "transcending"; there's always a chance that somebody somewhere hears the old use in it (that's the "borrowed-word problem" from day two of this meditation series; where a word's old use quietly decides what has to be true in the new place!). If I borrow a word that's at home in heaven, then I imply that I mean what it meant there: that perfection is waiting on the other side, that leaving the world behind is what this is about, and that the limits of physics go together with the ones of biology. Or that if only a better mind looked at the table, it would finally get seen from all sides. I mean none of that, and if the name changes again, what's behind the three names should stay as it is.
