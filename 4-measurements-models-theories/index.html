Measurements, Models and Theories

15 November 2026·2,072 words

Originally posted on XReplies and discussion happen there.Open on X →

The second goal of the Ontology is simply called "understand consciousness". To be clear about which goals I mean: the Ontology ([Sep 2026]) has three of them, which are "solve death" (that was yesterday: [Nov 2026]), understand consciousness and human transcendence, and they're no longer identical with the original goals of mind uploading that I listed on day two. What I mean by this second one is that in order to make hybrid mind uploading work as a mission towards the vision of synthetic consciousness, one will definitely have to solve death, as the first goal, but probably, and that's a big probably, also understand consciousness. I do believe that in the coming years, especially due to the progress in artificial intelligence, we will see a great "rigorisation of science", and especially of the consciousness sciences as well. The reason being that we can now reason across hundreds of papers, or across whole piles of data, at the same time, and with that we uncover many of the inconsistencies in thinking and in terminology, and also the data flaws and mistakes that we've made in science up to this point (computational philosophy is basically the same move btw). In the consciousness sciences, this matters even more, as that field can't hide its measurement problem and is being forced to work out what even counts as a test of a theory ([Oct 2026]). You can already see the first signs of it btw, e.g. in the adversarial collaborations, where theories with conflicting predictions get tested against each other. I also assume that the overfocus on neurocomputational functionalism will fade away, as right now it's mostly held as a faith rather than tested as a hypothesis ([Jul 2026]), and that we'll look back at those days and wonder how quickly we anthropomorphised anything that outputs "I'm angry" (the angry chair from an earlier tweet: [Feb 2026]).

And due to this rigorisation, I do expect that we'll get somewhere with consciousness. Probably not to a theory, but probably to a model, or at least to a measurement theory, and as these three get mixed up all the time, let me say how I use them (for the first one, see Johannes Kleiner and his workshop "Towards a Measurement Theory for Consciousness Science": [Feb 2026]). A measurement theory is the most "modest" of the three, as it only tells you what kind of data count at all (e.g. reports, behaviour, neural markers, etc.), and how to read that data as experience => it doesn't say why. With a model, you can already predict which experience goes with which physical state. And a theory, well, that would say what consciousness is and why there is any of it in the first place. Why would one precede the other? Because you can calibrate well before you can explain. So what I expect is measurement first, within years, then predictive models, and a theory of what consciousness is may never be reachable by science at all. And no, measurement doesn't come for free either, as what you think consciousness is already shapes what you accept as a measurement of it (that's Birch's point: [Apr 2026]), so it's measurement before a full theory, with the two correcting each other in turns.

The nuanced difference here, and why this is in the Ontology at all, is that while working on what I nowadays call biomimetic neuromorphics (it takes the slot that whole brain emulation has in classical mind uploading, but in hybrid mind uploading), we do what I call "consciousness engineering" (which is also the name of the Frontiers Research Topic that I co-organised: [May 2026]). Meaning: we engineer with matter that we try to couple and integrate with the biological brain, so that experience extends across both parts (that's what I mean by "bridging" phenomenal consciousness), with the aim of ultimately letting it "host" synthetic consciousness. And my bet is that measurement theories, and maybe even models of consciousness, will emerge from exactly this kind of work. And yes, understanding consciousness is such a huge goal, so why is it "only" the second one? I order the three goals by what depends on what (and no longer by importance): solving death is what all of this is for, understanding consciousness is what's needed to check that death really got solved (more on that in a second), and human transcendence, the third goal (and the meditation for tomorrow!), presupposes both, as it's even more far-reaching than solving death, comes at, or shortly after, the inflexion point of solving death, and is actually coupled with some of the outcomes of the Ontology. I do make a difference between goals and outcomes btw: a goal is something that we strive for and could fail at, and an outcome is what follows either way, as long as we work on the mission. So why isn't understanding consciousness just an outcome then? My first answer was "because we should strive to do that", which is no reason yet. The reason is that hybrid mind uploading can sidestep a theory of consciousness when it comes to building the thing that carries the process, but it can't sidestep measuring, as without a way to tell that experience is still there, and that it's still this experience, we could never know if the mission succeeded (that's the point that I left open yesterday btw, that it has to be shown physically if a world-line really went on). Hence a goal, at least up to the level of measurement, and no mere outcome.

I always say that the whole point of hybrid mind uploading is to not need a theory of consciousness in order to make it work, or to have confidence in it (as in classical mind uploading via simulations, where one has to believe that it's going to work due to a theory of consciousness, an unproven one, which might be wrong), but to actually sidestep it. So not ignoring the problem, but sidestepping it! Ignoring the theories completely would be insanity, as you'd end up assuming one anyway, just without noticing ([Oct 2026]). What sidestepping means is that hybrid mind uploading doesn't let its success depend on any single theory of consciousness being the only true one. It assumes a few things openly (the process world-line is one of them => I hold it as a hypothesis, and yes, yet again, this is for another meditations challenge day!), and the idea is that rival theories become predictions that a measurement can test. So instead of betting on just a single one of them, the route should be built in a way so the question can be checked gradually/step by step. It's, e.g. similar to how steam engines came before thermodynamics (Newcomen had his engine running in 1712, and Carnot explained what such an engine does in 1824), or to how Faraday had a wire rotating around a magnet in 1821, the first electric motor basically, long before anybody knew what an electron is (Thomson, 1897). You don't need to understand electrons in order to build an electric motor. The analogy breaks at one place tho, and well, yes, of course it's the place that matters a lot: you can see from the outside that a motor works; it turns. You can't see from the outside that a world-line went on. And that's exactly why the measuring part can't be sidestepped => what has to be shown is the same experience before, during and after a replacement step, from the reports as well as from physical markers.

It's all about doing something useful, and for doing something useful we're using an engineering mindset and not a science mindset (hence clearly consciousness engineering, and not consciousness science). However, I do think that this goal in the Ontology is where the science can slip back in a bit, as, like I said earlier, one can probably solve the measurement problem of consciousness with this work, or at least contribute heavily towards it, and then, with that, maybe even go towards very pragmatic modelling of consciousness. The example from another discipline that fits best here is temperature, I think. Thermometers as well as fixed scales (Fahrenheit, 1724) were around long before a kinetic theory explained what temperature even is (1850s–1870s). Hasok Chang showed in "Inventing Temperature" how people got out of this circle there as you can't calibrate a thermometer without knowing what temperature really is (and you can't know what temperature is without a thermometer in the first place, so every round of measuring corrected the concept, and also the other way around). So, did we achieve the goal of "understand consciousness" when we "only" have a model of consciousness instead of a theory? Well, not really, I guess? Or let me be more exact, as "understand" comes in three levels for me => measuring it, modelling it and explaining it. The goal counts as reached at the second level, and the third one is the horizon, where I don't know if science can ever get to. Why the second and not already the first? Because measuring only tells you afterwards, and for a step that you can't undo you want to know before, so the model that I'm asking for has to be able to tell me before a replacement step if the experience will change.

Trying to answer this question properly is a Pandora's box tho, or rather a deep tunnel of issues that we're opening here (which is btw kind of on purpose why I added this goal to the Ontology). The reason being: what is a theory of consciousness even? It might be that the scientific method as we have it isn't equipped to answer such questions, as it might sit on the same level of investigation as "what is an electron?", "why is there an electron?" and "why is there stuff rather than no stuff?". Careful though, as these are three different questions and I shouldn't ask them in one breath: the first one is a question for physics, the second one asks why the laws are as they are, and the third one is Leibniz's question. Science is great at the first kind, and my worry is that a theory of what consciousness is belongs to the other two, so basically just one level above absolute explanatory nihilism (the point where the only answer left is "it just is"), and that you slowly converge towards metaphysics instead of physics there, and that it might never be testable. Maybe a theory of consciousness will always be poetry, in the sense in which Bohr said that when it comes to atoms, language can be used only as in poetry ([Oct 2026]), and never actually within the realm of the scientific method (which is no insult to the people who describe experience carefully btw, that's rigorous work). So maybe we're just left with models of consciousness, in order to make useful predictions, which is basically what all of science does, and what the big break in physics was about back then when Bohr and Einstein had their discussion about quantum physics: is the theory complete and does it describe what's really there, or is a wave function "just" a model for us to predict with? So, if we have a really good model of consciousness, we might just say that this is useful enough (esp. in engineering, e.g. for hybrid mind uploading within biomimetic neuromorphics) to say that "we indeed understand consciousness now". And I think that this is legit and not a cheap way out. For a very "Wittgensteinian reason": the meaning of a word like "understand" isn't fixed in advance. What "fixes" it is how we're using it. We say that a pupil understands a rule when he knows how to go on (that's from his Philosophical Investigations, §§143–155). Understanding in the engineer's use of the word in that sense is a model that allows us to measure, predict, but also steer experience across a replacement step. There is another use that may well remain open => the one that asks why there is experience at all! I put this goal into the Ontology to keep both uses in view (in my first draft I ended with "we're just humans, writing down our higher-dimensional thoughts as lower-dimensional language", but that's the picture of the early Wittgenstein, which he later gave up himself).
