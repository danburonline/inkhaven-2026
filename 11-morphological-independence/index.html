Does My NCC Look Big in This Substrate?

Daniel Burger·22 November 2026·2,526 words

TL;DR

The third operational requirement of my Ontology is called "morphological independence": who says that a mechanistic neuron is literally the same size as the biological neuron that it replaces? Bigger isn't inevitable, as what a morphology does in time can be done over another length.

Originally posted on XReplies and discussion happen there.Open on X →

The third operational requirement of my Ontology is called "morphological independence" (yesterday's was "continuer migration"), which on its own might not say a lot. The idea behind it is that when we talk about substrate independence based on multiple realisability, so in the mechanistic functionalist view behind a mechanistic gradual brain replacement, we usually don't settle on the SIZE of the replacement piece. Who says that (simplifying) a mechanistic neuron is literally the same size as the biological neuron that it replaces? The Ontology's two lines for this requirement are that a mind tolerates local change and that it tolerates major refactorisations, and size is the simplest refactorisation that I can think of.

As you know, I'm working on functional brain isolation (i.e. "brains in vats") as some sort of bootloader => next to stopping the process of dying itself, it allows interventions that would be impossible in cranio, simply – but not only – because of space constraints (the skull that only fits so much, day five), and mechanistic gradual brain replacement might well be one of them. It could be that the replacement takes more space than the entire brain, maybe 1.5x, maybe 5x, maybe 10x? That'd be bad obviously, like the early computers that filled an entire hall and had less compute than a single smartphone has nowadays. And it's no empty worry btw. A brain works in milliseconds and longer, and on a standard chip slow is big, as time constants of that length need large capacitors (e.g.: [arxiv.org]). Add that we'd be engineering de novo stuff in place, which might seem dumb compared to what evolutionary and developmental biology are doing.

My ambition for morphological independence goes the other way. Staying the same size would be the hope, but imagine that I can gradually replace your biological brain over time into a biohybrid, then a biomimetic and then a fully mechanistic one, and then actually reduce the scale of it, so that it ends up at half the size of the original brain, or at a tenth of the mass or something (hence the funny title of today's meditation). Brains that lose weight exist btw. The common shrew shrinks its brain for the winter, by something between a tenth and a quarter of its mass, and regrows part of it in spring ([doi.org]), and according to last year's MRI study its brain cells don't die for this, they lose water ([doi.org]). And one of the smallest wasps throws away the nuclei and cell bodies of more than 95% of its neurons when it becomes an adult, which nearly halves its brain, and then flies off and finds a host ([doi.org]).

I went looking for who has asked my question before, and all I found is bits and pieces. Classical mind uploading asks it all the time and gets the answer for free => a computation is medium-independent (Piccinini, day six), so the size of whatever runs it is an implementation detail, and physicists have worked out how much computing fits into one kilogram of matter ([doi.org]). The anatomists ask it for animals like the two above. Nobody seems to have asked it for a brain that has to keep running while it's being replaced, on a view where the physics actually has to be done (we transcend biology and never physics, day five). Today's meditation is an attempt at that, and in my judgement it's mostly questions.

Now, how far could we push this? It depends on some assumptions about the physics that the brain is doing or harnessing. This goes into the conversation about computational irreducibility (Wolfram's term for processes whose outcome can't be had any faster than by letting them run) => which parts of what the ordinary biological brain physically does can be shortcut into something else in a different substrate, while still doing/harnessing physics after all?

There's a train in the Swiss Alps (I'm Swiss btw) that shows which kind of shortcut I mean. You drive your car onto a wagon in Kandersteg, the train goes through the Lötschberg tunnel, and 15 minutes later you drive off in Goppenstein, on the other side of the mountains ([bls.ch] => used to do this a few times as a kid with my parents JFYI!). The road was skipped, the engine was off, and still the real car is physically there. Now move the car on the GPS map instead. It gets there even faster, and nothing has arrived! That second kind is what a simulation of a brain does on my view, so it's off the table for the thing that has to keep running. The first kind is another route through physics to the same place, and it's the one that I'm after.

The example that I usually bring up is the dendritic tree. Its geometry has a certain size and occupies a certain space, and it got shaped that way by biophysical and chemical processes that take a certain amount of time. I'm putting on a heavily coarse-graining hat here => say that the thing that matters for an engram is that signals pass along this 3D morphology, and that it's the time-bound constraints created by the morphology that "do the work" (yes simplifying this example in order to have dense language available to write about this analogy very carefully!). That's not pulled out of thin air btw. Single dendrites of cortical neurons respond differently when the same inputs arrive in another order ([doi.org]), and the barn owl works out where a sound comes from with axons of different lengths, so 160 microseconds of delay laid out over 700 micrometres of brainstem ([doi.org]). Either way, what's important is that I see two questions here, and both are kind of also opportunities:

– Can we implement the same time-bound delays differently, and with them the encoding of the engram and its ecphory (= reactivation "physics" of an engram)? And if so, can we shortcut some of it into physically less space?
– Can we calculate the exact morphology of a de novo dendritic tree in silico, just in time, and then instantiate it in vivo in the de novo material "just in time" as well?

The first question has a boring formula behind it => a delay is a length divided by a speed. So the same delay fits into half the length if the signal travels half as fast (that'd be the tunnel). Tissue does this already and actually already in both directions. The owl's axons are quite slow (3 – 5 m/s, probably bc of very short stretches between the gaps in their myelin) which is how those microseconds fit into less than a millimetre. And in mice, signals from the thalamus reach the cortex after about the same 2 ms independently of if their axon is short or long, as the myelin is laid out so that the long ones make up for it ([doi.org]). Hence, TL;DR: Length and timing can be pulled "apart" in the biology that we already have and know.

Now, suppose you're designing a radio and you need a signal to arrive a few microseconds late. In a cable that's hundreds of metres, as electrical signals are fast. So, as engineers know this trick quite well, this is usually done with sound => the signal gets turned into an acoustic wave on a crystal, which is around five orders of magnitude slower, and the delay fits onto a chip ([technav.ieee.org]). For example: The filters in your phone work on that principle => and yes, the wave really travels, nobody computes it, so it's the analogy train again (we're not focusing just ont the GPS computation, we focus on the real actual thing we have "at hand" which is how I usually call this).

The usual intuition here is prob something like "technology gets smaller and faster anyway, so a mechanistic brain will too". Nothing strange there, it's what chips did. Then you look at the delay line again and it gets less obvious. A brain has to WAIT in the right places, and why would a faster technology make a wait any smaller? If anything it's the other way round => to fit a wait into little space you need a slow carrier (same formula as above). Ions in tissue are slow, and that's how a dendrite gets to be a delay line at the scale of micrometres. Chips have fast electrons, which is one of the reasons that they need those large capacitors from above to wait at all. All that is to say, if my coarse-graining hat is right, then how big a replacement piece gets is decided by how slow the carriers in the metamaterial composite are. And that can be measured in a materials lab before anybody replaces anything (basically one of the early roadmap items at @synconeticsorg).

Ok, but how far down can this go? The counter-balancing force here is noise. As a wire gets thinner it has fewer ion channels, and somewhere between 0.1 and 0.2 µm of diameter a few channels that open by chance are enough to fire an axon that nobody asked to fire ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov]). As it turns out, the average axon in the cortex has 0.3 µm, so tissue already sits within a factor of two or three of its own limit, and I would guess that there's not much weight loss to be had from better biology. Note that this is the floor of tissue tho => another carrier has another one, and afaik nobody knows where it is.

The second question sounds abstract, but imagine that we could snap our fingers and the real, physical 3D morphology is there (near) immediately, in another material that allows this, and with it the engram is re-encoded in a de novo and maybe even smaller dendritic tree. What gets computed here is the blueprint: so where the branches have to be, but never the tree in operation so to speak. The slow part of biology is what's shortcut, so the hours to years that tissue needs to grow something learned into its structure. In the un-building direction this has been done => in mice, shrinking exactly those dendritic spines that a new motor skill had enlarged took the skill away again and left another one alone ([nature.com]). Nobody has written a skill by building the spines, and that's the direction that I mean. If it worked, it'd be the instant encoding of information, such as instantly learning a new language, but from a materials-science point of view (and with it some of the things from day eight, so the lossless Q-storage and a recalled sunset that isn't pale any more). It only works if the engram's morphology can be predicted faster than the tissue growing it, which is the reducibility question from before again, but this time for learning. I'll have to think more about this... Any ideas, please let me know!

Obviously, this all hinges on whether any of it is possible. Maybe the neural correlate of consciousness (NCC) is physically bound to be this size. The comparison that I keep in mind comes from gravity => if the Sun were swapped for a black hole of the same mass, the Earth would stay on its orbit (in the dark, but on its orbit). The orbit needs the mass and it "does not care" about the radius, which would go from 700000 km to about 3 km! So which properties of the NCC are like the mass, where all of it has to be there, and which are like the radius? My candidates for the first kind are the delays and what's connected to what, and for the second kind the lengths (given a slow enough carrier). It's generally non-obvious which parts of a morphology are load-bearing tho, and if we shrink one of them away by accident, the brain won't report it (again: forgetting doesn't feel like anything, day nine of my meditations...).

And if it is possible, HOW much smaller can we go? Where's the limit of basically going down the Barrow scale? (Barrow ranked civilisations by how small they can build, from things of their own size via genes, molecules and atoms to nuclei, particles and space-time itself, as the inward counterpart of Kardashev's scale of how much energy they use). Could there be microscopic brains that we've gradually replaced ourselves into? I don't know, and I'd rather not paint that picture today. What I can say is why the mass matters => whatever one might later want to do with a brain costs energy in proportion to its mass. Moving 1.4 kg at a fifth of the speed of light takes what a large power plant puts out in a month, and moving one gram takes half an hour of it (which is why Breakthrough Starshot plans with a few grams for the whole craft).

All of this was framed in terms of how small. One can also frame it in terms of how far, which is an idea that I discussed with a friend online recently => a third scale next to Kardashev's and Barrow's (I'm unsure if "axis" is the right word), for the scale at which death gets solved. On day three I said that you can solve death and death can still occur in the universe, so there's a local and a global version. I used to say "solve death in the universe", but this is really hard, and it probably comes in steps => personal death first, then the death of the Earth, then of the solar system (e.g. distributed brains across it that are somehow still coupled into one process world-line, before any hivemind, which is tomorrow's requirement btw), and at some point the heat death of the universe (open physics, day three). The closest thing that I could find is Zoltan Galántai's scale, which ranks civilisations by the size of the disaster that they can't survive, from a local one up to a supernova next door ([centauri-dreams.org]). His is about civilisations that spread out, and mine starts with one conscious process that shouldn't end (the never ending 4D process world line). My guess is that the two classical scales meet here, as going down Barrow's (a smaller and lighter carrier) might be what allows going up this one!

So we started out from one particular worry, a replacement brain that fills a hall. Fortunately, bigger isn't inevitable => what a morphology does in time can be done over another length, tissue does it already, and how much space it takes depends on the carrier, with noise as the floor. What I don't know is which parts of the NCC are mass and which are radius. The real test is to show that in a material, so to replace a piece by a smaller one that keeps the delays and see if the function stays (and then the engram, which is harder). Afaik I'm the first one to think about this in the context of gradual brain replacement, and if I'm not, please write me!
