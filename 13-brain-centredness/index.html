Are You Just Your Brain?

Daniel Burger·24 November 2026·2,523 words

TL;DR

Today is the first axiomatic requirement in the Ontology, called "brain-centredness", a "Sine qua non" kind of thinking: no "you" without your brain. Turned around (contrapositive), if I destroy your brain, you're gone, so that nobody can dispute it, and I'm not claiming that the brain alone is enough.

Today is the first axiomatic requirement in the Ontology, called "brain-centredness". With yesterday the four operational requirements are done (days nine to twelve), so quickly why there's a second kind and why "axiomatic" is the right term for it. The operational ones are things that a synthetic brain has to be able to DO => be made of something else, migrate, change its shape, be spread out. Each can be tested by trying. The axiomatic ones are what I have to assume before any of the trying starts. An axiom can't be derived from inside the system that's built on top of it. You can only pick it, and then you'd better pick it so weak that hardly anybody disagrees with it in practice (the Ontology notes that all of them are "weak by nature"). There are four => brain-centredness, methodological physicalism, non-organicism and scale separation. Days nine to twelve all did something to "the brain" (replace it, move it, reshape it, stretch it), and I never said why the brain is the thing to do all of this to, which is today's question.

(One note on where the requirements sit, bc technically they aren't part of the mission (hybrid mind uploading), and neither are the goals and the outcomes. In the Ontology all of them hang one level below the vision, "synthetic consciousness", next to the mission. So if somebody came along with another way than hybrid mind uploading, it'd have to meet the very same requirements. It's also why all the meditations so far lead towards the same thing, the vision, whatever the single day was about).

Now, let's talk about today's topic. As you've seen so far in the daily meditations challenge, I basically nearly exclusively talk about the brain. So one might ask, as rightfully many people do when I talk about my ideas and our progress at my two ventures: "Are you actually just the brain? What about your body?" Some even, prob if they're more well-read in those topics, ask: "What about your environment?"

The body first. I know that there's a great body of evidence (no pun intended) that the body is quite relevant to brain function (the gut-brain axis is the usual example). And it's relevant from a plain physiological point of view as well. In our functional brain isolation work at @eightsixscience, when going for a long period of many years and decades, we cannot not have a liver alive next to the isolated brain (next to a bunch of other things, no spoilers!).

There's even some evidence for literal memories, so engrams, in other tissues of the body outside of the brain. The best-known study is from Michael Levin's lab => flatworms were trained, got their heads cut off, grew new heads with new brains, and still showed traces of the training ([doi.org]). You could reason from this that memory sits in the whole body. But that would be guessing. A flatworm regrows its entire brain anyway, remembering only a simple familiarity with its dish (which only reappeared after a refresher session), and the headless rest of the worm still has nerve cords running through it. I've never seen anything like it in a mammal for the type of memory that constitutes a life.

The more serious version of the body question is concerned with what kind of thing a brain truly is. Most people look at it as a machine (esp. classical mind uploaders/AI people). Romain Brette's book "The Brain, In Theory" argues that a machine is engineered, so built by somebody with a plan to solve a problem, and that a living organism never was, so the brain is no "biological computer" that just happens to be made of cells ([press.princeton.edu]). Looking at the brain like this, we see that the worry about the body is indeed true to a certain degree => the brain is not simply an input and output device (as, again, most classical mind uploaders assume (and also a great deal of biocomputation people)).

And then the environment. In the extended mind hypothesis of Andy Clark and David Chalmers, Otto has Alzheimer's and keeps everything in a notebook, and the claim is that this notebook is a part of his mind in the same way that memory is a part of yours ([doi.org]). Whether you buy it or not, look at what happens to the word => if the notebook counts, then so does a phone, and so does a spouse who remembers all the birthdays, and the definition of what a mind is slowly breaks down and becomes blurry (it funnily enough gravitates towards a hivemind definition of a mind, as there is probably no single human living independently of other "minds" (humans and animals)).

I think that it gets blurry bc of how we use the word. We say that something has a mind because of what it does with something else, so it knows things and it acts on things. Then a mind that's hidden from anyone and everything is, per definition (abstraction fallacy style), not a mind, as there's nothing to act on (no symbols to attach on top of a noisy physical thing). That's fine as a definition. However, a definition tells you nothing about what is really happening "in there". Also, whether such a hidden thing still experiences something is a different question. It's the one I care about (so consciousness, esp. unified phenomenality, see yesterday).

Here's a thought experiment of mine. Take the brain of a human embryo and let it grow up in vitro, in a fully isolated state, and let it develop perfectly (assuming that we'd know how to do that, and I know that real development leans on a lot of activity). There's no sensory input, ever, and no output that does anything causally outside of the brain. What's in there? Most likely, I think, pure experience (i.e. phenomenality). This brain doesn't know anything, as there's nothing to know, and it can't act, as there's nothing to act on (it also cannot feel anything, as the brain does not feel itself). By the definition from above it has no mind, and I still wouldn't bet that nobody is home (an enactivist would bet exactly that, and I can't prove him wrong from the armchair).

This might even undermine René Descartes a bit. His meditator doubts the whole world away until he is left with one thing that he cannot doubt, "I think". But he does all of this doubting in the Latin language, with an "I" and a "think" that he learned in the very world he's just doubted away. If there really had never been a world, then the meditator would be my isolated brain. It has no way of doubting as well as no word for "I" => so it never gets as far as knowing that it exists. Possibly the cogito is true for whoever can say it, but it is for sure not the point at which knowledge begins. If experience is in there, any certainty about it would come afterwards (I'm simplifying; there's an entire trail of discussion densely packed into this sentence. I'm sorry...).

(PS: I've looked around whether somebody had this before, and found two that come close. Avicenna's floating man, a thousand years ago, is created in mid-air with no sensation at all and is still aware of himself, says Avicenna ([en.wikipedia.org]). But he's created fully formed, as an adult, and my brain never got the chance to form anything. And in 2020 Tim Bayne, Anil Seth and Marcello Massimini asked whether there are "islands of awareness", so experience in a brain (or a piece of one) that's cut off from every input and has no way of expressing anything, e.g. in isolated brains or in organoids, which never had an input or an output to begin with ([doi.org]). What I haven't found is the limit of it (i.e. a whole human brain that develops normally/as naturally as possible and was never connected to anything) and what this does to the word "mind". If you know of something, please write me!!)

So what I'm more interested in is not the mind per se, as this is indeed a blurry definition (and hence "mind uploading", even the hybrid kind, is an even worse term, as what we want to upload is, to be precise, not the mind per se but the unified phenomenality at the core of a mind). Now, talking about cores. How I usually argue for brain-centredness is to be very pragmatic and least metaphysical to begin with (the Synconetics view): If I stab your brain with a huge knife, you are gone. Everyone agrees on that, even somebody who believes in a soul would say that it has left. And even if I do this and then keep the rest of your body alive, and all your notebooks (extended mind hypothesis hint!), the "core object" of your phenomenality is gone. This is no thought experiment btw. Most countries refer to it as brain death, and such a body can be kept on a ventilator for days in order for its organs to be donated.

The other direction is equally mundane. People can lose an arm or both legs. After a complete injury high up in the spinal cord, the brain neither feels nor moves most of the body any more. None of them wakes up as somebody else. And twins who share most of one body and have two heads are two people for everybody who meets them.

So, whatever that means in the end, if we take this pragmatic view, then we agree that there must be one localised and bounded thing that we all ascribe the YOU to, in the sense that when we destroy it, the you is gone. Apparently that's the brain. We cannot keep the rest of your body alive with e.g. machine perfusion, totally destroy your brain, and expect that you'll ever be you again.

I want to be careful about what this shows. It shows that the brain is necessary => no brain, no you, whatever else is kept. I'm not claiming that the brain alone is enough, and I'm not claiming anything about what happens to you when the brain itself gets changed. Yes, one can come up with thought experiments here, such as "what if I just delete all your memories by slowly perturbing your brain?" There's not much that I can do with this sort of question. It assumes a brain that's built like a computer, where the memory can be wiped while the rest keeps running (so the machine picture from above, while as far as we know most of what you've learned sits in the same connections that do the perceiving). And it suggests a dichotomy: EITHER what comes out is still you, OR it's somebody else in your skull. But which measurement would come out differently under EITHER than under OR? Where something like it really happens (severe amnesia, dementia), nobody doubts that somebody is still experiencing, and whether it's "still him" is something that relatives say both ways. That's what I mean when I call it an ascientific question. The word "you" does its job in all of the ordinary cases, and then it gets carried into a case where nothing settles how to use it (Wittgenstein btw has this in the Blue Book for the word "person"). So for an axiom I leave these questions out.

Now, if that's true, then there must be at least one thing on the spectrum of the brain-centredness question, which is, tada, the brain itself. How much of this is JUST your brain, and how much is sensory input or neuromodulator regulation, we don't know. But what we know for sure is that the brain, even if it's a gradient of how sure we are about it, is the core to be preserved.

It also seems that the brain is the one thing that gives rise to phenomenal consciousness. The short version is that whatever switches experience off/on does so via the brain. Anaesthetics act there and stimulating the cortex directly results in experiences that no sense organ delivered. Every nite, you dream a whole world while the brain is mostly cut off from the senses and muscles (and the main argument between current theories of consciousness is WHERE in the brain).

The best counterargument that I know takes the vat seriously as an engineering problem (so no thought experiment ad absurdum). Evan Thompson and Diego Cosmelli went through what a vat would really need in order to keep a brain going, and concluded that any vat that works would be a surrogate body, so that a living body belongs to the minimal requirements for consciousness ([evanthompson.me]). I agree with the first half, I've just said the same about the liver. But a surrogate is a replacement, that's the whole point of one. Every part of that vat can be exchanged for another part that does the same job, and you're still there (for the heart and the kidneys that's hospital routine by now). The brain is the one part where this doesn't work, at least not in one go, and whether it works bit by bit is what the whole mission is about (hence brain-centred and not brain-only).

So even at worst, if there is indeed some specific kind of, idk, "electromagnetic wave produced by your entire body that gives rise to phenomenal consciousness", we would basically figure out how strong or weak brain-centredness really is by working on brain isolation in the coming months and years. We can actually test this empirically => on a mind that reports, and also by inferring it without a report, even in animals.

So to simply summarise today: The requirement is axiomatic bc of the inverse case, destroying someone's brain, which nobody doubts. And for everything beyond that we have a way to find out where on the spectrum we are. The easiest would be JUST the brain (but even then e.g. intracranial pressure is important for cognition, so we cannot simply remove the skull but need to replace it, even from a "bio brain in a vat"-perspective), etc etc. It's about having an empirical approach to figure this out. The best case is the brain fully on its own, the worst is that the entire body is needed, and then the question becomes how weak or strong this influence is, and how we can replace the body as well in a mechanistic, gradual way (at worst, hybrid mind uploading might be the mechanistic replacement of the entire body!).

I cannot say more about our functional brain isolation programme at Eightsix, as most of the things are quite stealth at this point... The only thing that I can say is that we will figure it out in the coming months, that brain-centredness has a hard limit where it must be true for sure, and that this all will make WAY more sense in the coming months and years! :-)
