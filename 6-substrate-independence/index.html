Computational Vs. Physical Substrate Independence

Daniel Burger·17 November 2026·2,330 words

TL;DR

Substrate independence, the first outcome, is physical, not computational: a mind depends on what its carrier physically does, so whatever does the same will do, biological or not. What has to be re-created in another material are the brain's constraints, independent of biology but never of physics.

Originally posted on XReplies and discussion happen there.Open on X →

Over the past three days, we discussed the goals of my Ontology. Today we start with the first of the three outcomes, which also sit right below the mission called hybrid mind uploading. It's called "substrate independence". What does this term mean in this context? In the Ontology, it's listed as a state, and the one line under it says: mind no longer tied to biological substrate. There's a widespread reading of these two words in which the substrate simply doesn't matter. That's no good frame of mind for somebody who wants to build one, and it's not what I mean. The trouble sits in "independence", as the word asks a question that it doesn't answer (the same way "transcending" did yesterday with its "beyond what?") => independent of what? And dependent on what instead?

On day four I said that a goal is something that we strive for and could fail at, and that an outcome is what follows either way, as long as we work on the mission ([Nov 2026]). We don't strive for substrate independence for its own sake. Each time a piece of brain gets swapped for an engineered one while the process keeps running, there's a bit less of the tissue that it started in. So it comes along with the work, and that's what the "either way" was about. It was never meant as a guarantee tho, as how far this can go is decided by physics and not by us. The three outcomes btw pair up with the three goals, one each. Solving death the way that this mission goes about it, so by (mechanistic) gradual replacement, brings a carrier that's no longer the biology that we were born with, which is substrate independence. Understanding consciousness brings augmented cognition, because what we can measure and model, we can at some point also steer and modify. And transcending humanity has the collective mind as its outcome, a term that I'm (as well) not happy with, and by far the weirdest of the three (but that's for its own day!).

So why substrate independence? I'll go through it in the order in which I went through it myself. The idea is older than the term. In 1950, Turing remarked that Babbage's Analytical Engine was meant to be purely mechanical, and that the fact that both our computers and our nerves work electrically therefore "cannot be of theoretical importance". Ten years later, Hilary Putnam figured out how to carry it over into the philosophy of mind (which is btw where functionalism starts). In "Minds and Machines" (1960) he compares mental states with the logical states of a Turing machine, and in "Psychological Predicates" (1967) he argues that pain can't be one particular state of the brain, as an octopus presumably feels pain with a brain that's built very differently, so pain has to be a functional state that many kinds of matter can realise. That's multiple realisability. And in 2003, Nick Bostrom wrote "substrate-independence" into his simulation argument. I'm not sure who used the term first, but he doesn't present it as his own, as he introduces it as "a common assumption in the philosophy of mind". Btw: What I find interesting is how much of what's relevant for my kind of work runs through Putnam => functionalism, multiple realisability, with them substrate independence, and even the brain in a vat (i.e. our functional brain isolation work at @eightsixscience). And I used the term in exactly this sense for years myself, e.g. in 2023, when I called one of my early aims "substrate-independent cognitive preservation" ([Nov 2023]).

That went on until this May, when I wrote down the founding commitments of classical mind uploading and "substrate independence as a working assumption" was one of them. That was the moment to finally put my name under it... and I couldn't go through with it. Whether because of Lerchner's paper on the Abstraction Fallacy ([deepmind.google]), the weeks of arguing about it afterwards ([Apr 2026]), or a sudden second-guessing of how calculating a process is supposed to be(!) that process, I filed it under the commitments that fix the answers in advance, and ended years' worth of using it ([May 2026]). Putnam, of all people, had been there looong before me. In 1988, he showed how to get any finite automaton out of pretty much any ordinary open system (it's in the appendix of "Representation and Reality"), which allows for a rock that runs whatever program you like. So if I take a lens from Putnam, then it's the one that he ended with.

Now, here I am again, with the same two words as the first outcome of my own Ontology. "They" always say that a mind goes wherever its computation goes, and the other "they" say that it only ever lives in living tissue (which is the only place where we've found one so far) => so I guess it's on me to say that nobody has shown either! Yeah, I know that this looks like I'm taking the assumption back in, but I'm only keeping its two words. And, tbh, as something that one arrives at (or doesn't), I like them a lot better than as something that one starts from. My take, however, is that I don't look at substrate independence through a functionalist lens, in the sense of having already subscribed to a theory of consciousness such as neurocomputational functionalism. What I start from is an observation => if we all have somewhat the same kind of experience across humans, then there must be some sort of multiple realisability, in the sense that we all do somewhat the same even tho our brains are all different. Sometimes there's a slow-growing tumour sitting right in somebody's speech area (i.e. a low-grade glioma), and it takes the brain a few years to "move" the speech area somewhere else, so that a surgeon can later cut where no textbook would let him (I'm simplifying the gradual reorganisation principle and serial lesion effect here btw, as this is also a topic for another day's meditation). Either way, I still find that rather wild. Of course, there's only so much that this can show, as it's living neural tissue taking over from living neural tissue. So the process doesn't seem to care which cells carry it. However, the substrate class is kind of the same in all of these cases, and that's the important question => does the process also not care what kind of thing carries it? I also have to correct myself. In June I wrote that because of the gliomas, bio-to-bio multiple realisability "must exist" in the biological brain, and I put a "hence substrate independence" in brackets behind it ([Jun 2026]). I'd still sign the part before the bracket. The bracket itself, not so...

Gualtiero Piccinini (a philosopher of computation) has this "mousetrap example" that I keep coming back to; he points out that while you can build a mousetrap in a variety of ways (in his terms this is what he calls multiple realisability), it still has to catch actual mice, while a computation doesn't "care" at all what its vehicles are made of – as long as they have the right degrees of freedom, which is what he calls "medium independence". Classical mind uploading turned out to need the second one for minds, and I found myself needing at most the first. The first one sits in every heart-lung machine btw, where the "lung" is a bundle of plastic fibres with blood on one side and gas on the other. There's no lung cell anywhere, and the blood comes out oxygenated anyway! Supposedly, nobody has managed that with a simulation of a lung yet (btw this work with artificial lungs vs what can be simulated as an input to a brain is again full-circle back to our work on functional brain isolation (= brains in vats)). What I'm wondering is if one can have the physics of the brain without the biology, or as I put it in September: a mechanistic piece of brain that does the same kind of physics as the real biological brain, and at the same time is computable in the sense that we can steer it ([Sep 2026]).

So in that modest sense, substrate independence already exists, as nature does it from tissue to tissue all the time. The question is if the thing can work without biology, and that's really two questions. Does physics allow it, so can any material other than living cells do the job? It's a question for experiments, and it has an awkward asymmetry => a single working case answers it with yes, whereas no number of failed attempts adds up to a no, as for that we'd have to understand why. And if physics allows it, what does it cost? The way that I think about this one, in terms of materials science: if the engineering effort is a scale with "running a SaaS company" on the left and "CERN and the LHC" on the right, where on that scale is showing that substrate independence is economically feasible, given the engineering complexity and efforts needed? I don't know. It can also come out as a yes from physics and a no from the economics, and then hybrid mind uploading becomes extreme biology instead (I've said so before: [Oct 2026]).

There's a kind of substrate independence in all of this that I think is the only one which can be an outcome, and I didn't have a word for it. So I'm putting a word in front that says what has to be kept. Computational substrate independence is what Bostrom and classical mind uploading mean => a mind depends only on the computation, so whatever runs it will do. Physical substrate independence is what I mean => a mind depends on what its carrier physically does, so whatever does the same will do, be it biological or not. And the other half has to be said out loud, as, in my view, a mind depends on its substrate a great deal => what it's supposed to become independent of is biology, and physics it never leaves. The physical version claims less than the computational one btw, as a material that does the same physics also runs the same computation along the way, but not the other way round.

There's a spectrum in this, and it's about how many materials can carry a mind. First, there's the tissue that you were born with, and nothing else will do. Then other living neural tissue will do as well, which is as far as the gliomas go. Then engineered living tissue, then at least one material that isn't alive (the candidate that I've been writing about is the alien neuron: [Sep 2026]), then many. And it could turn out that the class ends at living tissue. Then we'd have gone full circle => we set out to leave biology, and we arrive at building it (and then go towards maybe extreme biology by merging with genetically engineered fungi or something?).

That's basically the outcome => if we engineer this, then we'll have substrate independence, in the physical sense. A copy on another substrate shows nothing, as a copy would tell us that everything is fine in any case (day two). But if one takes a piece of a living brain and doesn't copy it, and instead lets an engineered piece take over its job while the rest keeps running, then this can show both that the piece does the job (because the experience is the same before, during and after the step, day four) and that it's still the same someone (again, because the process never stopped, if the hypothesis from day four holds). Careful tho: this is NOT Moravec's procedure, and it's not the setup of Chalmers's fading qualia either. Read "Mind Children" (1988) and pay close attention => the robot surgeon there, ostensibly doing the very same thing piece by piece, actually swaps each layer of the brain for a simulation of it, and Chalmers's silicon chips are defined as doing what a neuron does at its inputs and outputs. The actual difference, in both of these cases, is that what the brain does has already been coarse-grained (down to e.g. action potentials) before the first piece gets replaced, and for me that grain is the open part ([Jul 2026]).

Which leaves the question of what exactly has to be re-created. Earlier this year I started reading Romain Brette's "The Brain, in Theory" (on Lerchner's recommendation: [Sep 2026]), and its main point is one that an engineer doesn't like to hear: living organisms aren't engineered. So there's no plan of the brain that I could copy into another material, as nobody ever drew one. The brain is ultimately a physical thing, dynamical, organised and bounded, that goes forward in time (it's the carrier, and the process world-line is the activity that runs through it). So, however that activity is constrained by physics, originally via neuronal cells, the bet is that it's somehow engineerable with the same constraints. Neurons keep charges apart with membranes and let ions through them at some voltages and not at others, and all the while their chemistry rebuilds the very structure that the activity runs on. These are constraints, and I think that the brain is all about constraints. Not all of them sit inside the skull, btw, as a brain is always in a loop with something that it senses and acts on. Of course, it's hard to know where this list stops, and it's just as hard to know at which grain it starts (that's the lattice question that I've owed since day one: [Nov 2026]). So what has to be re-created in another material are these constraints, and the brain's parts only as far as they're needed for them. That's the quest, and THIS re-creation of the constraints is what I actually mean by substrate independence!
