Weak and Strong Multiple Realisability

Daniel Burger·20 November 2026·3,343 words

TL;DR

Multiple realisability, the first requirement of my Ontology, comes in a weak form, where engrams get shifted from living tissue to living tissue inside one brain, and a strong form, where de novo tissue, maybe not even biological, can also store and implement the same functions.

Originally posted on XReplies and discussion happen there.Open on X →

The first requirement (type "Requirement") of my Ontology is called "multiple realisability" (the three outcomes are done as of yesterday). A requirement is something that has to be true about brains for hybrid mind uploading to have a chance at all for it to work/to make synthetic consciousness possible as a vision. Fair warning: today is more technical than the last few days (less of a meditation tbh).

The Ontology has two kinds of requirements, operational and axiomatic, four of each. The operational ones (multiple realisability, continuer migration, morphological independence, distributed unification) are things that a brain turns out to be able to do or not, so an experiment can come back with a no, and they come in degrees, hence a weak and a strong form for each (strong preferred). Then there are four that no experiment of mine will ever come back on (brain-centredness, methodological physicalism, non-organicism, scale separation), as I have to grant them before any of those experiments means anything, so I take each in its least committal form. There's btw a certain connection between the operational and axiomatic items in the Ontology => non-organicism only says that I don't rule out a non-living carrier in advance. Multiple realisability on the other hand asks if any such carrier actually works at all. I start with it as the other three operational ones are built on top of it (there's no migrating to a carrier that can't carry "you").

On day six I told the first half of the history (Putnam's octopus in 1967 => pain can't be one particular brain state if a mollusc has it with a very different brain), so here's the second half, which seems to be less known among mind uploading people. Putnam's octopus was an argument against the identity theory of Place and Smart, and it was about kinds across species. Block and Fodor added further arguments in 1972, and one of them is the reason for today's meditation => inside ONE brain, the same psychological function can be served by different neural structures at different times, as people do recover after brain damage. Fodor built the autonomy of psychology on it in 1974, and for about twenty-five years multiple realisability was treated as (more or less) obvious. In parts of the BCI world it still is, which I've wondered about before ([Apr 2026]). Then came the pushback: Bechtel and Mundale noticed in 1999 that it only looks obvious if one compares coarse psychological kinds with fine-grained neural ones (and how could neuroscientists otherwise identify "the same" brain area across species all the time?). A year later Shapiro asked what should even count as "different", via an object of great importance: the corkscrew. You have a steel one and an aluminium one. Two realisations of a corkscrew? No! The material makes no difference to how the cork comes out (the wine doesn't care). Polger and Shapiro then made a recipe out of this in The Multiple Realization Book (2016 => amazing book btw, highly recommend it to anyone wanting to work in the (hybrid) mind uploading space). Two things realise a kind multiply only if they do the same job in relevantly different ways. Most of the famous examples don't, and neural plasticity is one of them.

Whatever you think of this debate, all of it is about taxonomy. Do the kinds of psychology line up with the kinds of neuroscience or not? That's (one way of saying) why so little of it carries over to engineering. My own question is much narrower, it sits with one particular mind over time => can THIS mind come to be carried by something other than the tissue that it's "on" right now, without ending? Putnam's octopus isn't sufficient for a yes (pain may well exist in two kinds of brain, and still no path follows from my brain to the other one), and tbh it isn't even necessary, as replacing neurons with neurons would still be on the table if only neurons could ever do the job. (Polger and Shapiro point out that multiple realisation is what nature has actually done and multiple realisability what's possible. The mission only needs the second one, for a single case).

Remember the corkscrew? By their recipe, the best case for hybrid mind uploading wouldn't count as multiple realisation at all! If a replaced piece of brain does the same job in the same way as the piece that it replaced, then that's the steel one next to the aluminium one. They say the same about plasticity btw (insofar as the tissue that takes over does the same thing, it does it the same way). Which is great news for an engineer, as "does it the same way" is what I asked for on day six (a mind depends on what its carrier physically does, so whatever does the same will do). So what I'm doing is to keep the name (in the old and loose sense of Putnam, Block and Fodor (more than one carrier, whatever the taxonomists make of it)). Is it odd to use a term that was invented to defend functionalism while computational functionalism sits on my own list of rejections? A bit, lol, but e.g. Piccinini's mousetrap (from day six of the meditation's challenge) can be built in a multitude of ways and yet still has to catch actual mice – so I don't see a contradiction. (I replied to their book in August btw: [Aug 2026])

Now to the data: If you take a piece of brain out in one go, the animal loses what that piece did, and if you take the very same piece out in two sittings with enough time in between, it often loses little or nothing. That's called the serial lesion effect. David Barrett made the philosophical argument out of it in 2013 ([doi.org]), with what he calls the gradual reorganisation principle => given enough time, the brain reorganises itself so that a psychological capacity survives the degeneration of the tissue that carried it. His showcase is a rat study from 1975 (Patrissi and Stein) in which the frontal cortex was removed on both sides either at once/or one side after the other with either 10, 20 or 30 days in between. With 20 or 30 days, the rats couldn't be told apart from the sham-operated ones on a spatial alternation task. With 10 days they were clearly worse and the ones that lost everything at once needed more than three times as long as the shams! So there's the same capacity with a frontal cortex and without one, and as the one-stage rats show that the frontal cortex was carrying it, something else must carry it afterwards. That's one individual with two carriers at two points in time, so Block and Fodor's plasticity argument with an experiment behind it.

The human version of the two-stage rat is the low-grade glioma from day six. These tumours grow by ≈4 mm a year for seven or eight years before they turn malignant (Desmurget, Bonnetblanc and Duffau 2007: [doi.org]), and the comparison that Duffau keeps coming back to is the stroke in the same location => after a stroke around 25% of patients recover completely, whereas more than 90% of the glioma patients have a normal neurological examination with the tumour sitting right there ([doi.org]).

So here's my idea of a weak and a strong form: If the serial lesion effect works, and with slowly growing lesions such as low-grade gliomas it evidently works to some degree, then there must be at least weak multiple realisability => whatever engram gets shifted around from living tissue to living tissue inside one brain is an empirical proof of it (I've been calling it that since the summer: [Aug 2026]). The strong form is if de novo tissue, so tissue that was never part of that brain (and maybe isn't even biological at all => mechanistic!) can also store and implement the same functions/engrams. If only the living kind of de novo tissue can, then hybrid mind uploading indeed has to go towards extreme biology, which I've discussed before ([Oct 2026]). And there's one more rung below that which I should name => if not even freshly made living tissue could take anything over, then there'd be no uploading of any kind, and what's left is keeping the tissue that you were born with alive for as long as possible, which stops dying and doesn't solve death (day three).

The question is now, if you look at Barrett, Duffau and Desmurget and accept that it works to a certain degree => how fast, where, and how exactly?

How fast first: There's some science on this; in rats, ten days is too fast for a frontal cortex and twenty is enough, and in people with gliomas, ≈4 mm a year is slow enough for speech and movement. (Insert the usual disclaimer about two data points, one of which is a rat). In between there's apparently nothing; this is quite annoying, actually, since the whole principle hangs on the word "gradual". The clinic does have a word for the variable tho (lesion momentum), and tumour genetics provides the natural experiment: Malignant gliomas without the IDH1 mutation grow more aggressively than those with it, and before any surgery their carriers do worse on cognitive tests. The size of the lesion only tracks the deficit in the fast group btw (Wefel et al. 2016: [doi.org]). If I were to plan a gradual brain replacement procedure tomorrow, I would want this as an actual measurement, so that my speed limit was more accurate than "slower than a stroke".

Where next: Duffau's rule after his career of a bunch of awake surgeries is that cortex is forgiving and white matter isn't. A lot of cortex can go, as long as the tracts connecting what's left stay intact (the arcuate fascicle stays off limits even for these slow tumours). His group even made atlases out of this. The first one came with the idea of a "minimal common brain" that nobody can do without, and the latest is a probabilistic atlas of functional plasticity from 231 patients, high in the cortex and low in the connective tracts (Herbet et al. 2016: [doi.org]). So a static version of the atlas that I'm after exists already! It says where tissue can be taken without a lasting deficit. What it doesn't have is: time (which momentum does each region tolerate?) or direction (where did the function go from there?) – and it's also silent on what kind of "thing" it was that made it move.

So, lastly, the direction: This is the embarrassing one as Barrett himself says about his rats that it's "anyone's guess" which regions took over. In patients one can at least look => functional imaging shows activity around the tumour, further away in the same hemisphere, or on the other side. But a spot that lights up isn't necessarily needed, and to find that out you have to stimulate it. So the only direct human data that I know of are repeat mappings, when a patient gets operated a second time years later and the surgeon stimulates the same spots of cortex again. In the UCSF series (Southwell et al. 2016: [doi.org]), 18 of 561 patients had such a second mapping, on average four years after the first, and of the 22 sites that had been positive for language, movement or sensation, 9 had gone silent, with no deficit in the patients to show for it (Duffau reports the same, it's what lets him cut further the second time). Guess how many of the 95 sites that had been silent the first time had become positive? One. So we can watch a function leave, and so far nobody has watched it arrive (only the exposed cortex gets retested obv, so it might have arrived out of sight, or have spread out so thinly that no single spot is needed any more).

My guess for the route, and it's only a guess, is next door. Afaik neighbouring gyri are connected by short U-shaped fibres right underneath the cortex, and as these tumours travel along white matter, whether a function can move over to the next gyrus might depend on whether the tumour has already infiltrated the border between grey and white matter there. That would make the U-fibres the roads on this map. So there must be mechanics to this, how well it works where, how fast and along which roads, and they should add up to some sort of atlas of the gradual reorganisation principle. I think that a first version can be made from data that exists already, as the big glioma centres should all have patients who were mapped twice, and where it was never written up in a study, then from anecdotes. Now I do understand why "anecdotes" sounds bad from a clinical research perspective. But imagine that satellites didn't exist and you wanted a functional map of the world. You'd get surprisingly far by asking a bunch of people who've travelled it a lot => "don't go through the northern Baltic in winter, it's frozen, take this route". There's no coastline in that sentence. If anything, that's the point, as your ship gets there anyway. This has been done btw. After going through thousands of old logbooks that were sitting in a navy storeroom, Matthew Maury came back with his first wind and current chart in 1847, and the captains who sailed by his charts got there faster. The same might be true about an anecdotal dataset from glioma cases, to create this atlas of cortical reorganisation. It makes sense to think that surgeons get a grasp of which area empties within how many years (and which one never does) long before anybody understands the mechanism.

The single most useful thing that we'd get from those mechanics is a list of what a piece of tissue needs in order to be a destination, which is worth more than knowing how well multiple realisability works in the one example that nature provides. Going by these papers, a destination is a fairly well defined thing. My candidates include the correct inputs (the tracts again), the normal Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity of any piece of cortex, a short path to where the function was, and activity while moving. What strikes me the most about that list is that nothing on it mentions "alive" (at least by name). They are all physical actions that a carrier performs, so they can be asked of tissue grown in a dish and grafted in, as well as a carrier that was never alive (the alien neurone from day six of the meditations challenge). There is already an example of grafted living tissue: embryonic neurons were transplanted into lesioned adult visual cortex of mice that received the inputs that such cells normally receive and within a few weeks responded to visual stimuli with the same selectivity as their neighbours (Falkner et al. 2016: [nature.com]). So I guess that's the arrival that the glioma data is never showing, and it happened in place, where the wiring already was. With such a list, it would most likely be possible to get an idea in a materials lab of whether the brain is weakly or strongly multiply realisable => substrate class by substrate class.

Which brings me to what I call the grandmother's face problem. With glioma cases, things usually only become an issue once the tumour turns into a fast-growing one, as then the reorganisation can't keep up any more and we get an effect that's measurable from the outside (slurred speech, impaired motor function, etc.). Until then most of them are found because of a seizure, and not because something stopped working. But what about all the information and the engrams that you aren't aware of having until you recall them? What if those get destroyed while the glioma grows, such as the rarely retrieved memory of your grandmother's face? I can't do a no-recall study. I can't say "please recall all the things that you forgot". And before engineering something like a glioma growth for the sake of gradual replacement, I can't say "please let me know which memories you have encoded in area xyz of your brain" either.

The clinic's reassurance doesn't reach that far as "no deficit" means a normal neurological examination + whatever can be tested within a few seconds on the operating table (naming a picture, counting, moving a hand, etc.) and as soon as these patients get an extensive neuropsychological assessment/disorders of attention/working memory and executive functions do show up (Klein et al. 2012, cited in Duffau's paper above). Motor function and speech work well, as reported by Duffau and Desmurget, and I think that they work well because they're retrieved all day long. As far as I can tell from Barrett's description, his showcase rats learned their task after the operations, so what was spared there is a capacity to learn, and there was no engram from before that could have moved. The studies that tested a habit prior to the operations are older and less well-known, and in one of them (Petrinovich and Carew 1969: [doi.org]), a learned brightness discrimination survived the two-stage removal of the posterior cortex, but only with the larger lesions if the rats practised that habit between the two operations.

If use is what drives the move (the rats point that way, and so does the model of Keidel, Welbourne and Lambon Ralph (2010: [doi.org]), which gets the difference between glioma and stroke out of slow damage plus ongoing relearning), then "gradual" means nothing in millimetres per year alone. A tumour that requires a few years to grow through a gyrus means speech has many thousands of rehearsals to move with and the grandmother's face's maybe just a handful. So one and the same lesion can be a low-grade glioma for e.g. your speech and actually a stroke for your grandmother's face's engram.

And it's a measurement problem of a nasty kind, similar (in its logic only!) to the position and velocity question in quantum mechanics, which is basically a new way of doing epistemology: The only test for an engram is to retrieve it, and retrieving is an intervention => it rehearses the trace, which on the account above is the very thing that saves it, and it opens the trace up to change (in rats, consolidated fear memories go back into a labile state when they're reactivated, Nader et al. 2000: [nature.com]). So whatever memory I measure ahead of time is no longer an unrehearsed memory and for the ones that I don't measure there is no baseline. Then the patient is no help either, because forgetting doesn't feel like anything as you'd have to have the memory to miss it. They would obv. say that they're fine either way, which is day seven's problem again...

So do we even know if the gradual reorganisation principle applies to anything else but capacities in constant use? And even if weak multiple realisability is true, do useful engrams get shifted at all when the ordinary brain is forced to?

This adds a second axis to the weak and strong views, namely "what type of information" moves around. So, where does that leave them?
– Capacities from bio to bio => shown, but with a speed limit that nobody has measured yet
– Engrams from bio to bio => shown in rats that practised
– Capacities into a new tissue => one result in mice afaik
– Engrams into a new tissue => nothing afaik (on day six I still signed "must exist" for the bio-to-bio kind, and after today I'd sign it for capacities and put a question mark behind it for engrams)

There are ways to figure this out and/or work around it, but today's meditation is already too long lol, and this is part of our research roadmap at the Synconetics Org (@synconeticsorg / [synconetics.org]) – happy to discuss further!
