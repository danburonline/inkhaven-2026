Solving Death

14 November 2026·3,538 words

Originally posted on XReplies and discussion happen there.Open on X →

I run a company called @eightsixscience, where we try to functionally isolate the brain ([Sep 2026]); in other words, we take living brains out of their bodies and keep them alive outside their original bodies for as long as possible. We have early success in doing so in rodents; we're working toward the same for a large-animal brain, and ultimately we want to do this for humans as well. When I tell people about this – friends, family, people I meet at scientific conferences, investors or also just random people I meet throughout life (e.g. my hairdresser, who I recently told what exactly I'm doing) – I usually get the one question that gets asked most: "Why are you doing this?" I simply answer: "Because I want to solve death, and functionally isolating brains is, according to my own reasoning, the most pragmatic route towards that goal". And then usually the question comes right after: "But, why? Why do you want to solve death?"

The short answer, before I ramble on for way too long: it's complicated, and no, I still don't have a clean one after ≈10 years of getting asked. I can tell you why death is bad for the one who dies; that part I'm fairly sure about. But why exactly me, why I can't put this thing down again? No idea, and it gets worse the longer I think about it – same as with "what has to be true along the way" as well as "when would we even call it solved"; because every time I answer one of those, I do actually end up with a new question instead: what even is a world-line, what makes a continuation worth having, and so on (which is btw exactly why solving death is one of the three hard goals that the mission has to achieve in the Ontology).

Depending on who asks, I've come to several answers over the years, depending on the person, the context, and the time available. It ranges from personal upbringing reasons, e.g. my mom going through the Yugoslav wars and telling me stories as a child about all the terrible things that she had seen (hence developing a morbid and catastrophic view on death early on), or that I was depressed as a teenager and prob read the wrong kinds of books in a row that led me, back then, towards thinking that death is actually the only answer (that phase is long over btw). Now, the thing is, and I have to be strict with myself here, that both of these stories answer a question that nobody asked. When somebody asks me "why do you want this?", they could mean "what made you want it?" or they could mean "what makes it worth wanting?". Those are actually two very different things => the first one is a cause and the second one a reason (and my mom's stories and my teenage bookshelf are e.g. obv causes). They tell you how I ended up with death as my main topic. But if the goal itself is any good: they don't tell you at all (and tbh, they also can't tell you that it's bad. People do try that one, and it's the genetic fallacy, just used in the other direction!). The usual comeback here is "so it's just your fear of death talking", and I think that one deserves an honest answer: yes, maybe, I even posted a quote once saying that one of the principal things that moves people is the terror of death ([Oct 2024]). But you can play the exact same game with the other side! For basically all of human history there was nothing, like really nothing, that anybody could do about it, so what else would you do other than making your peace with it (Elster calls this adaptive preferences, or just "sour grapes"). So fine, my wish can be explained away, but so can everybody else's acceptance, and then explaining away doesn't decide anything here.

Other avenues of explanation are usually more mythological, as in: death always seemed to be humanity's forbidden fruit and life in general; it's the one great barrier one shall not pass, and if you're curious, you obviously love to explore that boundary. Again, curiosity is a cause and no reason, but the "shall not pass" part deserves a closer look, as nearly every culture has a story that warns you about going there (e.g. Gilgamesh loses his plant of youth to a snake, and Tithonus gets his eternal life without the eternal youth). Even my own "forbidden fruit" picture fits btw, as in Genesis the forbidden fruit is strictly speaking knowledge, and it's the tree of life that gets guarded afterwards, so that nobody eats from it and lives forever. People sometimes hand me these stories as an argument, as in "humanity has always known that this is a bad idea". I read them as the wisdom of a time in which nothing could be done, so they belong to the same making-peace as above, and if you look closely, what most of them warn against is a bad version of not having to die (ageing forever, being alone forever), which nobody is asking for. The one reading that's left is the hubris one, that it's against the order of things, and "the order of things" is no argument by itself, as the night is part of that order as well, which brings me to the lightbulbs.

So, more societally as an explanation, one of the analogies I've recently been bringing up during conversations is the example of pitching the lightbulb in front of people working on candles in the early 1800s. Instead of accepting the limitations of candles for brightening up the night, which is what we've done for thousands of years as Homo sapiens, why not go beyond those limits by inventing magically glowing things called lightbulbs, whose light doesn't have to burn down the way a candle does? It's coming from a totally different angle to solve the same problem, which is: keep it bright for as long as you want (you see the analogies I'm drawing slowly creeping in!). When working on lightbulbs, you don't iterate on candles; you approach it with different things. And that's the beauty, because the upshot of solving "it being bright at night, against nature's reason to make it dark at night due to the earth blocking the sunlight" is that we ultimately needed to work on electricity, have constant access to electricity, DC then AC, and this led towards the electrification of a bunch of things, and ultimately also to the digital computer, and then AI, AGI and superintelligence (quite literally btw: the "Edison effect" was found inside a lightbulb in the early 1880s, Fleming turned it into the first vacuum tube in 1904, and the first electronic computers were rooms full of the descendants of that tube). Who would have thought that the people working on lightbulbs back in the day were working on this uphill technology with a tail that leads us towards superintelligence? I usually say that solving death kind of feels like this nowadays. It seems like, for the first time, we have enough of the technology and knowledge to start seriously, so let's try. The upshot of this is unknown, and nobody can predict it; we can only guess. A few people like Nikola Tesla predicted, quite accurately, some future scenarios back then, but I don't think this is possible anymore, and I don't know anything about what the future has in store for us when we solve death, other than that solving death is just step one and that life in a post-death society will be stranger than we can imagine ([Oct 2025]).

I used to end this pitch with "but whatever it is, it must be net positive, otherwise it will be filtered out either way" (basically "dialectic" in society), and I have to take that part back, as it doesn't survive my own scrutiny. Bad things can stick around for a very long time before a society filters them out, and some things do the filtering on us instead. So let me say what I think the analogy really gives me. It shows the kind of move, as the improvement path of the candle (better wax, better wicks) never arrives at the lightbulb, and the candle here is biology. You can make it burn longer, and I'm all for that, but, e.g. reversing ageing halts the dying and doesn't solve death, as you can still get hit by a bus ([Sep 2026]). It also shows that the upshots came through the infrastructure that the solution needed (electricity), which nobody working on it could foresee, and I expect the same here, as you can't work on this without understanding and engineering the thing that carries consciousness. And it takes care of the "order of things" from above, as nobody thinks today that the lightbulb was an offence against the night. Now, does any of this tell me that the result will be good? No, and no analogy ever could; I mean, it's lightbulbs. And there's one place where my nice analogy just falls apart, and it's kind of the most important place => continuity. If a lightbulb is off and you swap it for a new one, nobody cares; the light is simply "on again". With a consciousness, that's the whole difference between going on and being replaced (the 4D process world-line gets its own day). And what's left of my "it must be net positive" is weaker; I'm afraid => the direct effect is good if death is bad for the one who dies (next paragraph), and for everything that comes after that, I really don't know.

Now, to come back, I technically have even more examples, and they range from the personal "life is great, death is bad, so I want more of life" to the very logical reasons as described in one of the earlier meditations ([Nov 2026]), to the very visionary views such as the lightbulb scenario above. Out of these, the logical one is the one that carries the most weight for me (it's the central reason, if you want), so I'll go through it once more, slowly. It starts with the thing from day one => nothing matters except to someone conscious. Death is the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line, so when such a world-line ends, everything that would have come after just doesn't happen for the someone that this world-line is, and if that would have been worth having, then missing out on it is bad for that someone (that's Nagel's deprivation view; I didn't invent this). The "worth having" matters a lot btw, as it's the reason why the goal in the Ontology says "no one has to end" and why it never said, "no one is allowed to end". Also, yes, there are some well-known objections (e.g. Epicurus, Lucretius, "death gives life its meaning", "we have to make room", and so on, etc.), but I'll potentially talk about those things on another day, especially as today's meditation is getting way too long already...

OK, so that's the "why-is-death-bad"-part, and I think it holds up. But did you notice? I still haven't answered the question, as "why is death bad" and "why do YOU want to solve it" are just two different questions, and the second one is so much harder, which is kind of the main thing that I want to deliver today. A person can have every reason in their hands and yet still not know exactly why they actually want it. Arguments don't make people magically want things (on that part, Hume was right!). Plenty of people nod at every single step above and then go on with their lives as if nothing happened. So why do I want to solve death? Well, I don't know. Personal urge? I guess this is where the causes from the beginning come back in, as the reason is there for everybody, and my mom's stories and the wrong books are why I'm one of the people who can't put it down again. To put it simply: I will try doing so, otherwise I'll die either way, so who cares? That last part might sound like a joke, but I think there's actually a real argument hiding in it. Say nothing changes => I die, like everybody before me, that's the baseline. Say I try, and it doesn't work out => I die as well, so the same thing, except that there's whatever the attempt produced along the way. And if I try and it does work (for me, I mean), then that's obviously better, and if it only works for the people after me, then it's a gain for them and none for me, I'm aware of that. So for me, as far as I can see, trying is at least as good as not trying. There are two "buts" though. The attempt shouldn't cost me a life that I'd rather have lived (it doesn't – I love this work), and nothing worse than simply dying should be reachable along the way, and that second one doesn't come for free, nothing guarantees it, somebody has to design for it blabla. I know of two such outcomes on my own route btw. A conscious brain that's kept going without any input, without any way to act and without anything that protects it from suffering would be one of them, and I'd call that worse than death (more on that below). The other one is a "solution" that only produces a copy, or something that merely behaves like me, which people would walk into believing that they'd go on, and which is the reason why I only want engineering means that need no metaphysical gambling ([Nov 2025]), as that would be worse than no solution at all.

"Solving death" is one of the three goals of the Ontology ([Sep 2026]), and with the above in place I can say more exactly what it implies. The Ontology has a vision (synthetic consciousness, so consciousness that no longer has to end, which is a state), a mission (hybrid mind uploading, which is the undertaking) and the goals, which are what the mission has to achieve. Now, somebody who read day one carefully could say: wait, isn't "solving death" just the vision again, in other words? I asked myself the same, and I think it isn't, and the easiest way to see it is this => imagine that there's exactly one synthetic consciousness around, and everybody else still dies like today. Then you'd have a first instance of the vision, and you'd be nowhere near the goal. So with the vision, I'm talking about a state that should exist at all, and with the goal, I mean every single someone: your world-line doesn't have to end, and neither does anybody else's. It's also the one part that you can share with me without sharing my route, btw, as somebody who works on purely biological ways has the same goal (even if I think that biology alone can't get them there) and nothing else of the Ontology in common with me.

What is death? => the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line. In last year's article ([May 2025]) I called it the irreversible destruction of the physical and dynamic processes of consciousness, which is the same thing seen from the engineering side. And the one word in there that I had to think about the longest is "irreversible", because, well, irreversible according to whom, and when? A stopped heart was the end until CPR showed up. Then the end got moved into the brain, and nowadays surgeons cool brains down until the EEG is flat (nuanced detail: until EEG electrodes can't measure anything meaningful anymore!) and people still wake up as themselves, so medicine keeps pushing the point where a stop turns into an end further out (where exactly that point is, is for the meditation day on the 4D process world-line). I mean the strict version => death is the end that can't be undone by anything that physics allows. This also implies that "solving death" can never be about bringing somebody back from an end, only about never letting a stop turn into one (yes, this has implications for biostasis, where the open question is exactly if it's a stop or an end!). Preventing that point from ever being reached is the problem that I mean, and that one is an engineering problem ([Jan 2025]).

What is solving death? Solving meaning that death does not occur in the universe anymore? Well, not really. You can solve a thing practically, and it can still happen. We "solved flying" by making things fly (Icarus didn't stop anybody in the end), but you can still have things meant to fly that cannot fly, and planes that come down. Meaning, you can solve death, and death can still occur in the universe. The analogy only goes so far btw, as a steady crash rate is fine for flying and exactly not enough for death. I quickly discussed this on day one, and there's a big difference here that I want to be exact about, as three things get mixed up all the time. Step zero is to stop dying. If you halt the dying (the ageing and e.g. the failing organs), you get what one might call amortality => no clock in you runs out by itself anymore. However, a bus can still hit you, and a risk that just stays where it is still gets everybody in the end => only later. Step one is solving death => and that's EXACTLY what today's Ontology goal is about: no one has to end from any cause that engineering could potentially get rid of. So the remaining risk has to keep falling, but also fast enough so that all together the yearly risks add up to a finite total. A finite total is where the "has to" stops, and from there on, the smaller that total gets, the less likely it is that death still occurs at all, which is all that "solved" can mean for something that can still happen, and it's what I meant by making it very unlikely that death still occurs ([Oct 2025]). And then there's immortality, where death can't occur at all anymore, which is something that one can seek once death is solved, and nothing that I'd put into a goal. Stop dying and achieve amortality, then solve death and seek immortality, that's the order ([Jun 2026]).

So when I said "towards" at the beginning of today's meditation, I meant it literally. Functionally isolating the brain is step zero, the stopping of the dying, and no solution to death per se ([Jun 2026]). Most of us die because something in the body fails first and the brain goes down with it. That's why I say that this is the urgent part, and you can do it for somebody without having solved a single identity/philosophical puzzle before, as it's the same brain that simply keeps going (this is also kind of a very medical systems view btw, as any doctor is doing!). But a conscious brain that's just kept going is worth nothing if it's no life worth continuing, so input, some agency as well as protection from suffering are part of step zero itself! Mechanistic gradual brain replacement, the core of hybrid mind uploading, is step one ([Sep 2026]), and, according to my own reasoning, it's the kind of route on which the remaining risk can keep falling at all (and no, a backup doesn't count, a backup is a copy). So yes, I do think that functional brain isolation, combined with mechanistic gradual brain replacement, is probably the best shot, and the most pragmatic shot, at going towards solving death. They're both bets tho, and if a world-line truly went on via a replacement has to be shown physically (=> on the process that "does" the carrying, as a copy would tell you the same story (it's just a copy)). So all I did in today's meditation was to tell what the goal in my Ontology is, and why I think it is worth wanting.

One last thing: I deliberately stopped the definition at the universe itself, as a risk that keeps falling forever only helps if there is actually a forever to begin with (no pun intended), and if anything can go on through the heat death (or whatever other end stage it'll be) is open physics afaik => so no more engineering, "just" cosmology. So, yes, that's where I am with my thoughts after all these years. I can tell you why it's probably worth wanting, but I still can't tell you cleanly enough why I want it this badly – as well as that I also can't tell you (yet) when it would count as "goal achieved". The annoying part is that the last two get harder and harder the more I think about them – which is, I guess, why it's among the three hard goals of the Ontology!
