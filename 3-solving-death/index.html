Meditations on Synthetic Consciousness: Solving Death

14 November 2026

I run a company called Eightsix, where we try to functionally isolate the brain ([Sep 2026]); in other words: we take living brains out of their body, and keep them alive outside of their original bodies for as long as possible. We have early success in doing so in rodents, we're working towards the same for a large animal brain, and ultimately we want to do this for humans as well. When I tell people about this, friends, family, people I meet at scientific conferences, investors or also just random people I meet throughout life (e.g. my hairdresser, who I told what exactly I'm doing), I usually get the one question that gets asked most: "Why are you doing this?" I simply answer: "Because I want to solve death, and functionally isolating brains, and later replacing them gradually, is, according to my own reasoning, the most pragmatic route towards that goal, with the isolation as step zero". And then usually the question comes right after: "But, why? Why do you want to solve death?"

The short answer, before I ramble on for way too long: it's complicated, and no, I still don't have a clean one after ≈10 years of getting asked. I can tell you why death is bad for the one who dies, that part I'm fairly sure about. But why exactly me, why I can't put this thing down again? No idea, and it gets worse the longer I think about it.. – same as with "what has to be true along the way" as well as "when would we even call it solved"; because every time I answer one of those I do actually end up with a new question instead: what even is a world-line, what makes a continuation worth having, and so on (which is btw exactly why solving death is one of the three hard goals that the mission has to achieve in the Ontology)

Depending on who asks, I, over the years, came to several answers, depending on the person and the context and the time available. It ranges from personal upbringing reasons, e.g. my mom going through the Yugoslav wars and telling me stories as a child about all the terrible things that she has seen (hence developing a morbid and catastrophic view on death early on, as a developing brain), or that I was depressed as a teenager and prob read the wrong kinds of books in a row that led me, back then, towards thinking that death is actually the only answer (that phase is long over btw). Now, the thing is, and I have to be strict with myself here, that both of these stories answer a question that nobody asked. When somebody asks me "why do you want this?", they could mean "what made you want it?" or they could mean "what makes it worth wanting?". Those are actually two very different things => the first one is a cause and the second one a reason (and my mom's stories and my teenage bookshelf are e.g. obv causes). They tell you how I ended up with death as my main topic. But if the goal itself is any good: they don't tell you at all (and tbh, they also can't tell you that it's bad. People do try that one and it's the genetic fallacy, just used in the other direction!). The usual comeback here is "so it's just your fear of death talking", and I think that one deserves an honest answer: yes, maybe, I even posted a quote once saying that one of the principal things that moves people is the terror of death ([Oct 2024]). But you can play the exact same game with the other side! For basically all of human history there was nothing, like really nothing, that anybody could do about it, so what else would you do other than making your peace with it (Elster calls this adaptive preferences, or just "sour grapes"). So fine, my wish can be explained away, but so can everybody else's acceptance, and then explaining away doesn't decide anything here.

Other avenues of explanation are usually more mythological, as in: death always seemed to be the forbidden fruit of humanity and life in general, it's the one great barrier one shall not pass, and if you're a curious person you obviously love to explore that boundary. Again, curiosity is a cause and no reason, but the "shall not pass" part deserves a closer look, as nearly every culture has a story that warns you about going there (e.g. Gilgamesh loses his plant of youth to a snake, and Tithonus gets his eternal life without the eternal youth). Even my own "forbidden fruit" picture fits btw, as in Genesis the forbidden fruit is strictly speaking knowledge, and it's the tree of life that gets guarded afterwards, so that nobody eats from it and lives forever. People sometimes hand me these stories as an argument, as in "humanity has always known that this is a bad idea". I read them as the wisdom of a time in which nothing could be done, so they belong to the same making-peace as above, and if you look closely, what most of them warn against is a bad version of not having to die (ageing forever, being alone forever), which nobody is asking for. The one reading that's left is the hubris one, that it's against the order of things, and "the order of things" is no argument by itself, as the night is part of that order as well, which brings me to the lightbulbs.

So, more societally as an explanation, one of the analogies I've recently been bringing up during conversations is the example of pitching the lightbulb in front of people working on candles in the early 1800s. Instead of accepting the limitations of candles for brightening up the night, which is what we've done for thousands of years as Homo sapiens, why not go beyond those limits by inventing magically glowing things called lightbulbs, whose light doesn't have to burn down the way a candle does? It's coming from a totally different angle to solve the same problem, which is: keep it bright for as long as you want (you see the analogies I'm drawing slowly creeping in!). When working on lightbulbs you don't iterate on candles, you approach it with different things. And that's the beauty, because the upshot of solving "it being bright at night, against nature's reason to make it dark at night due to the earth blocking the sunlight" is that we ultimately needed to work on electricity, have constant access to electricity, DC then AC, and this led towards the electrification of a bunch of things, and ultimately also to the digital computer, and then AI, AGI and superintelligence (quite literally btw: the "Edison effect" was found inside a lightbulb in the early 1880s, Fleming turned it into the first vacuum tube in 1904, and the first electronic computers were rooms full of the descendants of that tube). Who would have thought that the people working on lightbulbs back in the day were working on this uphill technology with a tail that leads us towards superintelligence? I usually say that solving death kind of feels like this nowadays. It seems like, for the first time, we have enough of the technology and knowledge to start seriously, so let's try. The upshot of this is unknown and nobody can predict it, we can only guess. A few people like Nikola Tesla predicted, quite accurately, some future scenarios back then, but I don't think this is possible anymore, and I don't know anything about what the future has in store for us when we solve death, other than that solving death is just step one and that life in a post-death society will be stranger than we can imagine ([Oct 2025]).

I used to end this pitch with "but whatever it is, it must be net positive, otherwise it will be filtered out either way" (dialectic in society basically), and I have to take that part back, as it doesn't survive my own scrutiny. Bad things can stick around for a very long time before a society filters them out, and some things do the filtering on us instead. So let me say what I think the analogy really gives me. It shows the kind of move, as the improvement path of the candle (better wax, better wicks) never arrives at the lightbulb, and the candle here is biology. You can make it burn longer, and I'm all for that, but e.g. reversing ageing halts the dying and doesn't solve death, as you can still get hit by a bus ([Sep 2026]). It also shows that the upshots came through the infrastructure that the solution needed (electricity), which nobody working on it could foresee, and I expect the same here, as you can't work on this without understanding and engineering the thing that carries consciousness. And it takes care of the "order of things" from above, as nobody thinks today that the lightbulb was an offence against the night. Now, does any of this tell me that the result will be good? No, and no analogy ever could, I mean, it's lightbulbs. And there's one place where my nice analogy just falls apart, and it's kind of the most important place => continuity. If a lightbulb is off and you swap it for a new one, nobody cares, the light is simply "on again". With a consciousness that's the whole difference between going on and being replaced (the 4D process world-line gets its own day). And what's left of my "it must be net positive" is weaker, I'm afraid => the direct effect is good if death is bad for the one who dies (next paragraph), and for everything that comes after that, I really don't know.

Now, to come back, I technically have even more examples, and they range from the personal "life is great, death is bad, so I want more of life" to the very logical reasons as described in one of the earlier meditations ([Nov 2026]), to the very visionary views such as the lightbulb scenario above. Out of these, the logical one is the one that carries the most weight for me (it's the central reason, if you want), so I'll go through it once more, slowly. It starts with the thing from day one => nothing matters except to someone conscious. Death is the irreversible end of a conscious process world-line, so when it ends, everything that would have come after just doesn't happen for the someone that this world-line is, and if that would have been worth having, then missing out on it is bad for that someone (that's Nagel's deprivation view, I didn't invent this). The "worth having" matters a lot btw – as it's the reason why the goal says "no one has to end" and why it never said "no one is allowed to end". And yes, there are the well-known objections (e.g. Epicurus, Lucretius, "death gives life its meaning", "we have to make room", and so on etc.), but those things I'll take on another day, especially as today's meditation is getting way too long already...
